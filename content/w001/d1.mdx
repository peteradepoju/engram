<Step n={1} title="Why this field exists" minutes={10} />

In 2016 a computer program beat the world champion at Go. It used thousands of processors and many megawatt-hours of electricity to learn. A child learns to recognise a cat from a handful of examples, runs on the energy of a dim light bulb, and does not forget how to ride a bicycle when she learns to swim.

Both the program and the child **learn**. Both use networks of simple units connected by adjustable links. Yet they differ in almost every detail. NeuroAI is the field that studies natural and artificial intelligence side by side and asks two questions:

1. **Brain to AI.** What do brains know about learning, memory, efficiency and robustness that AI does not yet use?
2. **AI to brain.** Can artificial networks serve as working models, testable theories and analysis tools for how brains compute?

You will spend four years on these two questions. This course is built so that every technical idea you learn, from a dot product to a Transformer, is paired with its counterpart on the other side.

### The biological neuron

Your brain has about 86 billion neurons. Each one is a cell with three main parts:

- **Dendrites** branch out and receive signals from other neurons, at junctions called **synapses**. A typical cortical neuron receives several thousand synapses.
- The **cell body** (soma) adds up the incoming signals. The inside of the cell sits at about -70 millivolts relative to the outside; inputs push this voltage up or down.
- If the voltage at the start of the **axon** crosses a threshold, the neuron fires an **action potential** (a spike): a brief, all-or-nothing electrical pulse that travels down the axon to the next neurons.

A spike lasts about one millisecond. Information is carried by *when* and *how often* neurons spike. Learning happens largely by changing the strength of synapses, an idea called **synaptic plasticity**.

### The artificial neuron

An artificial neuron is a mathematical function. It takes inputs $x_1, \dots, x_n$, multiplies each by a weight $w_i$, adds a bias $b$, and passes the sum through a nonlinear function $f$:

$$
y = f\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i x_i + b\right)
$$

The weights play the role of synapses, the sum plays the role of the cell body, and $f$ plays the role of the threshold. Learning means adjusting the weights, usually by gradient descent on an error.

### Same idea, very different machines

| | Brain | Artificial network |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Units | About 86 billion neurons | Thousands to trillions of parameters |
| Signals | Spikes in continuous time | Real numbers, updated in steps |
| Learning | Local changes at synapses, many mechanisms | Mostly backpropagation of a global error |
| Power | About 20 watts | Kilowatts to megawatts in training |
| Data needed | Often a few examples | Often millions of examples |
| Strengths | Robustness, flexibility, lifelong learning | Speed, precision, scale |

<Step n={3} title="A neuron you can play with" minutes={25} />

Real neurons are complicated, but a very simple model captures their most important behaviour. The **leaky integrate-and-fire** (LIF) model treats the cell membrane like a leaky bucket. Input current fills it, a leak drains it back towards rest, and when the level reaches a threshold the neuron spikes and resets.

<Figure caption="A leaky integrate-and-fire neuron. Input current is switched on from 50 to 250 ms. Each vertical line is a spike. Try a current just below and just above 1.6 nA." credit="Interactive figure by Engram. Parameters typical of a cortical neuron model.">
  <LIFNeuron />
</Figure>

Things to try, and note what happens:

- Set the current to 1.5 nA. The voltage rises but never reaches threshold: no spikes at all.
- Set it to 1.7 nA. A few spikes. Now 3 nA: many more. Firing rate grows with input.
- Make $\tau$ small (5 ms), then large (50 ms). A long time constant means the neuron integrates input over a longer window, so it reacts more slowly.

The model is one equation. The voltage $V$ changes at a rate set by the leak towards rest and by the input current $I$:

$$
\tau \frac{dV}{dt} = -(V - V_\text{rest}) + R\,I
$$

When $V$ reaches the threshold $V_\text{th}$, we record a spike and set $V$ back to $V_\text{reset}$. You will derive this equation from the physics of the cell membrane in Week 29, and analyse it with calculus in Week 21. For today, notice two things:

<Derivation title="Why there is a threshold current">
  <div>If the input is constant, the voltage stops changing when $\frac{dV}{dt} = 0$. Set the right-hand side to zero.</div>
  <div>$$0 = -(V - V_\text{rest}) + R I \quad\Rightarrow\quad V_\infty = V_\text{rest} + R I$$ This is the voltage the neuron settles at if it never spikes.</div>
  <div>The neuron fires only if this resting point is above threshold: $V_\infty \ge V_\text{th}$.</div>
  <div>$$V_\text{rest} + R I \ge V_\text{th} \quad\Rightarrow\quad I \ge \frac{V_\text{th} - V_\text{rest}}{R}$$</div>
  <div>With $V_\text{rest} = -70$ mV, $V_\text{th} = -54$ mV and $R = 10$ M$\Omega$, the threshold current is $16/10 = 1.6$ nA. That is exactly where the figure starts to spike.</div>
</Derivation>

<Step n={4} title="Marr's three levels" minutes={20} />

In 1982 the neuroscientist David Marr argued that any information-processing system, brain or machine, must be understood at three levels:

1. **Computational.** What problem does the system solve, and why? (For example: estimate where a sound came from.)
2. **Algorithmic.** What representations and steps solve it? (For example: compare the arrival time of the sound at the two ears.)
3. **Implementational.** How is it built physically? (For example: a chain of neurons in the brainstem that act as coincidence detectors.)

These levels are the backbone of NeuroAI. A deep network and a brain area may solve the same computational problem with different algorithms and completely different hardware. Or they may share an algorithm, which would be a deep discovery. The paper you read today (Hassabis and colleagues, 2017) argues that neuroscience is most useful to AI at the first two levels.

<Callout kind="research">
Good research questions usually name a level. "Does the brain use backpropagation?" is an algorithmic question. "Is the visual cortex solving object recognition?" is computational. "Which synapses change during learning?" is implementational. When you write journal questions, label the level.
</Callout>

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def lif_rate(I):
    tau, R = 20.0, 10.0
    v_rest, v_th, v_reset = -70.0, -54.0, -75.0
    dt = 0.1
    v, count = v_rest, 0
    for k in range(int(300 / dt)):
        t = k * dt
        current = I if 50 <= t <= 250 else 0.0
        v += dt / tau * (-(v - v_rest) + R * current)
        if v >= v_th:
            count += 1
            v = v_reset
    return count / 0.2

currents = np.arange(0, 4.01, 0.25)
rates = [lif_rate(c) for c in currents]

plt.figure(figsize=(5, 3))

<Prove title="The time to first spike" task="With constant input $I$ switched on at $t=0$ and $V(0) = V_\text{rest}$, the solution of the LIF equation (before any spike) is $V(t) = V_\text{rest} + RI\,(1 - e^{-t/\tau})$. Use it to find the time $t^*$ of the first spike. Check your answer: for $I = 2.2$ nA, is $t^*$ close to the first spike time printed above (remember the input started at 50 ms)?">
  <p>Set $V(t^*) = V_\text{th}$:</p>
  <p>$$V_\text{rest} + RI(1 - e^{-t^*/\tau}) = V_\text{th} \Rightarrow e^{-t^*/\tau} = 1 - \frac{V_\text{th} - V_\text{rest}}{RI}$$</p>
  <p>Taking logs: $$t^* = -\tau \ln\left(1 - \frac{V_\text{th} - V_\text{rest}}{RI}\right) = \tau \ln\frac{RI}{RI - (V_\text{th} - V_\text{rest})}$$</p>
  <p>With $\tau = 20$, $RI = 22$ and $V_\text{th} - V_\text{rest} = 16$: $t^* = 20 \ln(22/6) \approx 26.0$ ms. The input started at 50 ms, so the first spike should be near 76 ms. The simulation agrees up to the small error of the Euler method. Notice that $t^*$ is only defined when $RI > 16$, which is the threshold current again.</p>
</Prove>
