<Step n={1} title="Five turning points" minutes={25} />

**1943: neurons as logic.** Warren McCulloch, a neuroscientist, and Walter Pitts, a young logician, showed that idealised neurons that are either on or off could compute any logical function. This was the first model of a neuron as a computing element, and it influenced the design of the first digital computers.

**1949 and 1958: learning.** The psychologist Donald Hebb proposed that when one neuron repeatedly helps to fire another, the connection between them strengthens. Frank Rosenblatt turned the idea of learnable connections into the **perceptron**, a machine that learned to classify patterns, presented as a theory of the brain (you will read his paper on Day 5).

**1980s: parallel distributed processing.** Hopfield showed how networks can store memories as stable states. Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams popularised **backpropagation** for training multi-layer networks. A generation of cognitive scientists modelled perception, memory and language with neural networks.

**2012: deep learning.** A deep convolutional network trained on GPUs (AlexNet) won the ImageNet competition by a large margin. The architecture echoed the hierarchy of the visual cortex, which Hubel and Wiesel had described in the 1960s.

**2014 onwards: AI as a model of the brain.** Networks trained only to recognise objects turned out to predict the responses of neurons in monkey visual cortex better than any model built by hand. Since then AI has been used to model hearing, movement, navigation, decision-making and language in the brain.

<Callout kind="history">
Many founders of AI were trained in neuroscience or psychology: Hebb, Rosenblatt, Hinton and Hassabis among them. The split into separate fields came later, as each grew large. NeuroAI is partly a reunion.
</Callout>

<Step n={3} title="The big open questions" minutes={20} />

These are some of the questions the field is working on now. Each appears later in the course; the week is in brackets.

- **Credit assignment.** Deep networks learn with backpropagation, which needs information to flow backwards through the network. Neurons do not obviously do that. How does the brain work out which synapses to change? (Week 136)
- **Learning from little data.** Animals learn from a few examples and keep learning for life without forgetting. Can AI do the same? (Week 140)
- **Energy efficiency.** The brain uses spikes and runs on about 20 watts. What computational advantage do spikes give? (Week 138)
- **Representations.** When a network and a brain area solve the same task, do they represent the world the same way, and how would we know? (Week 134)
- **World models.** Do brains build internal models of the world to plan and imagine, and should AI do the same? (Week 142)
- **Language.** Large language models predict brain activity during listening and reading. What does that tell us about language in the brain? (Week 145)

<Step n={4} title="Code: two connected neurons" minutes={25} />

Yesterday you simulated one neuron. Brains are networks, so let us connect two. Neuron A receives an input current. Each time A spikes, it gives neuron B a kick of voltage through a synapse of strength `w`.

<PyCell code={String.raw`
  import numpy as np
  import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

  def simulate(w, I_A=2.5, T=300.0, dt=0.1):
      tau, R = 20.0, 10.0
      v_rest, v_th, v_reset = -70.0, -54.0, -75.0
      vA = vB = v_rest
      spikes_A, spikes_B, trace_B = [], [], []
      for k in range(int(T / dt)):
          t = k * dt
          vA += dt / tau * (-(vA - v_rest) + R * I_A)
          vB += dt / tau * (-(vB - v_rest))
          if vA >= v_th:
              spikes_A.append(t); vA = v_reset
              vB += w                      # the synapse: a jump in B's voltage
          if vB >= v_th:
              spikes_B.append(t); vB = v_reset
          trace_B.append(vB)
      return spikes_A, spikes_B, np.array(trace_B)

  sA, sB, trace = simulate(w=6.0)
  print(f"A fired {len(sA)} times, B fired {len(sB)} times")
  plt.figure(figsize=(6, 2.5))
  plt.plot(np.arange(len(trace)) * 0.1, trace)
  plt.xlabel("time (ms)"); plt.ylabel("B voltage (mV)")
  plt.show()
`} />

Notice that B can fire even when one kick is smaller than the 16 mV gap to threshold. The kicks add up if they arrive faster than the leak drains them. This is **temporal summation**, and it is why the time constant matters.

<Prove title="When do kicks add up?" task="Between two spikes of A, neuron B's voltage decays towards rest: the distance from rest shrinks by a factor $e^{-\Delta t/\tau}$ in time $\Delta t$. Suppose A fires regularly every $\Delta t$ ms and each spike adds $w$ mV. Show that just after many spikes, B's distance from rest approaches $w / (1 - e^{-\Delta t/\tau})$. When does this exceed 16 mV?">

Let $u_n$ be the distance from rest just after the $n$-th kick. Between kicks it decays, then a kick adds $w$:

$$
u_{n+1} = u_n e^{-\Delta t/\tau} + w
$$

This is a geometric series. Starting from $u_0 = 0$, $u_n = w(1 + q + q^2 + \dots + q^{n-1})$ with $q = e^{-\Delta t/\tau}$, which approaches $w/(1-q)$ as $n$ grows.

B fires eventually if $w/(1 - e^{-\Delta t/\tau}) \ge 16$ mV, that is $w \ge 16(1 - e^{-\Delta t/\tau})$. Fast input (small $\Delta t$) makes $1 - q$ small, so a weak synapse is enough.

</Prove>
