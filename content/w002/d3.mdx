<Step n={1} title="The rule" minutes={25} />

When two arrays have different shapes, NumPy compares their shapes **from the right**. Two sizes are compatible if they are equal, or if one of them is 1 (or missing). A size of 1 is stretched to match the other. If any pair is incompatible, you get an error.

<Figure caption="Broadcasting two 2D arrays. Pale cells are virtual copies: NumPy acts as if the array were stretched, without copying memory." credit="Interactive figure by Engram.">
  <Broadcasting />
</Figure>

Examples, aligned on the right:

| a | b | result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| (20, 60, 200) | (200,) | (20, 60, 200) |
| (20, 60, 200) | (20, 1, 1) | (20, 60, 200) |
| (20, 1) | (8,) | (20, 8) |
| (20, 60) | (20,) | error: 60 and 20 differ |

The last row is the classic mistake. To combine a per-neuron value with a (neurons, trials) array, the per-neuron value needs shape (20, 1), not (20,).

<Step n={2} title="Lining up axes" minutes={25} />

Two tools turn a shape like `(20,)` into `(20, 1)`:

- `keepdims=True` in a reduction keeps the reduced axis with size 1.
- Indexing with `None` (also called `np.newaxis`) inserts a new axis of size 1.

<PyCell code={String.raw`
  import numpy as np
  rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
  rates = rng.gamma(2.0, 5.0, size=(20, 60))       # (neurons, trials), Hz

  m = rates.mean(axis=1)                            # (20,)
  try:
      rates - m
  except ValueError as e:
      print("error:", e)

  m_keep = rates.mean(axis=1, keepdims=True)        # (20, 1)
  centred = rates - m_keep                          # (20, 60)
  print(centred.shape, np.allclose(centred.mean(axis=1), 0))

  centred2 = rates - m[:, None]                     # same thing with None
  print(np.allclose(centred, centred2))
`} />

**Z-scoring** each neuron (subtract its mean, divide by its standard deviation) is one of the most common steps in population analysis, because neurons fire at very different rates. With broadcasting it is one line.

<Derivation title="Z-scores have mean 0 and standard deviation 1">
  <div>

Let $z_i = (x_i - \bar{x})/s$ where $s$ is the (population) standard deviation of the $x_i$.

  </div>
  <div>

Mean: $\bar{z} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i \frac{x_i - \bar{x}}{s} = \frac{1}{s}\left(\bar{x} - \bar{x}\right) = 0$.

  </div>
  <div>

Variance: $\frac{1}{n}\sum_i z_i^2 = \frac{1}{s^2}\cdot\frac{1}{n}\sum_i (x_i - \bar{x})^2 = \frac{s^2}{s^2} = 1$.

  </div>
  <div>

So after z-scoring, every neuron is on the same scale: a value of 2 means two standard deviations above that neuron's own mean.

  </div>
</Derivation>

<Step n={3} title="A population of tuning curves" minutes={30} />

Neurons in primary visual cortex (V1) respond most strongly to edges at one orientation, their **preferred orientation**, and less to others. A simple model of a neuron's mean rate when shown an edge at angle $\theta$ is

$$
r(\theta) = r_0 + g \, \exp\!\big(\kappa\,(\cos 2(\theta - \theta_\text{pref}) - 1)\big)
$$

where $r_0$ is the baseline, $g$ the gain and $\kappa$ how sharp the tuning is. The factor 2 is there because an edge at $0^\circ$ and $180^\circ$ is the same edge.

To compute this for 40 neurons and 36 orientations, give preferred orientations shape (40, 1) and stimulus orientations shape (36,). Broadcasting produces a (40, 36) table: every neuron's response to every stimulus.

<PyCell code={String.raw`
  import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
  n = 40
  pref = np.linspace(0, np.pi, n, endpoint=False)[:, None]   # (40, 1)
  theta = np.linspace(0, np.pi, 36, endpoint=False)          # (36,)
  r0, g, kappa = 2.0, 30.0, 2.5
  tuning = r0 + g * np.exp(kappa * (np.cos(2 * (theta - pref)) - 1))   # (40, 36)
  print(tuning.shape)

  fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(8, 3))
  for i in [0, 10, 20, 30]:
      axes[0].plot(np.degrees(theta), tuning[i], label=f"pref {np.degrees(pref[i, 0]):.0f}°")
  axes[0].set_xlabel("stimulus orientation (deg)"); axes[0].set_ylabel("rate (Hz)"); axes[0].legend(fontsize=7)
  axes[1].imshow(tuning, aspect="auto", extent=[0, 180, n, 0], cmap="magma")
  axes[1].set_xlabel("stimulus orientation (deg)"); axes[1].set_ylabel("neuron (sorted by preference)")
  plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()
`} />

<Key title="Before you go">
<ul>
<li>Shapes are compared from the right; 1 stretches to match; anything else must be equal.</li>
<li>Use keepdims or None to line axes up deliberately.</li>
<li>Z-scoring puts neurons on a common scale.</li>
<li>One broadcast can build a whole population model: (neurons, 1) against (stimuli,).</li>
</ul>
</Key>
