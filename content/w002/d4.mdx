<Step n={1} title="The raster" minutes={20} />

A **raster plot** shows one row per trial and a tick at each spike time. It is the rawest honest view of a neuron's response: you see the variability from trial to trial, and you see structure (a burst after the stimulus, a pause) that averages can hide.

Try: 2 trials with 10 ms bins (noisy), 60 trials with 10 ms bins (better), 60 trials with 200 ms bins (smooth but the two peaks merge). There is no free lunch: wide bins reduce noise but blur fast changes.

<Step n={2} title="Binning and the PSTH" minutes={30} />

Spike times usually arrive as a list per trial. `np.histogram` counts how many fall into each bin.

<PyCell code={String.raw`
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

def true_rate(t):                       # t in seconds, rate in Hz
    return 8 + 55 * np.exp(-((t - 0.35) ** 2) / (2 * 0.06 ** 2)) + 20 * np.exp(-((t - 0.65) ** 2) / (2 * 0.04 ** 2))

# Simulate 40 trials, 1 s each, at 1 ms resolution
dt, T, n_trials = 0.001, 1.0, 40
t = np.arange(0, T, dt)
spike_trains = [t[rng.random(t.size) < true_rate(t) * dt] for _ in range(n_trials)]
print("spikes on first 3 trials:", [len(s) for s in spike_trains[:3]])

edges = np.arange(0, T + 1e-9, 0.02)                       # 20 ms bins
counts = np.array([np.histogram(s, bins=edges)[0] for s in spike_trains])   # (trials, bins)
psth = counts.mean(axis=0) / 0.02                          # Hz
print(counts.shape, psth.shape, "peak rate:", psth.max().round(1), "Hz")
`} />

The PSTH is the mean count per bin divided by the bin width, so its units are spikes per second. Its standard error across trials is the SEM you met in Week 1, and it tells you how much to trust each bar.

<Derivation title="The noise in one PSTH bar">
  <div>In a bin of width $\Delta$ where the true rate is $r$, a Poisson neuron's count has mean and variance $r\Delta$.</div>
  <div>The PSTH bar averages $n$ trials and divides by $\Delta$: $\hat{r} = \frac{1}{n\Delta}\sum_{k} c_k$.</div>
  <div>Its variance is $\frac{1}{n^2\Delta^2}\cdot n \cdot r\Delta = \frac{r}{n\Delta}$.</div>
  <div>So the relative noise, standard deviation over mean, is $\frac{\sqrt{r/(n\Delta)}}{r} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{r\, n \Delta}}$. Halving the bin width increases the noise by $\sqrt{2}$; quadrupling the trials halves it. That answers today's prediction.</div>
</Derivation>

<Step n={3} title="Smoothing" minutes={25} />

Instead of wide bins, you can use narrow bins and then **smooth** with a kernel: replace each value by a weighted average of its neighbours. A Gaussian kernel of width $\sigma$ is the usual choice. This is a **convolution**, an operation you will study properly in Week 10 and meet again as the core of convolutional networks in Week 84.

<PyCell code={String.raw`
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

fine = np.array([np.histogram(s, bins=np.arange(0, T + 1e-9, 0.001))[0] for s in spike_trains])   # 1 ms bins
raw_rate = fine.mean(axis=0) / 0.001

def gaussian_kernel(sigma, dt):
    x = np.arange(-4 * sigma, 4 * sigma + dt / 2, dt)
    k = np.exp(-x ** 2 / (2 * sigma ** 2))
    return k / k.sum()                       # weights sum to 1, so the mean rate is unchanged

plt.figure(figsize=(7, 3))
plt.plot(t, raw_rate, color="0.8", lw=0.5, label="1 ms bins")
for sigma in [0.005, 0.02, 0.08]:
    plt.plot(t, np.convolve(raw_rate, gaussian_kernel(sigma, 0.001), mode="same"), label=f"σ = {sigma*1000:.0f} ms")
plt.plot(t, true_rate(t), "k--", lw=1, label="true rate")
plt.xlabel("time (s)"); plt.ylabel("rate (Hz)"); plt.legend(fontsize=7); plt.ylim(0, 120)
plt.show()
`} />

<Key title="Before you go">
<ul>
<li>Rasters show the data; PSTHs and smoothed rates are estimates built from it.</li>
<li>Rate = mean count / bin width. Its noise falls with more trials, rate and bin width.</li>
<li>Smoothing is convolution with a normalised kernel; choose its width deliberately and report it.</li>
<li>Peak time and latency come from argmax on the smoothed rate.</li>
</ul>
</Key>
