<Step n={1} title="Random numbers done properly" minutes={20} />

Simulations need randomness, and research needs that randomness to be **reproducible**. NumPy's modern interface is a generator object made with a **seed**. The same seed gives the same numbers every time, on every computer.

<PyCell code={String.raw`
  import numpy as np
  rng = np.random.default_rng(seed=42)
  print(rng.random(3))            # uniform on [0, 1)
  print(rng.normal(0, 1, 3))      # Gaussian with mean 0, SD 1
  print(rng.poisson(4.0, 5))      # Poisson counts with mean 4
  print(rng.exponential(0.05, 3)) # exponential with mean 0.05

  rng2 = np.random.default_rng(seed=42)
  print("same seed, same numbers:", rng2.random(3))
`} />

<Callout kind="research">
Report seeds in papers and run important simulations with several seeds. If a result appears with one seed and vanishes with another, it was luck. Machine learning papers now routinely report the mean and spread over seeds.
</Callout>

<Step n={2} title="The Poisson process" minutes={30} />

A **Poisson process** with rate $r$ has two defining properties: in any tiny interval $dt$ the probability of a spike is $r\,dt$, and what happens in one interval is independent of every other. It has no memory. Two equivalent ways to simulate it follow from this.

**Method 1: bins.** Divide time into small bins of width $dt$ and put a spike in each bin with probability $r\,dt$.

**Method 2: intervals.** The waiting time between spikes follows an **exponential distribution** with mean $1/r$. Draw intervals and add them up.

<Derivation title="Why the intervals are exponential">
  <div>

Let $P(\tau)$ be the probability that no spike happens in a stretch of length $\tau$.

  </div>
  <div>

Split $\tau$ into $n$ tiny bins of width $dt = \tau/n$. Each is empty with probability $1 - r\,dt$, independently, so $P(\tau) = (1 - r\tau/n)^n$.

  </div>
  <div>

As $n \to \infty$, $(1 - x/n)^n \to e^{-x}$, so $P(\tau) = e^{-r\tau}$.

  </div>
  <div>

The waiting time $W$ exceeds $\tau$ exactly when no spike happens before $\tau$, so $\Pr(W \gt \tau) = e^{-r\tau}$. Differentiating, its density is $r e^{-r\tau}$: the exponential distribution, with mean $1/r$.

  </div>
</Derivation>

The **count** of spikes in a window of length $T$ follows the **Poisson distribution** with mean $\lambda = rT$:

$$
\Pr(N = k) = \frac{\lambda^k e^{-\lambda}}{k!}, \qquad \mathbb{E}[N] = \lambda, \qquad \operatorname{Var}[N] = \lambda
$$

Mean equals variance. That is the fingerprint of a Poisson process, and the answer to today's prediction: 20.

<Step n={3} title="Measuring variability" minutes={25} />

Two numbers summarise a neuron's variability:

- The **Fano factor** of spike counts: $F = \operatorname{Var}[N] / \mathbb{E}[N]$. Poisson gives $F = 1$.
- The **coefficient of variation** of interspike intervals: $\text{CV} = \text{SD}(\text{ISI}) / \text{mean}(\text{ISI})$. For an exponential distribution the SD equals the mean, so Poisson gives $\text{CV} = 1$. A perfectly regular clock gives $\text{CV} = 0$.

Cortical neurons typically have both near 1 (Softky and Koch, 1993, today's paper). But a real neuron cannot fire again for a millisecond or two after a spike: the **refractory period**. That makes very short intervals impossible and the train more regular.

<PyCell code={String.raw`
  def poisson_train(rate, T, rng, refractory=0.0):
      """Spike times in [0, T) from exponential intervals, plus an optional dead time after each spike."""
      times, t = [], 0.0
      while True:
          t += refractory + rng.exponential(1 / rate)
          if t >= T:
              return np.array(times)
          times.append(t)

  rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
  for ref in [0.0, 0.002, 0.01]:
      isi = np.diff(poisson_train(50, 200, rng, refractory=ref))
      print(f"refractory {ref*1000:4.0f} ms: mean ISI {isi.mean()*1000:5.1f} ms, CV {isi.std()/isi.mean():.2f}")
`} />

<Prove title="CV with a dead time" task="With a refractory period $d$ followed by an exponential wait of mean $1/r$, each ISI is $d + E$ where $E$ is exponential. Show that $\text{CV} = \dfrac{1}{1 + r d}$.">

The mean ISI is $d + 1/r$. The variance is that of $E$ alone (adding a constant does not change variance): $1/r^2$, so the SD is $1/r$.

$$
\text{CV} = \frac{1/r}{d + 1/r} = \frac{1}{1 + rd}
$$

With $r = 40$ Hz and $d = 0.01$ s, $rd = 0.4$ and CV $= 1/1.4 \approx 0.71$. Check this against your simulation. (Here $r$ is the rate of the exponential part; the neuron's actual firing rate is $1/(d + 1/r)$, which is lower.)

</Prove>
