<Step n={1} title="Honest axes" minutes={20} />

A bar's **length** encodes its value, so a bar chart must start at zero. If the axis starts at 19 Hz, a 20 Hz bar is 1 unit long and a 22 Hz bar is 3 units long: three times as long, for a 10 per cent difference. The numbers are right and the picture lies.

Points and lines encode value by **position**, not length, so their axis may start elsewhere, as long as the reader can see where it starts.

<Step n={2} title="Colour maps" minutes={25} />

When colour encodes a number (a heatmap, an image of a receptive field), the colour map decides what readers see. Our eyes judge **lightness** far better than hue, so a good map changes lightness steadily from one end to the other. Rainbow maps such as `jet` do not: their lightness rises and falls, so smooth data appear to have bright bands and sharp edges. Try it.

<Figure caption="The same smooth receptive field (an excitatory centre with an inhibitory surround) in four colour maps, with each map's perceived lightness underneath." credit="Interactive figure by Engram. Lightness computed as CIELAB L*. Colour map values sampled from Matplotlib.">
  <Colormaps />
</Figure>

Rules of thumb:

- **Sequential** data (firing rate, power, probability): `viridis`, `magma`, `cividis`, or greyscale.
- **Diverging** data with a meaningful zero (change from baseline, correlation, excitation against inhibition): `RdBu_r` or `coolwarm`, centred on zero with `vmin=-m, vmax=m`.
- **Cyclic** data (orientation, phase, direction): `twilight` or `hsv`, so the two ends match.
- **Categories** (brain areas, conditions): a qualitative palette such as `tab10`, and never more colours than a reader can tell apart.

About 1 in 12 men and 1 in 200 women have a colour-vision deficiency, most often red against green. Uniform maps like `viridis` and `cividis` remain readable for them.

The diagonal you see is a **sequence**: each neuron fires at its own moment. Plots like this, with neurons sorted by peak time, appear in studies of hippocampal time cells and of task-trained recurrent networks (Week 135). Sorting is a choice, so always say how the neurons were sorted, and ideally sort on half the trials and plot the other half (Week 2, Day 2).

**The histogram.** Bin width changes the story, just like the PSTH last week. Try `bins=5`, `bins=20` and `bins=100` on the same data and see what disappears.

<Step n={5} title="Show the data" minutes={10} />

A bar with an error bar summarises dozens of points with two numbers. Very different datasets give the same bar: one outlier, two clusters, or a smooth spread. Whenever there are fewer than a few hundred points, draw them.

<PyCell code={String.raw`
  rng = np.random.default_rng(4)
  groups = {"smooth": rng.normal(20, 4, 30),
            "two clusters": np.r_[rng.normal(15, 1, 15), rng.normal(25, 1, 15)],
            "one outlier": np.r_[rng.normal(18.5, 1.5, 29), 60]}

      axes[0].bar(i, x.mean(), yerr=x.std(ddof=1) / np.sqrt(len(x)), capsize=4, color="0.7")
      axes[1].plot(np.full(len(x), i) + rng.uniform(-0.15, 0.15, len(x)), x, "o", ms=4, alpha=0.7)

  for ax, t in zip(axes, ["bars hide it", "points show it"]):

<Key title="Before you go">
<ul>
<li>Bars start at zero; points and lines may not, if the axis is clear.</li>
<li>Use perceptually uniform colour maps of the right kind, and never jet.</li>
<li>Use log axes when data span orders of magnitude.</li>
<li>Rasters, heatmaps with colour bars and well-binned histograms are the daily tools; show individual points when you can.</li>
</ul>
