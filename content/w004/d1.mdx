<Step n={1} title="Three views of a vector" minutes={20} />

A **vector** is an ordered list of numbers, such as $\mathbf{r} = (12, 4)$. It can be read in three equivalent ways:

1. **A list.** Neuron 1 fires at 12 Hz and neuron 2 at 4 Hz.
2. **A point.** In a plane with one axis per neuron, the population state is the point $(12, 4)$.
3. **An arrow.** From the origin (silence) to that point. Its direction says which neurons are active relative to each other; its length says how much activity there is overall.

With $n$ neurons the list has $n$ numbers and the space has $n$ axes. We cannot draw 100 axes, but every rule you learn in two dimensions works unchanged in 100. That is the power of linear algebra.

<Video id="fNk_zzaMoSs" title="Vectors | Chapter 1, Essence of linear algebra" by="3Blue1Brown (Grant Sanderson)" note="If you watched it in Week 2, watch it again now: it is the clearest picture of today's three views of a vector." />

We write vectors in bold, $\mathbf{r}$, and their entries with subscripts, $r_1, r_2, \dots, r_n$. A vector with $n$ real entries lives in the space $\mathbb{R}^n$.

<Step n={2} title="Adding and scaling" minutes={25} />

Two operations define everything else.

- **Addition** works entry by entry: $\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b} = (a_1 + b_1, \dots, a_n + b_n)$. As arrows: put the tail of $\mathbf{b}$ at the tip of $\mathbf{a}$.
- **Scaling** by a number $k$ multiplies every entry: $k\mathbf{a} = (k a_1, \dots, k a_n)$. As arrows: stretch by $k$, and flip if $k$ is negative.

In neuroscience terms, if $\mathbf{a}$ is the population's baseline activity and $\mathbf{b}$ is the change a stimulus causes, then $\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b}$ is the response, and $2\mathbf{b}$ is the response to a stimulus with twice the effect (if the neurons respond linearly, a big if that you will test in Week 35).

<PyCell code={String.raw`
  import numpy as np
  baseline = np.array([12.0, 4.0, 7.0, 0.5])     # Hz, four neurons
  change = np.array([3.0, 8.0, -2.0, 0.0])       # effect of a stimulus
  print("response:", baseline + change)
  print("twice the effect:", baseline + 2 * change)
  print("difference between responses:", (baseline + 2 * change) - (baseline + change))
`} />

NumPy arrays are vectors: `+` and `*` with a number do exactly the entry-by-entry operations above. That is why Week 2's array thinking was the first step in linear algebra.

<Step n={3} title="Length and distance" minutes={25} />

The **length** (or **norm**) of a vector is the straight-line distance from the origin to its tip.

<Derivation title="Length in any number of dimensions">
  <div>

In two dimensions, $\mathbf{r} = (r_1, r_2)$ is the hypotenuse of a right triangle with sides $r_1$ and $r_2$, so by Pythagoras its length is $\sqrt{r_1^2 + r_2^2}$.

  </div>
  <div>

In three dimensions, first find the diagonal of the base, $\sqrt{r_1^2 + r_2^2}$, then use Pythagoras again with the height $r_3$:

$$
\lVert \mathbf{r} \rVert = \sqrt{\left(\sqrt{r_1^2 + r_2^2}\right)^2 + r_3^2} = \sqrt{r_1^2 + r_2^2 + r_3^2}
$$

  </div>
  <div>

Each new dimension adds one more squared term. In $n$ dimensions:

$$
\lVert \mathbf{r} \rVert = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n} r_i^2}
$$

  </div>
  <div>

The **distance** between two states $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$ is the length of the arrow between them, $\lVert \mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b} \rVert$. For today's prediction: $\sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = 5$ Hz.

  </div>
</Derivation>

A vector of length 1 is a **unit vector**. Dividing any non-zero vector by its length gives a unit vector pointing the same way: $\hat{\mathbf{r}} = \mathbf{r} / \lVert \mathbf{r} \rVert$. Unit vectors describe pure direction, which is how we will describe preferred directions and coding dimensions.

<Step n={4} title="Population state space" minutes={30} />

Now the real use. Record $N$ neurons over time and, at each moment, the population's firing rates form one vector in $\mathbb{R}^N$. As time passes the point moves: the population traces a **trajectory** through **state space**. This view, central to today's paper by Saxena and Cunningham, is how modern neuroscience describes motor preparation, decision-making and working memory.

  tt = np.linspace(0, 1, 100)
  common = 5 + 10 * np.exp(-((tt - 0.3) ** 2) / 0.01)
  w = rng.normal(0, 1, (50, 1))

<Prove title="The length of a sum" task="Using the length formula, show that $\lVert \mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b} \rVert^2 = \lVert \mathbf{a} \rVert^2 + \lVert \mathbf{b} \rVert^2 + 2\sum_i a_i b_i$. When is the length of the sum simply given by Pythagoras?">

Expand entry by entry:

$$
\lVert \mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b} \rVert^2 = \sum_i (a_i + b_i)^2 = \sum_i a_i^2 + \sum_i b_i^2 + 2\sum_i a_i b_i
$$

The first two sums are $\lVert \mathbf{a} \rVert^2$ and $\lVert \mathbf{b} \rVert^2$. The cross term $\sum_i a_i b_i$ is new: it is the **dot product** $\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b}$, tomorrow's topic.

The result reduces to Pythagoras, $\lVert \mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b} \rVert^2 = \lVert \mathbf{a} \rVert^2 + \lVert \mathbf{b} \rVert^2$, exactly when $\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} = 0$. That is the definition of $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$ being at right angles (orthogonal).

</Prove>

<li>A vector is a list, a point and an arrow at once; every rule works in any number of dimensions.</li>
<li>Add and scale entry by entry; NumPy arrays do this already.</li>
<li>Length is the square root of the sum of squares; distance is the length of the difference.</li>
<li>Population activity is a trajectory in a space with one axis per neuron.</li>
