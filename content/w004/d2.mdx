<Step n={1} title="The algebraic definition" minutes={20} />

The **dot product** of two vectors of the same length multiplies them entry by entry and adds up the results:

$$
\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_i b_i = a_1 b_1 + a_2 b_2 + \dots + a_n b_n
$$

The result is a single number (a scalar). You computed one in Week 1 every time a perceptron worked out $\mathbf{w} \cdot \mathbf{x}$, and yesterday it appeared as the cross term in $\lVert \mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b} \rVert^2$.

For today's prediction: $(10, 0) \cdot (0, 10) = 10 \times 0 + 0 \times 10 = 0$. The two patterns share no active neurons, and their dot product is exactly zero. That is not a coincidence, as the geometry shows.

<Step n={2} title="The geometric definition" minutes={30} />

The same number has a geometric meaning: lengths times the cosine of the angle between the arrows.

$$
\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} = \lVert \mathbf{a} \rVert \, \lVert \mathbf{b} \rVert \cos\theta
$$

<Derivation title="Why the two definitions agree">
  <div>

Draw $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$ from the same point, with angle $\theta$ between them. The third side of the triangle is $\mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b}$.

  </div>
  <div>

The **law of cosines** from school geometry says, for a triangle with sides of length $\lVert \mathbf{a} \rVert$, $\lVert \mathbf{b} \rVert$ and $\lVert \mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b} \rVert$:

$$
\lVert \mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b} \rVert^2 = \lVert \mathbf{a} \rVert^2 + \lVert \mathbf{b} \rVert^2 - 2\lVert \mathbf{a} \rVert \lVert \mathbf{b} \rVert \cos\theta
$$

  </div>
  <div>

Expand the left side with entries, as yesterday:

$$
\lVert \mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b} \rVert^2 = \sum_i (a_i - b_i)^2 = \lVert \mathbf{a} \rVert^2 + \lVert \mathbf{b} \rVert^2 - 2\sum_i a_i b_i
$$

  </div>
  <div>

The two expressions are equal. Cancel $\lVert \mathbf{a} \rVert^2 + \lVert \mathbf{b} \rVert^2$ and divide by $-2$:

$$
\sum_i a_i b_i = \lVert \mathbf{a} \rVert \lVert \mathbf{b} \rVert \cos\theta
$$

The left side is the algebraic definition, the right side the geometric one.

  </div>
</Derivation>

So the sign of the dot product tells you the angle:

- **Positive**: the vectors point roughly the same way ($\theta$ less than $90^\circ$).
- **Zero**: they are at right angles, **orthogonal** ($\theta = 90^\circ$).
- **Negative**: they point roughly opposite ways ($\theta$ more than $90^\circ$).

<Step n={3} title="Projection" minutes={20} />

The **scalar projection** of $\mathbf{a}$ onto the direction of $\mathbf{b}$ is how far $\mathbf{a}$ reaches along $\mathbf{b}$: the length of its shadow,

$$
\text{comp}_{\mathbf{b}}\,\mathbf{a} = \lVert \mathbf{a} \rVert \cos\theta = \frac{\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b}}{\lVert \mathbf{b} \rVert} = \mathbf{a} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{b}}
$$

and the **vector projection** is that length times the unit vector $\hat{\mathbf{b}}$: $\text{proj}_{\mathbf{b}}\,\mathbf{a} = (\mathbf{a} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{b}})\,\hat{\mathbf{b}}$.

When $\mathbf{b}$ is a unit vector, the dot product **is** the projection. This is the key to Week 7: projecting 100-dimensional population activity onto a few well-chosen unit vectors (principal components) is how we see it.

No single neuron separates the two conditions well, because the difference is spread thinly over all 30. Projected onto the right direction, the two conditions form two clear humps. Finding such directions from data is what decoders do (Week 56).

<Step n={4} title="Cosine similarity" minutes={30} />

To compare two patterns without caring about their overall size, divide out the lengths:

$$
\cos\theta = \frac{\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b}}{\lVert \mathbf{a} \rVert \, \lVert \mathbf{b} \rVert}
$$

This **cosine similarity** is 1 for patterns pointing the same way, 0 for orthogonal patterns and $-1$ for opposite ones. A population response at high contrast and the same pattern at low contrast (all rates halved) have cosine similarity 1: the same pattern, a different size.

The same measure compares word embeddings in machine learning. In this week's AI paper (Mikolov and colleagues, 2013), words with similar meanings have word vectors with high cosine similarity.

<Callout kind="warning">
Raw dot products between firing-rate vectors are dominated by the fastest neurons: a neuron firing at 80 Hz contributes far more than ten neurons at 2 Hz. Before comparing patterns, decide whether you want that. Cosine similarity removes overall size; z-scoring each neuron first (Week 2, Day 3) also stops fast neurons dominating.
</Callout>

<Prove title="Cosine similarity is bounded" task="Use the geometric definition to show that $-1 \le \dfrac{\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b}}{\lVert \mathbf{a} \rVert \lVert \mathbf{b} \rVert} \le 1$ for any non-zero vectors. (This is the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality.) When is it exactly 1?">

From the geometric definition, $\dfrac{\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b}}{\lVert \mathbf{a} \rVert \lVert \mathbf{b} \rVert} = \cos\theta$, and the cosine of any angle lies between $-1$ and $1$.

It equals 1 when $\theta = 0$: the vectors point the same way, so $\mathbf{a} = k\mathbf{b}$ for some $k \gt 0$. It equals $-1$ when they point exactly opposite ways ($k \lt 0$).

A proof without geometry, which works in any dimension where angles are not obvious, uses $\lVert \mathbf{a} - t\mathbf{b} \rVert^2 \ge 0$ for every $t$ and chooses $t$ to make it smallest. You will meet that argument in Week 67.

</Prove>

<Key title="Before you go">
<ul>
<li>The dot product is a sum of products, and equally lengths times the cosine of the angle.</li>
<li>Its sign gives the angle: positive, zero (orthogonal) or negative.</li>
<li>Dotting with a unit vector gives a projection: the basis of decoders and PCA.</li>
<li>Cosine similarity compares patterns regardless of size, in brains and in embeddings.</li>
</ul>
