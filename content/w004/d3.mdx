<Step n={1} title="The linear neuron" minutes={20} />

The simplest model of a neuron says: response = weights dot input, then a nonlinearity.

$$
r = f(\mathbf{w} \cdot \mathbf{x} + b)
$$

You built this as a perceptron in Week 1, with $f$ a step. In sensory neuroscience $\mathbf{x}$ is the stimulus (the pixel intensities of an image, or the sound energy at each frequency), $\mathbf{w}$ is the neuron's **receptive field**, and $f$ is usually a **rectification**, since firing rates cannot go below zero: $f(z) = \max(0, z)$. This is the **linear-nonlinear** (LN) model, which you will fit to real data in Week 35.

The dot product says something precise: the neuron responds most to stimuli that look like its receptive field (largest projection), not at all to stimuli orthogonal to it, and is pushed below baseline by stimuli that look like its opposite.

<Step n={2} title="Receptive fields as weight vectors" minutes={35} />

Hubel and Wiesel found neurons in primary visual cortex that respond to bars or edges at one orientation. A good model of such a **simple cell** is a **Gabor** receptive field: a stripe pattern under a Gaussian window. An image patch of 21 by 21 pixels is a vector of 441 numbers; so is the receptive field. The response is their dot product.

<PyCell code={String.raw`
  import numpy as np
  import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

  def gabor(size=21, theta=0.0, wavelength=8.0, sigma=4.0):
      """A Gabor receptive field: stripes at angle theta (radians) under a Gaussian window."""
      half = size // 2
      y, x = np.mgrid[-half:half + 1, -half:half + 1]
      xr = x * np.cos(theta) + y * np.sin(theta)
      return np.exp(-(x ** 2 + y ** 2) / (2 * sigma ** 2)) * np.cos(2 * np.pi * xr / wavelength)

  def grating(size=21, theta=0.0, wavelength=8.0):
      half = size // 2
      y, x = np.mgrid[-half:half + 1, -half:half + 1]
      return np.cos(2 * np.pi * (x * np.cos(theta) + y * np.sin(theta)) / wavelength)

  rf = gabor(theta=np.radians(30))
  fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 4, figsize=(9, 2.4))
  for ax, (img, t) in zip(axes, [(rf, "receptive field"), (grating(theta=np.radians(30)), "grating 30°"),
                                 (grating(theta=np.radians(120)), "grating 120°"), (-grating(theta=np.radians(30)), "grating 30°, inverted")]):
      ax.imshow(img, cmap="RdBu_r", vmin=-1, vmax=1); ax.set_title(t, fontsize=9); ax.axis("off")
  plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

  for name, img in [("30°", grating(theta=np.radians(30))), ("120°", grating(theta=np.radians(120))), ("30° inverted", -grating(theta=np.radians(30)))]:
      print(f"{name:>13}: w · x = {rf.ravel() @ img.ravel():8.2f}")
`} />

The matched grating gives a large positive dot product, the orthogonal one almost zero, and the inverted one a large negative value, which rectification turns into silence. That answers today's prediction: orthogonal stimuli give no response (above baseline), because the dot product is near zero.

<Step n={3} title="Cosine tuning in motor cortex" minutes={30} />

In this week's classic paper (Georgopoulos and colleagues, 1982), monkeys moved a handle to targets in eight directions while single neurons in motor cortex were recorded. Many neurons fired most for one direction and progressively less for others, following a cosine:

$$
r(\phi) = b_0 + k \cos(\phi - \phi_{\text{pref}})
$$

where $\phi$ is the movement direction, $\phi_{\text{pref}}$ the neuron's **preferred direction**, $b_0$ its baseline and $k$ its depth of modulation.

<Derivation title="Cosine tuning is a dot product">
  <div>

Write the movement direction and the preferred direction as unit vectors: $\mathbf{d} = (\cos\phi, \sin\phi)$ and $\mathbf{p} = (\cos\phi_{\text{pref}}, \sin\phi_{\text{pref}})$.

  </div>
  <div>

Their dot product is $\mathbf{d} \cdot \mathbf{p} = \cos\phi\cos\phi_{\text{pref}} + \sin\phi\sin\phi_{\text{pref}}$.

  </div>
  <div>

By the angle-difference identity, $\cos\phi\cos\phi_{\text{pref}} + \sin\phi\sin\phi_{\text{pref}} = \cos(\phi - \phi_{\text{pref}})$.

  </div>
  <div>

So the tuning curve is a linear neuron whose input is the movement direction:

$$
r = b_0 + k\,(\mathbf{d} \cdot \mathbf{p})
$$

The preferred direction $\mathbf{p}$ plays the part of the weight vector. The same equation holds in three dimensions, which the 1986 paper used.

  </div>
</Derivation>

<Step n={4} title="Estimating a preferred direction" minutes={20} />

Given a neuron's rates for eight evenly spaced directions, how do you find its preferred direction? Treat each direction as a unit vector, weight it by the rate, and add: the sum points towards the preferred direction.

      x = np.sum(rates * np.cos(dirs))

<Prove title="Why the weighted sum works" task="For evenly spaced directions $\phi_j = 2\pi j / N$ with $N \ge 3$, use $\sum_j \cos\phi_j = \sum_j \sin\phi_j = 0$ and $\sum_j \cos^2\phi_j = \sum_j \sin^2\phi_j = N/2$, $\sum_j \cos\phi_j \sin\phi_j = 0$ to show that $\sum_j r_j \mathbf{d}_j = \tfrac{Nk}{2}\,\mathbf{p}$ for a cosine-tuned neuron. Why does the baseline $b_0$ drop out?">

Write $r_j = b_0 + k\,\mathbf{d}_j \cdot \mathbf{p}$. Then

$$
\sum_j r_j \mathbf{d}_j = b_0 \sum_j \mathbf{d}_j + k \sum_j (\mathbf{d}_j \cdot \mathbf{p})\,\mathbf{d}_j
$$

The first sum is $(\sum_j \cos\phi_j, \sum_j \sin\phi_j) = (0, 0)$: evenly spaced unit vectors cancel, so the baseline drops out.

For the second, take the x-entry: $\sum_j (\cos\phi_j\, p_x + \sin\phi_j\, p_y)\cos\phi_j = p_x \tfrac{N}{2} + p_y \cdot 0$. The y-entry is $p_y \tfrac{N}{2}$ in the same way. So the sum is $\tfrac{Nk}{2}\,\mathbf{p}$, which points exactly along the preferred direction.

If directions are not evenly spaced, the first sum is not zero and the estimate is biased towards over-sampled directions. Tomorrow the same argument, applied to many neurons, gives the population vector.

</Prove>
