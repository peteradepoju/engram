<Step n={1} title="Linear combinations" minutes={20} />

A **linear combination** of vectors $\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_k$ is

$$
c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + c_2\mathbf{v}_2 + \dots + c_k\mathbf{v}_k
$$

for any numbers $c_1, \dots, c_k$, called **coefficients** or **weights**. Scale, then add: the only two operations from Day 1.

If you stack the vectors as the **columns** of a matrix $V$ and the coefficients in a vector $\mathbf{c}$, the linear combination is the matrix-vector product $V\mathbf{c}$. That is the definition you will build on for the rest of the course: **a matrix times a vector is a linear combination of the matrix's columns.**

  import numpy as np

  c = np.array([1.5, 1.0])
  by_hand = c[0] * v1 + c[1] * v2
  V = np.column_stack([v1, v2])         # vectors as columns, shape (2, 2)
  print("by hand:", by_hand, "  as V @ c:", V @ c)

<Step n={2} title="Span, independence and basis" minutes={30} />

- The **span** of a set of vectors is every point you can reach with linear combinations of them.
- The vectors are **linearly independent** if none of them is a linear combination of the others. Equivalently, the only combination that gives zero is the one with all coefficients zero.
- A **basis** of a space is a set of independent vectors that spans it. Every point then has exactly one set of coefficients: its **coordinates** in that basis.

In $\mathbb{R}^n$ any basis has exactly $n$ vectors. Two independent vectors span the plane; two parallel ones span only a line; three vectors in the plane can never be independent.

The **rank** of a matrix is the number of independent columns, which is the dimension of their span. NumPy computes it with `np.linalg.matrix_rank`.

<Step n={3} title="Coordinates in a new basis" minutes={20} />

The usual coordinates $(x, y)$ are coefficients in the **standard basis** $\mathbf{e}_1 = (1, 0)$, $\mathbf{e}_2 = (0, 1)$. In another basis $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}$, the coordinates of a point $\mathbf{w}$ are the $c_1, c_2$ that solve $c_1\mathbf{u} + c_2\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{w}$, which is the linear system $V\mathbf{c} = \mathbf{w}$.

<Derivation title="Solving for coordinates in two dimensions">
  <div>

Write $c_1\mathbf{u} + c_2\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{w}$ entry by entry:

$$
u_1 c_1 + v_1 c_2 = w_1, \qquad u_2 c_1 + v_2 c_2 = w_2
$$

  </div>
  <div>

Multiply the first equation by $v_2$, the second by $v_1$, and subtract to remove $c_2$:

$$
(u_1 v_2 - u_2 v_1)\,c_1 = w_1 v_2 - w_2 v_1
$$

  </div>
  <div>

The number $u_1 v_2 - u_2 v_1$ is the **determinant** of $V$. If it is not zero, $c_1 = (w_1 v_2 - w_2 v_1)/(u_1 v_2 - u_2 v_1)$, and similarly for $c_2$.

  </div>
  <div>

If the determinant is zero, $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ are parallel: they are not a basis, and most points have no coordinates at all. You will meet determinants properly in Week 6.

  </div>
</Derivation>

<Step n={4} title="The population vector" minutes={35} />

Now the payoff. Each motor-cortex neuron $i$ has a preferred direction $\mathbf{p}_i$ (a unit vector) and cosine tuning, $r_i = b_0 + k\,\mathbf{d} \cdot \mathbf{p}_i$. Georgopoulos and colleagues (1986, this week's deep read) asked: can we recover the movement direction $\mathbf{d}$ from all the rates? Their answer was a linear combination of the preferred directions, weighted by how much each neuron fired above baseline:

$$
\mathbf{P} = \sum_i \frac{r_i - b_0}{k}\,\mathbf{p}_i
$$

      d = np.radians(direction_deg); p = np.radians(prefs_deg)
      mean = (b0 + k * np.cos(d - p)) * T

solution={String.raw`
  def pop_vector(rates, prefs_deg, b0=20.0, k=15.0):
      w = (rates - b0) / k
      p = np.radians(prefs_deg)
      x, y = np.sum(w * np.cos(p)), np.sum(w * np.sin(p))
      return np.degrees(np.arctan2(y, x)) % 360

  def ang_err(a, b):
      return np.abs((a - b + 180) % 360 - 180)

  sizes = [5, 10, 20, 50, 100, 200, 500]
  errors = []
  for n in sizes:
      errs = []
      for _ in range(200):
          prefs = rng.uniform(0, 360, n)
          true = rng.uniform(0, 360)
          errs.append(ang_err(pop_vector(simulate_rates(true, prefs), prefs), true))
      errors.append(np.mean(errs))

  plt.figure(figsize=(5, 3)); plt.loglog(sizes, errors, "o-")
  plt.xlabel("number of neurons"); plt.ylabel("mean error (deg)"); plt.show()

On a log-log plot the errors fall along a straight line with slope about $-1/2$: error shrinks like $1/\sqrt{N}$, the same law as the SEM in Week 1, because independent noise averages out. With 100 neurons the error is a few degrees, which answers today's prediction: broadly tuned, noisy neurons together are precise.

The baseline-cancelling trick from yesterday's proof needs evenly spread preferred directions. When they cluster, the preferred-direction vectors are no longer balanced and the sum is pulled towards the cluster. (Try a movement at 135°: the bias vanishes, because the cluster centred on 45° then sits exactly at right angles to the movement and its pulls cancel. Bias depends on both the cluster and the direction.) Better linear decoders choose the weights from data instead of fixing them to $\mathbf{p}_i$ (Salinas and Abbott, 1994); you will build one with least squares next week.

<li>A matrix times a vector is a linear combination of its columns.</li>
<li>Independent vectors that span a space form a basis; each point then has unique coordinates.</li>
<li>Rank counts the dimensions a set of vectors really spans.</li>
<li>The population vector reads direction as a weighted sum of preferred directions; its error falls like one over root N, but it is biased when preferences are uneven.</li>
