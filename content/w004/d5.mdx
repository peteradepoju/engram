<Step n={1} title="Orthonormal bases" minutes={20} />

Vectors are **orthogonal** when their dot product is zero. A set is **orthonormal** when every vector has length 1 and every pair is orthogonal:

$$
\mathbf{q}_i \cdot \mathbf{q}_j = \begin{cases} 1 & i = j \\ 0 & i \neq j \end{cases}
$$

Orthonormal bases are the easiest coordinates there are. Yesterday you needed to solve a linear system to find coordinates; with an orthonormal basis each coordinate is just a dot product, $c_i = \mathbf{w} \cdot \mathbf{q}_i$, because every other basis vector contributes zero to it.

If the vectors are the columns of a matrix $Q$, orthonormality says $Q^\top Q = I$, the identity matrix (all coordinates computed at once by $Q^\top \mathbf{w}$). You will meet transposes properly next week; for now $Q^\top Q$ is the table of all dot products between columns.

<Step n={2} title="Gram-Schmidt" minutes={25} />

Any set of independent vectors can be turned into an orthonormal set with the same span. The **Gram-Schmidt** process takes the vectors one at a time: remove from each its projections onto all the earlier ones (leaving only the new direction), then scale it to length 1.

<Derivation title="One step of Gram-Schmidt">
  <div>

Suppose $\mathbf{q}_1, \dots, \mathbf{q}_{k-1}$ are already orthonormal and $\mathbf{v}_k$ is the next vector.

  </div>
  <div>

Remove its shadow on each earlier direction (Day 2's vector projection):

$$
\mathbf{u}_k = \mathbf{v}_k - \sum_{j=1}^{k-1} (\mathbf{v}_k \cdot \mathbf{q}_j)\,\mathbf{q}_j
$$

  </div>
  <div>

Check that it is orthogonal to each earlier $\mathbf{q}_i$: $\mathbf{u}_k \cdot \mathbf{q}_i = \mathbf{v}_k \cdot \mathbf{q}_i - (\mathbf{v}_k \cdot \mathbf{q}_i)(\mathbf{q}_i \cdot \mathbf{q}_i) = 0$, since every other $\mathbf{q}_j \cdot \mathbf{q}_i$ is zero and $\mathbf{q}_i \cdot \mathbf{q}_i = 1$.

  </div>
  <div>

Normalise: $\mathbf{q}_k = \mathbf{u}_k / \lVert \mathbf{u}_k \rVert$. If $\mathbf{u}_k = \mathbf{0}$, then $\mathbf{v}_k$ was already in the span of the earlier vectors: they were not independent.

  </div>
</Derivation>

  def gram_schmidt(V):
      Q = np.zeros_like(V, dtype=float)
      for k in range(V.shape[1]):
          u = V[:, k].astype(float)
          for j in range(k):
              u = u - (u @ Q[:, j]) * Q[:, j]

<Step n={3} title="Projecting out a signal" minutes={25} />

Population activity often contains a signal you want to remove: a slow drift, a global change in arousal that raises every neuron's rate, or the movement itself when you want to study preparation. If the unwanted signal lies along a unit direction $\mathbf{u}$, remove it from every state by subtracting the projection:

$$
\mathbf{x}' = \mathbf{x} - (\mathbf{x} \cdot \mathbf{u})\,\mathbf{u}
$$

What remains is orthogonal to $\mathbf{u}$: a readout along $\mathbf{u}$ now sees nothing.

This idea answers today's prediction, and it is one of the most influential ideas in motor neuroscience. Kaufman, Churchland, Ryu and Shenoy (2014) proposed that motor cortex prepares movements in an **output-null** subspace: patterns of activity that are orthogonal to the directions the muscles read out. Preparation can then change freely without moving the arm. Activity moves into the **output-potent** directions only when the movement begins. You will analyse this in Week 116.

<Step n={4} title="Representational similarity" minutes={35} />

How do you compare the visual cortex of a monkey with a layer of a deep network? They have different numbers of units, measured in different ways. Representational similarity analysis (Kriegeskorte, Mur and Bandettini, 2008, today's paper) side-steps the problem: for each system, measure how **dissimilar** its responses to every pair of stimuli are. The result is a stimulus-by-stimulus matrix, the **representational dissimilarity matrix** (RDM). Two systems represent the world alike if their RDMs are alike, whatever their units.

A common dissimilarity is one minus the correlation between the two response vectors, $1 - r$: 0 for identical patterns, 1 for unrelated ones, 2 for opposite ones.

The good model has 25 units and the brain has 60, yet their RDMs can be compared directly. Kriegeskorte and colleagues usually compare RDMs with a rank correlation (Spearman) rather than Pearson, because a model need only get the order of dissimilarities right. <Prove title="Projection removes exactly one dimension" task="Let $\mathbf{u}$ be a unit vector and $\mathbf{x}' = \mathbf{x} - (\mathbf{x} \cdot \mathbf{u})\mathbf{u}$. Show that (a) $\mathbf{x}' \cdot \mathbf{u} = 0$, (b) $\mathbf{x}'' = \mathbf{x}'$ if you project again, and (c) $\lVert \mathbf{x} \rVert^2 = \lVert \mathbf{x}' \rVert^2 + (\mathbf{x} \cdot \mathbf{u})^2$.">

(a) $\mathbf{x}' \cdot \mathbf{u} = \mathbf{x} \cdot \mathbf{u} - (\mathbf{x} \cdot \mathbf{u})(\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{u}) = \mathbf{x} \cdot \mathbf{u} - \mathbf{x} \cdot \mathbf{u} = 0$, since $\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{u} = 1$.

(b) Projecting again subtracts $(\mathbf{x}' \cdot \mathbf{u})\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{0}$, so nothing changes. Projections are **idempotent**: doing them twice is the same as once.

(c) $\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{x}' + (\mathbf{x} \cdot \mathbf{u})\mathbf{u}$, and the two parts are orthogonal by (a). By the Day 1 result, the squared length of a sum of orthogonal vectors is the sum of their squared lengths. So the variance of activity splits cleanly into the part along $\mathbf{u}$ and the rest: the principle behind PCA.

</Prove>
