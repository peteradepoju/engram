<Step n={1} title="What a matrix is" minutes={15} />

A **matrix** is a rectangular table of numbers. A matrix with $m$ rows and $n$ columns has **shape** $m \times n$; its entry in row $i$ and column $j$ is $W_{ij}$.

In neuroscience the most useful matrix is a **weight matrix**: $W_{ij}$ is the strength of the connection from input neuron $j$ to output neuron $i$. Row $i$ lists everything that output neuron $i$ listens to (its receptive field, last week's weight vector). Column $j$ lists where input neuron $j$ sends its signal. For today's prediction: 200 rows, 1,000 columns, 200,000 numbers.

$$
W = \begin{pmatrix} W_{11} & W_{12} & \cdots & W_{1n} \\ \vdots & & & \vdots \\ W_{m1} & W_{m2} & \cdots & W_{mn} \end{pmatrix}
$$

<Step n={2} title="Two ways to multiply" minutes={25} />

The product $\mathbf{y} = W\mathbf{x}$ of an $m \times n$ matrix with a vector of length $n$ is a vector of length $m$. There are two equally true ways to compute it.

**Row view.** Entry $i$ of the output is the dot product of row $i$ with $\mathbf{x}$:

$$
y_i = \sum_{j=1}^{n} W_{ij} x_j
$$

Each output neuron takes a weighted sum of all its inputs, exactly last week's linear neuron.

**Column view.** The output is a linear combination of the columns, weighted by the entries of $\mathbf{x}$ (Week 4, Day 4):

$$
W\mathbf{x} = x_1\,\mathbf{w}_{:,1} + x_2\,\mathbf{w}_{:,2} + \dots + x_n\,\mathbf{w}_{:,n}
$$

Each input neuron adds its column (its pattern of outgoing connections) into the output, scaled by how active it is.

The shapes must line up: an $m \times n$ matrix needs a vector of length $n$, and gives one of length $m$. When a NumPy `@` raises a shape error, write the shapes next to each other and check that the inner numbers match.

<Step n={3} title="Matrices move space" minutes={30} />

A matrix sends every input vector to an output vector, so it moves every point of a space. These moves are **linear**: they keep the origin fixed and keep lines straight, parallel and evenly spaced. Formally, $W(a\mathbf{x} + b\mathbf{y}) = aW\mathbf{x} + bW\mathbf{y}$.

The column view gives the key fact: **the columns of a matrix are where the basis vectors land.** $W\mathbf{e}_1$ is the first column, $W\mathbf{e}_2$ the second. Know where the basis vectors go and you know where everything goes.

| Matrix | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| $\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ | Scales everything by 2 |
| $\begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{pmatrix}$ | Rotates by $\theta$ anticlockwise |
| $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & k \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ | Shears: pushes points sideways in proportion to their height |
| $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ | Projects onto the x axis, flattening the plane |

A rotation's columns are orthonormal (Week 4, Day 5), which is why it preserves every length and angle. Matrices with orthonormal columns are called **orthogonal** matrices; you will see them again as the "rotation" part of every matrix in Week 7 (SVD).

<Step n={4} title="A layer of neurons" minutes={30} />

Put last week's linear-nonlinear neuron in a row, many times over, and you have a layer:

$$
\mathbf{r} = f(W\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b})
$$

where $\mathbf{x}$ holds the input rates, $W$ the weights, $\mathbf{b}$ each neuron's bias and $f$ is applied to every entry. This one line describes a population of model V1 cells receiving input from the LGN, and also every layer of a deep network.

The batch version used the **transpose** $W^\top$ (rows and columns swapped) so that trials could sit in rows. Machine learning code almost always stores data with one example per row, which is why you will write `X @ W.T` far more often than `W @ x`. Tomorrow you will meet transposes properly.

<Prove title="Columns determine the matrix" task="Show that if you know $W\mathbf{e}_1, \dots, W\mathbf{e}_n$ for the standard basis vectors, you know $W\mathbf{x}$ for every $\mathbf{x}$. Use this to write down the matrix that sends (1, 0) to (0, 1) and (0, 1) to (−1, 0). Which transformation is it?">

Any $\mathbf{x}$ is $x_1\mathbf{e}_1 + \dots + x_n\mathbf{e}_n$. Linearity gives $W\mathbf{x} = x_1 W\mathbf{e}_1 + \dots + x_n W\mathbf{e}_n$, so the images of the basis vectors fix everything.

Those images are the columns: $W = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$. It sends (1, 0) up to (0, 1) and (0, 1) to (−1, 0): a rotation by 90° anticlockwise. Check it against the rotation formula with $\theta = 90°$.

</Prove>

<Key title="Before you go">
<ul>
<li>A matrix is a table of weights; rows are outputs' receptive fields, columns are inputs' projections.</li>
<li>$W\mathbf{x}$ is dot products with rows, or a combination of columns.</li>
<li>A matrix is a linear transformation; its columns are where the basis vectors go.</li>
<li>A layer of neurons is $f(W\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b})$, in brains and networks alike.</li>
