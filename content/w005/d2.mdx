<Step n={1} title="Composition" minutes={20} />

Suppose $B$ transforms $\mathbf{x}$ into $B\mathbf{x}$, and then $A$ transforms the result. The combined effect $A(B\mathbf{x})$ is also linear, so it has a matrix. We define the **product** $AB$ to be that matrix:

$$
(AB)\mathbf{x} = A(B\mathbf{x})
$$

Read products from right to left: in $AB$, $B$ acts first. In network terms, if $W_1$ maps inputs to hidden units and $W_2$ maps hidden units to outputs, the combined linear map is $W_2 W_1$.

<Step n={2} title="Computing products" minutes={30} />

Where does column $j$ of $AB$ come from? Column $j$ of $AB$ is $(AB)\mathbf{e}_j = A(B\mathbf{e}_j) = A\,\mathbf{b}_{:,j}$: just $A$ times column $j$ of $B$. Written out entry by entry:

$$
(AB)_{ij} = \sum_{k} A_{ik} B_{kj}
$$

Entry $(i, j)$ is the dot product of row $i$ of $A$ with column $j$ of $B$. So the inner dimensions must match: an $m \times k$ matrix times a $k \times n$ matrix gives an $m \times n$ matrix.

Multiplying an $m \times k$ by a $k \times n$ matrix takes $mkn$ multiplications. GPUs are fast because they do thousands of these at once, and training a large network is mostly matrix multiplication.

<Step n={3} title="Order and the transpose" minutes={25} />

Rotating then stretching is not the same as stretching then rotating. In general $AB \neq BA$.

<Figure caption="Apply A then B, or B then A. The two composite transformations usually differ." credit="Interactive figure by Engram.">
  <Compose />
</Figure>

The **transpose** $A^\top$ swaps rows and columns: $(A^\top)_{ij} = A_{ji}$. An $m \times n$ matrix becomes $n \times m$. Two rules you will use constantly:

- A dot product is a product with a transpose: $\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} = \mathbf{a}^\top \mathbf{b}$, treating vectors as columns.
- The transpose of a product reverses the order: $(AB)^\top = B^\top A^\top$.

<Derivation title="Why (AB)ᵀ = BᵀAᵀ">
  <div>

Entry $(i, j)$ of $(AB)^\top$ is entry $(j, i)$ of $AB$: $\sum_k A_{jk} B_{ki}$.

  </div>
  <div>

Entry $(i, j)$ of $B^\top A^\top$ is $\sum_k (B^\top)_{ik}(A^\top)_{kj} = \sum_k B_{ki} A_{jk}$.

  </div>
  <div>

The two sums are the same products in a different order, so the matrices are equal. The order reverses because transposing swaps which matrix supplies the rows.

  </div>
</Derivation>

Two products with the transpose appear everywhere in data analysis. For a data matrix $X$ with trials in rows and neurons in columns:

- $XX^\top$ (trials by trials) holds the dot product between every pair of trials: how similar their population patterns are.
- $X^\top X$ (neurons by neurons) holds the dot product between every pair of neurons across trials: how much they fire together. After centring each neuron, it is proportional to the covariance matrix, the heart of PCA (Week 7).

<Step n={4} title="Why depth needs nonlinearity" minutes={30} />

Because $(W_2 W_1)\mathbf{x} = W_2(W_1\mathbf{x})$, two linear layers are exactly one linear layer with weights $W = W_2 W_1$. So are a hundred. Stacking linear layers adds nothing; that answers today's prediction. Depth only helps when there is a **nonlinearity** between the layers.

This is why every neural network puts a nonlinearity (ReLU, sigmoid, tanh) between layers. Combined with Week 1's result that a single layer cannot compute XOR, it explains why this week's classic paper (Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams, 1986) mattered so much: it showed how to train the hidden layers of nonlinear networks.

<Prove title="Matrix products as sums of outer products" task="The outer product of a column vector $\mathbf{u}$ ($m$ entries) and a row vector $\mathbf{v}^\top$ ($n$ entries) is the $m \times n$ matrix $\mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^\top$ with entries $u_i v_j$. Show that $AB = \sum_k \mathbf{a}_{:,k}\,\mathbf{b}_{k,:}$: the sum, over $k$, of column $k$ of $A$ times row $k$ of $B$.">

Entry $(i, j)$ of the sum is $\sum_k (\mathbf{a}_{:,k}\,\mathbf{b}_{k,:})_{ij} = \sum_k A_{ik} B_{kj}$, which is exactly $(AB)_{ij}$.

So a product is a sum of rank-one pieces, one per inner index. If only a few pieces matter, the product is approximately low rank. That is the idea behind the SVD and PCA (Week 7) and behind low-rank models of neural connectivity.

</Prove>
