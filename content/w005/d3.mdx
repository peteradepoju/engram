<Step n={1} title="Two pictures of a system" minutes={20} />

A **linear system** is a set of equations like

$$
\begin{aligned} 2x_1 + x_2 &= 4 \\ x_1 + 3x_2 &= 7 \end{aligned}
\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 4 \\ 7 \end{pmatrix}
$$

written compactly as $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$. As with matrix-vector products, there are two ways to see it:

- **Row picture.** Each equation is a line (a plane, in 3D). The solution is where they all meet.
- **Column picture.** Find the combination of $A$'s columns that equals $\mathbf{b}$. A solution exists exactly when $\mathbf{b}$ is in the span of the columns.

<Step n={2} title="Elimination" minutes={25} />

**Gaussian elimination** solves a system by subtracting multiples of one equation from the others until the system is triangular, then solving from the bottom up (**back substitution**).

<Derivation title="Elimination on the example">
  <div>

Start: $2x_1 + x_2 = 4$ and $x_1 + 3x_2 = 7$.

  </div>
  <div>

Remove $x_1$ from the second equation by subtracting $\tfrac{1}{2}$ times the first: $(3 - \tfrac{1}{2})x_2 = 7 - 2$, so $\tfrac{5}{2}x_2 = 5$.

  </div>
  <div>

Back substitute: $x_2 = 2$, then $2x_1 + 2 = 4$ gives $x_1 = 1$.

  </div>
  <div>

The numbers we divided by (2, then $\tfrac{5}{2}$) are the **pivots**. If a pivot is zero and no row swap fixes it, the matrix is singular: no unique solution exists.

  </div>
</Derivation>

  def eliminate(A, b):
      M = np.column_stack([A.astype(float), b.astype(float)])
      n = len(b)
      for c in range(n):
          p = c + np.argmax(np.abs(M[c:, c]))
          M[[c, p]] = M[[p, c]]
          for r in range(c + 1, n):
              M[r] = M[r] - (M[r, c] / M[c, c]) * M[c]
      x = np.zeros(n)
      for r in range(n - 1, -1, -1):
          x[r] = (M[r, n] - M[r, r + 1:n] @ x[r + 1:]) / M[r, r]

<Step n={3} title="Inverses and unmixing" minutes={25} />

If a square matrix $A$ can be undone, its **inverse** $A^{-1}$ satisfies $A^{-1}A = AA^{-1} = I$, and the solution of $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ is $\mathbf{x} = A^{-1}\mathbf{b}$. An inverse exists exactly when $A$ is **invertible** (non-singular), and these all say the same thing:

- the columns are independent (rank $n$);
- the determinant is not zero;
- $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$ only for $\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$;
- every $\mathbf{b}$ has exactly one solution.

In practice, never compute an inverse just to solve a system: `np.linalg.solve(A, b)` is faster and more accurate than `np.linalg.inv(A) @ b`.

In real recordings you rarely know the mixing matrix. Estimating it from the data, assuming the sources are independent, is **independent component analysis** (ICA, Week 61), used to separate brain signals from eye-blink and muscle artefacts in EEG.

<Step n={4} title="Null spaces" minutes={20} />

When a matrix is not invertible, some non-zero inputs produce zero output. The set of all such $\mathbf{x}$ with $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$ is the **null space**. Anything in it is invisible: adding it to an input does not change the output at all, because $A(\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{n}) = A\mathbf{x} + A\mathbf{n} = A\mathbf{x}$.

This is exactly last week's **output-null** idea: if muscles read motor cortex through a matrix $A$, preparatory activity in $A$'s null space moves nothing.

A wide matrix (fewer outputs than inputs) always has a null space. That matters when you try to recover inputs from outputs: with 3 unknowns and 2 measurements, infinitely many inputs fit, and you need extra assumptions to choose one. That is the setting of tomorrow's least squares and Day 5's regularisation.

<Step n={5} title="Ill-conditioning" minutes={15} />

A matrix can be invertible and still dangerous. If its columns are nearly parallel, a tiny change in $\mathbf{b}$ (measurement noise) causes a huge change in the solution. The **condition number** (`np.linalg.cond`) measures this: roughly, relative errors in $\mathbf{b}$ can be multiplied by up to this factor in $\mathbf{x}$.

<PyCell code={String.raw`
  for eps in [1.0, 0.1, 0.01]:
      A = np.array([[1.0, 1.0], [1.0, 1.0 + eps]])      # columns become nearly parallel as eps shrinks
      b = np.array([2.0, 2.0 + eps])
      x = np.linalg.solve(A, b)
      x_noisy = np.linalg.solve(A, b + np.array([0.0, 0.001]))   # 0.1 per cent noise in one measurement
      print(f"eps={eps:5}: cond = {np.linalg.cond(A):8.1f}   x = {np.round(x, 3)}   with tiny noise: {np.round(x_noisy, 3)}")

When electrodes record nearly the same mixture, or when two neurons in an encoding model carry nearly the same signal, the system is ill-conditioned and the solution is unreliable. Regularisation, on Day 5, is the standard cure.

<Prove title="The inverse of a 2 by 2 matrix" task="For $A = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}$ with $ad - bc \neq 0$, verify that $A^{-1} = \dfrac{1}{ad - bc}\begin{pmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{pmatrix}$ by multiplying it with $A$. Use it to solve the worked example.">

Multiply: $\begin{pmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} da - bc & db - bd \\ -ca + ac & -cb + ad \end{pmatrix} = (ad - bc)\,I$. Dividing by $ad - bc$ gives $I$.

For the example, $ad - bc = 2 \times 3 - 1 \times 1 = 5$, so $A^{-1} = \tfrac{1}{5}\begin{pmatrix} 3 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ and $\mathbf{x} = A^{-1}(4, 7) = \tfrac{1}{5}(12 - 7, -4 + 14) = (1, 2)$, matching elimination.

The factor $1/(ad - bc)$ shows directly why a zero determinant means no inverse, and why a small one amplifies errors.

</Prove>

<Key title="Before you go">
<ul>
<li>A linear system is lines meeting (rows) or columns combining (columns).</li>
<li>Elimination solves it; a square matrix is invertible when its columns are independent.</li>
<li>The null space holds inputs a readout cannot see.</li>
<li>Ill-conditioned systems amplify noise: check the condition number before trusting a solution.</li>
</ul>
