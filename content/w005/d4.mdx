<Step n={1} title="The least-squares problem" minutes={20} />

You show a neuron 200 stimuli and record 200 responses $y_1, \dots, y_{200}$. Your model says the response is a weighted sum of a few stimulus **features** plus noise:

$$
y_t = w_1 x_{t1} + w_2 x_{t2} + \dots + w_p x_{tp} + \varepsilon_t
\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
\mathbf{y} = X\mathbf{w} + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}
$$

$X$ is the **design matrix**: one row per trial, one column per feature. With 200 equations and only $p$ unknowns there is usually no exact solution. **Least squares** chooses the weights that make the total squared error as small as possible:

$$
\hat{\mathbf{w}} = \arg\min_{\mathbf{w}} \lVert \mathbf{y} - X\mathbf{w} \rVert^2
$$

<Step n={2} title="The normal equations" minutes={30} />

Every possible prediction $X\mathbf{w}$ is a combination of the columns of $X$, so the predictions form the column space of $X$ (Week 4, Day 4). The data $\mathbf{y}$ usually sit outside it. The closest point is the **projection** of $\mathbf{y}$ onto the column space.

<Derivation title="Least squares from projection">
  <div>

The closest point $X\hat{\mathbf{w}}$ is the one where the error $\mathbf{y} - X\hat{\mathbf{w}}$ is **orthogonal** to the column space, like the dashed line from a point to its shadow (Week 4, Day 2).

  </div>
  <div>

Orthogonal to the column space means orthogonal to every column, so every column's dot product with the error is zero:

$$
X^\top (\mathbf{y} - X\hat{\mathbf{w}}) = \mathbf{0}
$$

  </div>
  <div>

Rearrange to get the **normal equations**:

$$
X^\top X\,\hat{\mathbf{w}} = X^\top \mathbf{y}
$$

  </div>
  <div>

If the columns of $X$ are independent, $X^\top X$ is invertible and

$$
\hat{\mathbf{w}} = (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top \mathbf{y}
$$

The same result comes from setting the gradient of the squared error to zero, which you will do in Week 12.

  </div>
</Derivation>

To include an intercept (a baseline firing rate), add a column of ones to $X$. In practice use `np.linalg.lstsq(X, y, rcond=None)`, which solves the problem stably without forming $X^\top X$.

**How good is the fit?** The coefficient of determination compares the model's squared error with that of just predicting the mean:

$$
R^2 = 1 - \frac{\sum_t (y_t - \hat{y}_t)^2}{\sum_t (y_t - \bar{y})^2}
$$

1 is a perfect fit, 0 is no better than the mean. Computed on the trials used for fitting, $R^2$ always looks better than the truth, so encoding models report it on **held-out** trials (this week's deep read explains why).

<Step n={3} title="Fitting a tuning curve" minutes={25} />

"Linear" regression means linear in the **weights**, not in the stimulus. Any fixed nonlinear features can go in the design matrix. An orientation tuning curve that is roughly a cosine of twice the angle can be written as

$$
r(\theta) = w_0 + w_1 \cos 2\theta + w_2 \sin 2\theta
$$

which is linear in $w_0, w_1, w_2$. The preferred orientation is then $\tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{atan2}(w_2, w_1)$, the same angle trick as last week's preferred direction.

<Step n={4} title="Estimating a receptive field" minutes={30} />

Now the encoding model of today's deep read in miniature. A model neuron has a 1D receptive field of 40 "pixels". You show 2,000 random stimuli and record noisy responses $\mathbf{y} = X\mathbf{w} + \boldsymbol\varepsilon$. Two estimates of $\mathbf{w}$:

- The **spike-triggered average** (STA): $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{\text{STA}} = \frac{1}{n} X^\top \mathbf{y}$, the response-weighted average stimulus.
- **Least squares**: $\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{\text{LS}} = (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top \mathbf{y}$.

They differ by the factor $(X^\top X)^{-1}$, which corrects for correlations between pixels. For white noise, $X^\top X \approx nI$ and the two agree. For correlated stimuli, they do not.

The STA comes out blurred: the stimulus correlations smear it. That answers today's prediction. Least squares undoes the blur, but it pays a price, because $(X^\top X)^{-1}$ amplifies noise along the directions the stimuli rarely explore (Day 3's ill-conditioning). With fewer trials the least-squares estimate becomes noisy; tomorrow's ridge regression balances the two.

<Prove title="The residual is orthogonal to the predictions" task="Show that at the least-squares solution the residual $\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{y} - X\hat{\mathbf{w}}$ is orthogonal to the prediction $\hat{\mathbf{y}} = X\hat{\mathbf{w}}$. Deduce that $\lVert \mathbf{y} \rVert^2 = \lVert \hat{\mathbf{y}} \rVert^2 + \lVert \mathbf{e} \rVert^2$. If $X$ includes a column of ones, show also that the residuals sum to zero.">

From the normal equations, $X^\top \mathbf{e} = \mathbf{0}$. Then $\hat{\mathbf{y}} \cdot \mathbf{e} = \hat{\mathbf{w}}^\top X^\top \mathbf{e} = 0$.

Since $\mathbf{y} = \hat{\mathbf{y}} + \mathbf{e}$ with orthogonal parts, Week 4's Pythagoras result gives $\lVert \mathbf{y} \rVert^2 = \lVert \hat{\mathbf{y}} \rVert^2 + \lVert \mathbf{e} \rVert^2$.

The column of ones is one of the columns of $X$, so its dot product with $\mathbf{e}$ is zero: $\sum_t e_t = 0$. With an intercept, the fit is right on average. Applying the same split to the centred data is what gives $R^2$ its meaning as the fraction of variance explained.

</Prove>

<Key title="Before you go">
<ul>
<li>Least squares projects the data onto what the model can produce.</li>
<li>The normal equations come from the residual being orthogonal to the features.</li>
<li>Linear regression is linear in the weights: tuning curves and receptive fields both fit.</li>
<li>Correlated features blur simple averages; least squares corrects, but amplifies noise.</li>
</ul>
