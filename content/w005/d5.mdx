<Step n={1} title="Linear decoding" minutes={20} />

A **linear decoder** predicts a stimulus variable $s$ (contrast, orientation, reach direction) from the population's rates on each trial:

$$
\hat{s}_t = \beta_0 + \sum_{i=1}^{N} \beta_i r_{ti} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \hat{\mathbf{s}} = R\boldsymbol{\beta}
$$

$R$ has one row per trial and one column per neuron (plus a column of ones). It is yesterday's least squares with the roles reversed: the neurons are the features and the stimulus is the target. The population vector (Week 4) was a linear decoder with weights fixed in advance; regression learns the best weights from data, which is the **optimal linear estimator** of Salinas and Abbott (1994).

<Step n={2} title="Overfitting" minutes={25} />

With $N$ neurons and $n$ trials, least squares has $N + 1$ weights to fit to $n$ numbers. As $N$ approaches $n$, it can fit almost anything, including the noise. Training error falls towards zero while error on new trials grows.

With 95 neurons and 100 trials the training fit is nearly perfect and the test fit collapses. That answers today's prediction: more neurons made the decoder **worse** on new data. The information is there (every neuron is tuned); least squares just cannot use it with so few trials.

<Step n={3} title="Ridge regression" minutes={30} />

Overfit weights are typically huge, with large positive and negative values cancelling to fit the noise. **Ridge regression** adds a penalty on the squared size of the weights:

$$
\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}_\lambda = \arg\min_{\boldsymbol\beta}\ \lVert \mathbf{s} - R\boldsymbol\beta \rVert^2 + \lambda \lVert \boldsymbol\beta \rVert^2
$$

<Derivation title="The ridge solution">
  <div>

Expand the objective: $\mathbf{s}^\top\mathbf{s} - 2\boldsymbol\beta^\top R^\top \mathbf{s} + \boldsymbol\beta^\top R^\top R\,\boldsymbol\beta + \lambda\,\boldsymbol\beta^\top\boldsymbol\beta$.

  </div>
  <div>

Setting the gradient with respect to $\boldsymbol\beta$ to zero (you will learn to take such gradients in Week 9) gives

$$
-2R^\top \mathbf{s} + 2R^\top R\,\boldsymbol\beta + 2\lambda\boldsymbol\beta = \mathbf{0}
$$

  </div>
  <div>

So the ridge weights solve

$$
(R^\top R + \lambda I)\,\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}_\lambda = R^\top \mathbf{s}
$$

which is the normal equations with $\lambda$ added to the diagonal: Hoerl and Kennard's 1970 idea, this week's classic paper.

  </div>
  <div>

$R^\top R$ can be singular or ill-conditioned when neurons are many or correlated. Adding $\lambda I$ with $\lambda \gt 0$ makes it always invertible and well conditioned. With $\lambda = 0$ you get least squares; as $\lambda \to \infty$ the weights shrink to zero.

  </div>
</Derivation>

Penalising the intercept would pull predictions towards zero for no good reason. The usual fix is to **centre** first: subtract each neuron's training mean from its column and the training mean of $\mathbf{s}$ from the target, fit ridge without an intercept, then add the mean of $\mathbf{s}$ back to predictions.

<Step n={4} title="Cross-validation" minutes={30} />

How large should $\lambda$ be? Not too small (overfitting), not too large (all weights crushed). You cannot choose it by looking at test performance, because then the test set is no longer a fair test. **k-fold cross-validation** uses only the training data: split it into $k$ folds; for each fold, fit on the other $k - 1$ and measure error on this one; average over folds; pick the $\lambda$ with the lowest average error. Then refit on all the training data and report performance once on the untouched test set.

<Prove title="Ridge shrinks along each direction" task="Suppose the columns of $R$ are orthonormal, so $R^\top R = I$. Show that the ridge weights are the least-squares weights scaled by $1/(1 + \lambda)$. What happens when the columns are not orthonormal? (Think about directions where $R^\top R$ is small.)">

With $R^\top R = I$, least squares gives $\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}_0 = R^\top\mathbf{s}$ and ridge gives $(I + \lambda I)\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}_\lambda = R^\top\mathbf{s}$, so $\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}_\lambda = \dfrac{1}{1 + \lambda}\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}_0$. Every weight shrinks by the same factor.

In general, along a direction where $R^\top R$ has size $d$ (an eigenvalue, Week 6), least squares divides by $d$ and ridge divides by $d + \lambda$. Where the data are rich ($d$ large) ridge barely changes anything; where the data are thin ($d$ small, the ill-conditioned directions) ridge stops the division blowing up the noise. That is why it helps exactly where least squares fails.

</Prove>

<Key title="Before you go">
<ul>
<li>A linear decoder is least squares from neurons to stimulus.</li>
<li>With many neurons and few trials, least squares overfits: judge models only on held-out data.</li>
<li>Ridge adds λ to the diagonal, shrinking weights and stabilising the fit.</li>
<li>Choose λ by cross-validation on training data, then test once.</li>
</ul>
