<Step n={1} title="The eigen equation" minutes={25} />

A nonzero vector $\mathbf{v}$ is an **eigenvector** of a square matrix $A$ if multiplying by $A$ only scales it:

$$
A\mathbf{v} = \lambda \mathbf{v}
$$

The number $\lambda$ is the **eigenvalue** belonging to $\mathbf{v}$. ("Eigen" is German for "own": the vector's own direction is preserved.) If $\lambda = 2$ the vector doubles, if $\lambda = 0.5$ it halves, if $\lambda = -1$ it flips, and if $\lambda = 0$ it is flattened to the origin.

Two facts follow straight from the definition.

- **Any multiple of an eigenvector is an eigenvector** with the same eigenvalue, since $A(c\mathbf{v}) = cA\mathbf{v} = \lambda(c\mathbf{v})$. So an eigenvector is really a whole line through the origin; we usually pick the one of length 1.
- **Applying $A$ again multiplies by $\lambda$ again**: $A^2\mathbf{v} = A(\lambda\mathbf{v}) = \lambda^2\mathbf{v}$, and $A^k\mathbf{v} = \lambda^k\mathbf{v}$. This is why eigenvalues control repeated processes.

A quick check that $\mathbf{v} = (1, 1)$ is an eigenvector of $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$: $A\mathbf{v} = (3, 3) = 3\mathbf{v}$, so $\lambda = 3$. And $(1, -1)$ gives $(1, -1)$, so $\lambda = 1$. But $(1, 0)$ gives $(2, 1)$, which points somewhere new: not an eigenvector.

Try the presets.

- **stretch** (a diagonal matrix): the axes themselves are the eigenvectors, and the diagonal entries are the eigenvalues.
- **symmetric**: the eigenvectors are at 45 degrees and at right angles to each other. You will see on Day 5 that symmetric matrices always have perpendicular eigenvectors.
- **shear**: only one eigen-direction survives. Such a matrix cannot be described by eigenvectors alone (Day 3).
- **rotate**: no direction stays on its line, so there are no real eigenvectors. The eigenvalues are complex numbers, and on Day 4 you will see that complex eigenvalues mean oscillation.

<Step n={3} title="Computing eigenvectors" minutes={30} />

For anything bigger than a 2 by 2 matrix you let the computer find them. `np.linalg.eig(A)` returns an array of eigenvalues and a matrix whose **columns** are the matching unit eigenvectors. Two things to know: the order is not guaranteed, so sort them yourself, and each eigenvector's sign is arbitrary (both $\mathbf{v}$ and $-\mathbf{v}$ are valid).

Here is a small recurrent circuit: three populations that excite themselves and their neighbours. Entry $W_{ij}$ is the weight from population $j$ onto population $i$.

The single matrix equation $WV = V\Lambda$ packs every eigenvector equation into one line: column $j$ of the left side is $W\mathbf{v}_j$, and column $j$ of the right side is $\lambda_j\mathbf{v}_j$. Tomorrow you will see where the eigenvalues come from; on Day 3 this equation becomes $W = V\Lambda V^{-1}$.

<Step n={4} title="Repeated feedback and the power method" minutes={25} />

Now run the circuit: $\mathbf{x}_{t+1} = W\mathbf{x}_t$. Write the starting activity as a combination of eigenvectors, $\mathbf{x}_0 = c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + c_2\mathbf{v}_2 + c_3\mathbf{v}_3$. Because $W^t\mathbf{v}_j = \lambda_j^t\mathbf{v}_j$,

$$
\mathbf{x}_t = c_1\lambda_1^t\mathbf{v}_1 + c_2\lambda_2^t\mathbf{v}_2 + c_3\lambda_3^t\mathbf{v}_3
$$

Every term shrinks or grows geometrically at its own rate. The term with the largest $|\lambda|$ shrinks most slowly (or grows fastest), so after enough steps it dominates: the *direction* of $\mathbf{x}_t$ approaches $\mathbf{v}_1$ whatever the start (as long as $c_1 \neq 0$). That answers the prediction. Normalising at each step stops the numbers vanishing or exploding, and gives the **power method**, the simplest algorithm for the top eigenvector.

Every run decays at the same rate $\lambda_1^t$ and every run's pattern settles on the same shape. The circuit has a preferred activity pattern, written into its connections, and it is the top eigenvector. Because $\lambda_1 \lt 1$ the activity eventually dies away; push $\lambda_1$ above 1 and it would explode. Recurrent networks in the brain and in machine learning must keep their eigenvalues near this edge, a theme that returns on Day 4 and in Week 86.

<Prove title="Powers and inverses share eigenvectors" task="Suppose $A\mathbf{v} = \lambda\mathbf{v}$. Show that $\mathbf{v}$ is also an eigenvector of $A^3$, of $A + 2I$ and, if $\lambda \neq 0$ and $A$ is invertible, of $A^{-1}$. Give each eigenvalue.">

$A^3\mathbf{v} = A^2(\lambda\mathbf{v}) = \lambda A(\lambda\mathbf{v}) = \lambda^3\mathbf{v}$, so the eigenvalue is $\lambda^3$.

$(A + 2I)\mathbf{v} = A\mathbf{v} + 2\mathbf{v} = (\lambda + 2)\mathbf{v}$, so the eigenvalue is $\lambda + 2$. Adding a multiple of the identity shifts every eigenvalue and leaves the eigenvectors alone, which you will use on Day 4 when the leak of each neuron subtracts $I$.

Multiply $A\mathbf{v} = \lambda\mathbf{v}$ on the left by $A^{-1}$: $\mathbf{v} = \lambda A^{-1}\mathbf{v}$, so $A^{-1}\mathbf{v} = \frac{1}{\lambda}\mathbf{v}$. Directions that $A$ shrinks, $A^{-1}$ stretches, which is why inverting an ill-conditioned matrix (Week 5) amplifies noise.

</Prove>

<Key title="Before you go">
<ul>
<li>An eigenvector is a direction a matrix only stretches: $A\mathbf{v} = \lambda\mathbf{v}$.</li>
<li>Eigenvalues multiply under repetition: $A^k\mathbf{v} = \lambda^k\mathbf{v}$.</li>
<li>`np.linalg.eig` gives eigenvalues and eigenvectors as columns; sort and check them.</li>
<li>Repeated feedback converges on the top eigenvector; its eigenvalue sets growth or decay.</li>
</ul>
