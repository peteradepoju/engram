<Step n={1} title="The characteristic equation" minutes={25} />

<Derivation title="Where eigenvalues come from">
  <div>

Rewrite $A\mathbf{v} = \lambda\mathbf{v}$ as $A\mathbf{v} - \lambda I\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$, that is

$$
(A - \lambda I)\,\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}
$$

  </div>
  <div>

We want a **nonzero** $\mathbf{v}$ that $A - \lambda I$ sends to zero. An invertible matrix only sends $\mathbf{0}$ to $\mathbf{0}$, so $A - \lambda I$ must be singular (Week 5).

  </div>
  <div>

A matrix is singular exactly when its determinant is zero. So the eigenvalues are the solutions of the **characteristic equation**

$$
\det(A - \lambda I) = 0
$$

  </div>
  <div>

For an $n \times n$ matrix, $\det(A - \lambda I)$ is a polynomial of degree $n$ in $\lambda$, the **characteristic polynomial**. It has exactly $n$ roots, counted with multiplicity, some possibly complex. Once you know an eigenvalue, the eigenvectors are the nonzero solutions of $(A - \lambda I)\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$.

  </div>
</Derivation>

For $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$: $\det\begin{pmatrix} 2 - \lambda & 1 \\ 1 & 2 - \lambda \end{pmatrix} = (2 - \lambda)^2 - 1 = \lambda^2 - 4\lambda + 3 = (\lambda - 3)(\lambda - 1)$. The eigenvalues are 3 and 1, as you found yesterday. For $\lambda = 3$, $(A - 3I)\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$ reads $-v_1 + v_2 = 0$, so $\mathbf{v} = (1, 1)$.

<Step n={2} title="Trace and determinant" minutes={25} />

For a general 2 by 2 matrix $A = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}$,

$$
\det(A - \lambda I) = (a - \lambda)(d - \lambda) - bc = \lambda^2 - (a + d)\lambda + (ad - bc)
$$

The middle coefficient is the **trace** $\tau = a + d$ (the sum of the diagonal) and the last is the **determinant** $\Delta = ad - bc$. The quadratic formula gives

$$
\lambda_{1,2} = \frac{\tau}{2} \pm \sqrt{\frac{\tau^2}{4} - \Delta}
$$

  <CharPoly />
</Figure>

<Prove title="Trace is the sum, determinant the product" task="Show that for any 2 by 2 matrix, $\lambda_1 + \lambda_2 = \tau$ and $\lambda_1\lambda_2 = \Delta$.">

A monic quadratic with roots $\lambda_1$ and $\lambda_2$ factorises as $(\lambda - \lambda_1)(\lambda - \lambda_2) = \lambda^2 - (\lambda_1 + \lambda_2)\lambda + \lambda_1\lambda_2$.

Compare coefficients with $\lambda^2 - \tau\lambda + \Delta$: the sum of the roots is $\tau$ and the product is $\Delta$. The same holds for $n \times n$ matrices: the trace is the sum of all $n$ eigenvalues and the determinant is their product. So a matrix is singular exactly when one of its eigenvalues is zero, linking today to Week 5.

</Prove>

  def eig2(A):
      tr = np.trace(A)
      det = A[0, 0] * A[1, 1] - A[0, 1] * A[1, 0]
      root = np.lib.scimath.sqrt(tr ** 2 / 4 - det)
      return tr / 2 + root, tr / 2 - root

<Step n={3} title="Complex eigenvalues" minutes={20} />

When $\tau^2/4 \lt \Delta$ the square root is of a negative number and the eigenvalues are a **complex conjugate pair**,

$$
\lambda = \alpha \pm i\omega, \qquad \alpha = \frac{\tau}{2}, \quad \omega = \sqrt{\Delta - \frac{\tau^2}{4}}
$$

There is then no real direction that the matrix maps onto its own line: every vector is turned. The real part $\alpha$ says whether things grow or shrink and the imaginary part $\omega$ says how fast they rotate. In a discrete map, $|\lambda| = \sqrt{\alpha^2 + \omega^2}$ is the stretch per step and the angle of $\lambda$ is the rotation per step. Rotations are the clearest case.

<Step n={4} title="An excitatory-inhibitory circuit" minutes={35} />

Wilson and Cowan (1972), today's paper, described a local circuit with two numbers: the activity $E$ of an excitatory pool and $I$ of an inhibitory pool. Near a steady state, small deviations $\mathbf{x} = (\delta E, \delta I)$ obey a linear system $\frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt} = J\mathbf{x}$, with

$$
J = \begin{pmatrix} w_{EE} - 1 & -w_{EI} \\ w_{IE} & -1 - w_{II} \end{pmatrix}
$$

in units where each pool's time constant is 1. The $-1$ terms are each pool's leak, $w_{EE}$ is recurrent excitation, $w_{EI}$ is inhibition onto the excitatory pool and $w_{IE}$ is excitation onto the inhibitory pool. On Day 4 you will show that for $\frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt} = J\mathbf{x}$ a disturbance dies away when **both eigenvalues have negative real part**. For 2 by 2 matrices that is the same as $\tau \lt 0$ and $\Delta \gt 0$, and complex eigenvalues mean the decay or growth comes with **oscillation**.

A disturbance dies away while oscillating, as Wilson and Cowan found. The prediction's circuit (trace −0.4, determinant 2) behaves the same way. Now sweep recurrent excitation and watch the behaviour change.

As $w_{EE}$ grows the eigenvalues march to the right across the imaginary axis. At the crossing ($\tau = 0$, here $w_{EE} = 2.5$) the steady state loses stability and oscillations grow: in the full nonlinear model they settle into a sustained rhythm. This kind of crossing, a Hopf bifurcation, is one account of how cortical circuits generate gamma oscillations (Week 47).
