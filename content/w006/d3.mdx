<Step n={1} title="Change of basis" minutes={25} />

A vector's coordinates depend on the basis you describe it in. If the columns of $V$ are a basis, the coordinates $\mathbf{c}$ of $\mathbf{x}$ in that basis satisfy $\mathbf{x} = V\mathbf{c}$ (Week 4), so

$$
\mathbf{c} = V^{-1}\mathbf{x}, \qquad \mathbf{x} = V\mathbf{c}
$$

$V^{-1}$ translates from standard coordinates into the new ones, and $V$ translates back.

<Step n={2} title="Diagonalisation" minutes={25} />

Suppose an $n \times n$ matrix $A$ has $n$ linearly independent eigenvectors. Put them in the columns of $V$ and the eigenvalues on the diagonal of $\Lambda$. Yesterday's equation $AV = V\Lambda$ can be multiplied on the right by $V^{-1}$:

$$
A = V\Lambda V^{-1}
$$

Read it right to left as three moves: $V^{-1}$ converts $\mathbf{x}$ into eigen-coordinates, $\Lambda$ stretches each coordinate by its eigenvalue, and $V$ converts back. **In the eigenvector basis, $A$ is just a diagonal matrix.**

<Derivation title="Powers come for free">
  <div>

Square it: $A^2 = V\Lambda V^{-1}V\Lambda V^{-1} = V\Lambda^2V^{-1}$, because $V^{-1}V = I$ in the middle.

  </div>
  <div>

The same cancellation happens $k - 1$ times in $A^k$, so

$$
A^k = V\Lambda^kV^{-1}
$$

  </div>
  <div>

$\Lambda^k$ is diagonal with entries $\lambda_i^k$, which costs nothing to compute. A hundred steps of a recurrent circuit need one eigen-decomposition and $n$ powers of numbers.

  </div>
</Derivation>

Not every matrix can be diagonalised. The shear $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ has the eigenvalue 1 twice but only one eigen-direction, so there are not enough eigenvectors to fill $V$. Such defective matrices are rare in practice (a tiny random perturbation fixes them) but they mark the boundary where today's tools stop working. Every **symmetric** matrix can be diagonalised, with perpendicular eigenvectors (Day 5).

<Step n={3} title="Modes and their lifetimes" minutes={30} />

For the circuit $\mathbf{x}_{t+1} = A\mathbf{x}_t$, diagonalisation splits activity into **modes**. Write $\mathbf{c}_0 = V^{-1}\mathbf{x}_0$. Then

$$
\mathbf{x}_t = A^t\mathbf{x}_0 = V\Lambda^t\mathbf{c}_0 = \sum_i c_{0,i}\,\lambda_i^t\,\mathbf{v}_i
$$

Each mode evolves on its own, multiplied by $\lambda_i$ every step. A mode with $0 \lt \lambda \lt 1$ halves after $t_{1/2}$ steps, where $\lambda^{t_{1/2}} = \frac{1}{2}$:

$$
t_{1/2} = \frac{\ln(1/2)}{\ln\lambda}
$$

An eigenvalue of 0.99 gives a half-life of about 69 steps; 0.5 gives one step. Eigenvalues close to 1 make memories.

<Step n={4} title="When eigenvalues mislead" minutes={25} />

The mode picture suggests that if every $|\lambda| \lt 1$, activity simply shrinks. That is true eventually, but not necessarily at first. Consider a circuit where population 1 is driven strongly by population 2 but not the other way round:

$$
B = \begin{pmatrix} 0.9 & 2 \\ 0 & 0.8 \end{pmatrix}
$$

It is triangular, so its eigenvalues are 0.9 and 0.8. Yet its eigenvectors, $(1, 0)$ and about $(1, -0.05)$, are almost parallel. To express a vector like $(0, 1)$ in such a basis you need two huge coefficients of opposite sign that nearly cancel. As the modes decay at different rates the cancellation fails and the activity swells before it fades.

The kick of size 1 grows more than fivefold before decaying, even though both eigenvalues are below 1. That answers the prediction. A matrix whose eigenvectors are not perpendicular is called **non-normal** (formally, $A^\top A \neq AA^\top$). Murphy and Miller (2009), today's paper, showed that circuits with strong excitation balanced by strong inhibition are strongly non-normal: a small difference between excitatory and inhibitory activity is fed forward into a large, shared response. They called this **balanced amplification** and argued that it shapes activity in visual cortex.

Eigenvalues tell you what happens in the long run. For the short run with non-normal matrices, look at the geometry of the eigenvectors, or at the singular values.

</Bridge>

<Prove title="A defective matrix" task="Show that $N = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ has only one independent eigenvector, and compute $N^k$ directly. Does the size of $N^k\mathbf{x}$ stay bounded for every $\mathbf{x}$, as the eigenvalue 1 might suggest?">

The characteristic polynomial is $(1 - \lambda)^2$, so $\lambda = 1$ twice. $(N - I)\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$ reads $v_2 = 0$, so every eigenvector is a multiple of $(1, 0)$: only one direction, too few to diagonalise.

Multiply out: $N^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, and by induction $N^k = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & k \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$. Then $N^k(0, 1) = (k, 1)$, which grows without bound, linearly in $k$. So an eigenvalue of size 1 does not guarantee bounded activity when the matrix is defective: the missing eigenvector shows up as growth like $k\lambda^k$.

</Prove>

<Key title="Before you go">
<ul>
<li>In its eigenvector basis a diagonalisable matrix is just a set of stretches: $A = V\Lambda V^{-1}$.</li>
<li>Powers are cheap: $A^k = V\Lambda^kV^{-1}$, and each mode lasts according to its eigenvalue.</li>
<li>Eigenvalues near 1 give long-lived modes, the basis of memory in linear circuits.</li>
<li>Non-normal circuits can amplify transiently despite stable eigenvalues.</li>
</ul>
