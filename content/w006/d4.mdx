<Step n={1} title="Continuous-time dynamics" minutes={25} />

The standard rate model of a recurrent network of $n$ units is

$$
\tau\frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt} = -\mathbf{x} + W\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{u}(t)
$$

Each unit's activity $x_i$ leaks back to zero with time constant $\tau$ (the $-\mathbf{x}$ term), is pushed by the others through the weights $W$, and receives external input $\mathbf{u}$. Without input it is $\tau\dot{\mathbf{x}} = (W - I)\mathbf{x}$, a linear system.

<Derivation title="Solving it with eigenvectors">
  <div>

Let $W\mathbf{v} = \lambda\mathbf{v}$. Then $(W - I)\mathbf{v} = (\lambda - 1)\mathbf{v}$: subtracting $I$ shifts every eigenvalue by 1 and keeps the eigenvectors (Day 1).

  </div>
  <div>

Try activity that stays along $\mathbf{v}$: $\mathbf{x}(t) = c(t)\mathbf{v}$. Substituting gives $\tau\dot{c}\,\mathbf{v} = (\lambda - 1)c\,\mathbf{v}$, so $\tau\dot{c} = (\lambda - 1)c$, whose solution is

$$
c(t) = c(0)\,e^{(\lambda - 1)t/\tau}
$$

  </div>
  <div>

If $W$ is diagonalisable, any start is a combination of eigenvectors, and each piece evolves on its own:

$$
\mathbf{x}(t) = \sum_i c_i(0)\,e^{(\lambda_i - 1)t/\tau}\,\mathbf{v}_i
$$

  </div>
  <div>

A mode with real $\lambda \lt 1$ decays with an **effective time constant**

$$
\tau_{\text{eff}} = \frac{\tau}{1 - \lambda}
$$

As $\lambda$ approaches 1, recurrent excitation cancels the leak and the mode lasts longer and longer. At $\lambda = 1$ it does not decay at all; above 1 it grows.

  </div>
</Derivation>

So the network is stable when every eigenvalue of $W$ has real part less than 1, which is the same as every eigenvalue of $W - I$ having negative real part. For a complex pair $\lambda = \alpha \pm i\omega$, the factor $e^{(\alpha - 1)t/\tau}e^{\pm i\omega t/\tau}$ combines into a decaying (or growing) oscillation with angular frequency $\omega/\tau$, that is $\frac{\omega}{2\pi\tau}$ cycles per second.

<Step n={3} title="Simulating a network" minutes={25} />

The simplest way to simulate is **Euler's method**: step forward by a small $\Delta t$ using the current rate of change, $\mathbf{x}(t + \Delta t) \approx \mathbf{x}(t) + \frac{\Delta t}{\tau}\left(-\mathbf{x} + W\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{u}\right)$. It is accurate when $\Delta t$ is much smaller than every time constant in the system (Week 25 covers better methods).

<Step n={4} title="The oculomotor integrator" minutes={35} />

Each saccade is driven by a brief burst of velocity commands. Neurons in the brainstem (in the nucleus prepositus hypoglossi and the medial vestibular nucleus of the goldfish and the primate) integrate those bursts: their firing jumps with each saccade and then stays put, coding eye position. Seung (1996), this week's deep read, proposed that they hold the position through recurrent excitation tuned so that one eigenvalue of $W$ equals 1. Activity along that eigenvector neither decays nor grows, and the set of all such states is a **line attractor**.

Take two mutually exciting neurons with $W = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ b & a \end{pmatrix}$. Its eigenvectors are $(1, 1)$, both neurons together, with eigenvalue $a + b$, and $(1, -1)$, the difference, with eigenvalue $a - b$. Eye position is read from the sum mode.

To stretch 100 ms into 20 s the eigenvalue must sit at 0.995, within half a percent of 1. That answers the prediction, and it is the central puzzle of Seung's paper: biological synapses are noisy and change over time, so how does the brain keep this eigenvalue so finely tuned? A 2% error cuts the time constant from 20 s to 4 s and the eyes drift back towards the centre, which is just what is seen when the integrator is damaged. Seung suggested that visual feedback about the eyes slipping could be used to keep adjusting the weights.

<Prove title="Integration along the line attractor" task="With $W\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v}$ ($\lambda = 1$, $\lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert = 1$) and input $\mathbf{u}(t) = s(t)\mathbf{v}$, show that the activity along $\mathbf{v}$, $c(t) = \mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{x}(t)$, satisfies $\tau\dot{c} = s(t)$ when $W$ is symmetric, so the network integrates its input. What happens with $\lambda$ slightly below 1?">

For symmetric $W$, $\mathbf{v}^\top W = (W\mathbf{v})^\top = \mathbf{v}^\top$. Take the dot product of the dynamics with $\mathbf{v}$:

$\tau\dot{c} = -\mathbf{v}^\top\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{v}^\top W\mathbf{x} + s(t)\,\mathbf{v}^\top\mathbf{v} = -c + c + s(t) = s(t)$.

So $c(t) = c(0) + \frac{1}{\tau}\int_0^t s(t')\,dt'$: a perfect integrator. Velocity commands go in, position comes out.

With $\lambda = 1 - \epsilon$, the same steps give $\tau\dot{c} = -\epsilon c + s(t)$, a leaky integrator with time constant $\tau/\epsilon$. Between inputs, position decays exponentially towards zero; with $\epsilon$ negative it grows away instead.

</Prove>
