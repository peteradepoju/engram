<Step n={1} title="The spectral theorem" minutes={25} />

A matrix is **symmetric** if $A^\top = A$, that is $A_{ij} = A_{ji}$. The **spectral theorem** says that every real symmetric matrix can be written as

$$
A = Q\Lambda Q^\top
$$

where $\Lambda$ is diagonal with real eigenvalues and $Q$ is **orthogonal**: its columns are perpendicular unit eigenvectors, so $Q^\top Q = I$ and $Q^{-1} = Q^\top$. Compared with Day 3's $V\Lambda V^{-1}$, the change of basis is now a rotation (possibly with a reflection), so it preserves lengths and angles. No transient amplification, no defective cases.

<Prove title="Eigenvectors of a symmetric matrix are orthogonal" task="Let $A = A^\top$, with $A\mathbf{v}_1 = \lambda_1\mathbf{v}_1$, $A\mathbf{v}_2 = \lambda_2\mathbf{v}_2$ and $\lambda_1 \neq \lambda_2$. Show that $\mathbf{v}_1 \cdot \mathbf{v}_2 = 0$.">

Compute $\mathbf{v}_1^\top A\mathbf{v}_2$ in two ways.

Using $A\mathbf{v}_2 = \lambda_2\mathbf{v}_2$: $\mathbf{v}_1^\top A\mathbf{v}_2 = \lambda_2\,\mathbf{v}_1^\top\mathbf{v}_2$.

Using symmetry, $\mathbf{v}_1^\top A = (A^\top\mathbf{v}_1)^\top = (A\mathbf{v}_1)^\top = \lambda_1\mathbf{v}_1^\top$, so $\mathbf{v}_1^\top A\mathbf{v}_2 = \lambda_1\,\mathbf{v}_1^\top\mathbf{v}_2$.

Subtract: $(\lambda_1 - \lambda_2)\,\mathbf{v}_1^\top\mathbf{v}_2 = 0$. Since $\lambda_1 \neq \lambda_2$, $\mathbf{v}_1 \cdot \mathbf{v}_2 = 0$. (When eigenvalues repeat, the eigenvectors can still be chosen orthogonal by Gram-Schmidt within their eigenspace, Week 4. A similar argument with complex numbers shows the eigenvalues are real.)

</Prove>

For symmetric matrices use `np.linalg.eigh` rather than `eig`: it is faster, guarantees real eigenvalues and orthonormal eigenvectors, and returns the eigenvalues in **ascending** order.

Writing $A = Q\Lambda Q^\top$ as a sum of columns gives a picture you will use all next week:

$$
A = \sum_i \lambda_i\,\mathbf{q}_i\mathbf{q}_i^\top
$$

Each term $\mathbf{q}_i\mathbf{q}_i^\top$ is the projection onto one eigenvector (Week 4). A symmetric matrix is a weighted sum of perpendicular projections.

<Step n={2} title="Covariance matrices" minutes={30} />

Record $n$ neurons over $T$ trials of the same stimulus and stack the responses in a $T \times n$ matrix $X$. Subtract each neuron's mean to get $X_c$. The **covariance matrix**

$$
\Sigma = \frac{1}{T - 1}X_c^\top X_c
$$

is $n \times n$, with each neuron's variance on the diagonal and the covariance of neurons $i$ and $j$ in entry $(i, j)$. It is symmetric because $\Sigma_{ij}$ and $\Sigma_{ji}$ are the same sum. Shared fluctuations between neurons on repeated trials are called **noise correlations**, and they matter for how much information a population carries (Week 119).

<Step n={3} title="Quadratic forms and the direction of greatest variance" minutes={25} />

Project the noise onto a unit vector $\mathbf{u}$: each trial gives the number $\mathbf{u}^\top\mathbf{x}_c$. The variance of these projections is

$$
\operatorname{Var}(\mathbf{u}^\top\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{T - 1}\sum_t(\mathbf{u}^\top\mathbf{x}_{c,t})^2 = \mathbf{u}^\top\Sigma\,\mathbf{u}
$$

An expression $\mathbf{u}^\top A\mathbf{u}$ is a **quadratic form**. Because a variance cannot be negative, $\mathbf{u}^\top\Sigma\mathbf{u} \geq 0$ for every $\mathbf{u}$: covariance matrices are **positive semidefinite**, and so all their eigenvalues are at least zero.

The variance along a direction swings between the two eigenvalues, reaching the top one exactly along the top eigenvector. This is a general fact (proved in the Forge): for symmetric $A$, the **Rayleigh quotient** $\mathbf{u}^\top A\mathbf{u}$ over unit vectors is largest at the top eigenvector and smallest at the bottom one. Finding the directions of greatest variance in data is principal component analysis, next week's main topic.

<Step n={4} title="Energy in symmetric networks" minutes={25} />

Symmetry also changes what a network can do. For the linear network $\tau\dot{\mathbf{x}} = -\mathbf{x} + W\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}$ with a constant input $\mathbf{b}$, define

$$
E(\mathbf{x}) = \tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{x}^\top(I - W)\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{b}^\top\mathbf{x}
$$

When $W$ is symmetric, the rate of change is exactly downhill on this landscape: $\tau\dot{\mathbf{x}} = -\nabla E$ (gradients are Week 8; you can check this one entry by entry). So $E$ can only fall, and activity settles into a minimum. If every eigenvalue of $W$ is below 1, $I - W$ is positive definite and there is a single bowl-shaped minimum. Hopfield (1982), today's paper, used the same idea for binary neurons: with symmetric weights there is an energy, and memories are its valleys.

With symmetric weights the energy falls steadily. Without symmetry the same formula rises and falls, because the antisymmetric part of $W$ pushes activity round in circles, which no landscape can describe. Real cortex is not symmetric (excitatory neurons only excite), so energy pictures are an idealisation; they remain the clearest way to think about attractor memories (Week 108).
