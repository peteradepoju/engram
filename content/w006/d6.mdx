Weak excitation gives a quiet circuit. Adding inhibition onto the excitatory pool turns the decay into a damped oscillation, since the loop E to I to E acts like a spring. Strong recurrent excitation pushes the trace above zero and the circuit runs away, unless inhibition is strong enough to hold it.

  rng = np.random.default_rng(1996)
  N = 40
  m = rng.uniform(0.5, 1.5, N)
  R = rng.normal(size=(N, N))
  W0 = 0.9 * np.outer(m, m) / (m @ m) + 0.15 * (R + R.T) / np.sqrt(2 * N)

      """Euler simulation; returns the readout (dot product with a vector) at each step."""
      x = x0.copy(); out = [readout @ x]

The eigenvalues of a large random matrix with independent entries of variance $g^2/N$ fill a disc of radius $g$ almost uniformly: the **circular law**. So the linear network loses stability exactly when the gain passes 1 and the disc crosses the red line. With a nonlinearity added, the same transition at $g = 1$ separates quiet networks from chaotic ones (Sompolinsky, Crisanti and Sommers, 1988, Week 107), and the edge of chaos is where reservoir computers work best. Rajan and Abbott (2006) worked out how the disc changes when excitatory and inhibitory neurons have different weight statistics.

<Prove title="1. The Rayleigh quotient" task="For symmetric $A$ with eigenvalues $\lambda_1 \geq \dots \geq \lambda_n$, show that $\lambda_n \leq \mathbf{u}^\top A\mathbf{u} \leq \lambda_1$ for every unit vector $\mathbf{u}$, with equality at the top and bottom eigenvectors.">

Write $\mathbf{u}$ in the orthonormal eigenvector basis: $\mathbf{u} = \sum_i c_i\mathbf{q}_i$ with $\sum_i c_i^2 = \lVert\mathbf{u}\rVert^2 = 1$.

Then $A\mathbf{u} = \sum_i c_i\lambda_i\mathbf{q}_i$ and, because the $\mathbf{q}_i$ are orthonormal, $\mathbf{u}^\top A\mathbf{u} = \sum_i \lambda_i c_i^2$.

This is a weighted average of the eigenvalues with weights $c_i^2$ that sum to 1, so it lies between the smallest and largest eigenvalue. It equals $\lambda_1$ when all the weight is on $\mathbf{q}_1$ and $\lambda_n$ when it is all on $\mathbf{q}_n$. Next week this becomes the main argument for PCA.

</Prove>

<Prove title="2. Orthogonal matrices preserve memories" task="Show that every eigenvalue of an orthogonal matrix $Q$ ($Q^\top Q = I$) has $|\lambda| = 1$. Why do some recurrent neural networks initialise their recurrent weights as orthogonal matrices?">

Let $Q\mathbf{v} = \lambda\mathbf{v}$ (allowing complex $\mathbf{v}$ and $\lambda$). Orthogonal matrices preserve length: $\lVert Q\mathbf{v}\rVert^2 = \mathbf{v}^* Q^\top Q\mathbf{v} = \lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert^2$, where $^*$ is the conjugate transpose. But $\lVert Q\mathbf{v}\rVert = |\lambda|\,\lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert$, so $|\lambda| = 1$.

In a discrete-time network $\mathbf{x}_{t+1} = Q\mathbf{x}_t$, every mode neither grows nor shrinks, so information is carried forward without loss for many steps, and gradients flowing backwards through time do not explode or vanish. That is why orthogonal and identity initialisations help recurrent networks learn long dependencies (Week 86).

</Prove>
