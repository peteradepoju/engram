<Step n={1} title="The singular value decomposition" minutes={25} />

Every real $m \times n$ matrix $A$ can be written as

$$
A = U\Sigma V^\top
$$

- $V$ is $n \times n$ and orthogonal. Its columns $\mathbf{v}_i$ are the **right singular vectors**: perpendicular directions in the input space.
- $\Sigma$ is $m \times n$ and zero except for the **singular values** $\sigma_1 \geq \sigma_2 \geq \dots \geq 0$ on its diagonal.
- $U$ is $m \times m$ and orthogonal. Its columns $\mathbf{u}_i$ are the **left singular vectors**: perpendicular directions in the output space.

Multiply both sides by $V$ on the right and read column by column:

$$
A\mathbf{v}_i = \sigma_i\mathbf{u}_i
$$

The map sends the $i$th input direction to the $i$th output direction, scaled by $\sigma_i$. Unlike eigenvectors, the input and output directions are different (they live in different spaces when $A$ is not square), and they are always perpendicular sets. Equivalently, $A$ is a sum of rank-one pieces,

$$
A = \sum_{i} \sigma_i\,\mathbf{u}_i\mathbf{v}_i^\top
$$

each one reading the input along $\mathbf{v}_i$, scaling it by $\sigma_i$ and writing it out along $\mathbf{u}_i$. The number of nonzero singular values is the **rank** of $A$.

Any 2 by 2 matrix turns the unit circle into an ellipse. The singular values are the lengths of the ellipse's half-axes, the left singular vectors are their directions, and the right singular vectors are the directions on the circle that land on those axes. Their product $\sigma_1\sigma_2$ is the area scaling $|\det A|$ from Week 5.

<Step n={3} title="Computing and checking" minutes={25} />

`np.linalg.svd(A, full_matrices=False)` returns `U`, the singular values `s` as a 1D array in descending order, and `Vt`, which is $V^\top$ (its **rows** are the right singular vectors). With `full_matrices=False` you get the economy form: for a $5 \times 3$ matrix, `U` is $5 \times 3$ rather than $5 \times 5$, dropping columns that would only multiply zeros.

<Prove title="Singular values from eigenvalues" task="Using $A = U\Sigma V^\top$, show that $A^\top A = V(\Sigma^\top\Sigma)V^\top$ and $AA^\top = U(\Sigma\Sigma^\top)U^\top$. What are the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of $A^\top A$?">

$A^\top A = (U\Sigma V^\top)^\top U\Sigma V^\top = V\Sigma^\top U^\top U\Sigma V^\top = V(\Sigma^\top\Sigma)V^\top$, since $U^\top U = I$. The same steps give $AA^\top = U(\Sigma\Sigma^\top)U^\top$.

$\Sigma^\top\Sigma$ is diagonal with entries $\sigma_i^2$, so this is the spectral decomposition of the symmetric matrix $A^\top A$ (Week 6, Day 5): its eigenvectors are the right singular vectors $\mathbf{v}_i$ and its eigenvalues are $\sigma_i^2$. Likewise the eigenvectors of $AA^\top$ are the $\mathbf{u}_i$. This is also a proof that the SVD exists: diagonalise $A^\top A$, take $\sigma_i = \sqrt{\lambda_i}$ and set $\mathbf{u}_i = A\mathbf{v}_i/\sigma_i$.

</Prove>

Use `np.linalg.svd` in practice rather than this route: forming $A^\top A$ squares the condition number (Week 5), so small singular values lose accuracy.

<Step n={4} title="What a connection matrix transmits" minutes={30} />

Two singular values stand far above the rest, and on average 98% of B's response to a random input is carried by the two modes. Every weight is nonzero, but only a two-dimensional slice of A's activity really gets through. That answers the prediction. Activity in A along any of the other 28 directions is nearly invisible to B.

<Key title="Before you go">
<ul>
<li>Every matrix is a rotation, a stretch along perpendicular axes, and a rotation: $A = U\Sigma V^\top$.</li>
<li>$A\mathbf{v}_i = \sigma_i\mathbf{u}_i$: each input direction maps to an output direction with gain $\sigma_i$.</li>
<li>Singular values are the square roots of the eigenvalues of $A^\top A$.</li>
<li>A few large singular values mean a map transmits only a low-dimensional slice of its input.</li>
</ul>
</Key>
