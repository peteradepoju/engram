<Step n={1} title="Rank-k approximation" minutes={25} />

Yesterday's sum of rank-one pieces, cut short after $k$ terms, gives

$$
A_k = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \sigma_i\,\mathbf{u}_i\mathbf{v}_i^\top = U_k\Sigma_kV_k^\top
$$

where $U_k$ and $V_k$ keep the first $k$ columns and $\Sigma_k$ the first $k$ singular values. $A_k$ has rank $k$. Storing it takes $k(m + n + 1)$ numbers instead of $mn$: for a 1000 by 1000 matrix with $k = 10$, about 2% of the original.

**The Eckart-Young theorem.** Among all matrices of rank at most $k$, $A_k$ is the closest to $A$ in the sense of the sum of squared differences of entries. No other rank-$k$ matrix does better. This is today's paper.

<Step n={2} title="The error formula" minutes={20} />

The size of a matrix as a whole is measured by the **Frobenius norm**, the square root of the sum of squared entries: $\lVert A\rVert_F = \sqrt{\sum_{ij}A_{ij}^2}$.

<Derivation title="The error of truncating the SVD">
  <div>

The Frobenius norm does not change under orthogonal matrices: $\lVert QA\rVert_F = \lVert A\rVert_F$ and $\lVert AQ\rVert_F = \lVert A\rVert_F$, because multiplying by $Q$ rotates each column (or row) without changing its length.

  </div>
  <div>

So $\lVert A\rVert_F = \lVert U\Sigma V^\top\rVert_F = \lVert\Sigma\rVert_F$, giving

$$
\lVert A\rVert_F^2 = \sum_i \sigma_i^2
$$

The squared singular values share out the total "energy" of the matrix.

  </div>
  <div>

The leftover after truncating is $A - A_k = \sum_{i \gt k}\sigma_i\mathbf{u}_i\mathbf{v}_i^\top$, itself an SVD with singular values $\sigma_{k+1}, \sigma_{k+2}, \dots$. Hence

$$
\lVert A - A_k\rVert_F = \sqrt{\sum_{i \gt k}\sigma_i^2}
$$

The fraction of the matrix's energy kept is $\sum_{i \leq k}\sigma_i^2 / \sum_i\sigma_i^2$.

  </div>
</Derivation>

<Step n={3} title="Denoising population activity" minutes={35} />

Here is a simulated recording: 40 neurons over 60 time bins. Each neuron's activity is a weighted mix of three shared time courses (a slow rise and fall, an oscillation and a late bump) plus independent noise. Because the true activity is exactly rank 3, its information sits in three singular components; the noise spreads thinly over all 40.

Keeping three components gives an error well under half that of the raw recording. That answers the prediction: throwing information away made the data *more* accurate, because what was thrown away was mostly noise. Keep more components and the error climbs again as noise is let back in. In real data you do not know `truth`, so how to pick $k$ honestly is the subject of Day 5.

<Step n={4} title="Separable receptive fields" minutes={25} />

A visual neuron's **spatiotemporal receptive field** is a matrix: rows are positions along a line in the visual field and columns are time lags before a spike (the spike-triggered average of Week 5, extended in time). If it is **separable**, it is the product of a spatial profile and a temporal profile, $\text{RF}(x, t) = f(x)\,g(t)$, which is exactly a rank-one matrix $\mathbf{f}\mathbf{g}^\top$. Many neurons in the lateral geniculate nucleus and simple cells in V1 are close to separable. **Direction-selective** cells are not: their receptive fields are tilted in space-time, which is what makes them prefer motion one way. The SVD measures separability with one number, the fraction of energy in the first component, $\sigma_1^2 / \sum_i\sigma_i^2$.

The tilted field needs at least two components: by a trigonometric identity, $\cos(a - b) = \cos a\cos b + \sin a\sin b$, a space-time tilt is a sum of two separable pieces. Adelson and Bergen (1985) built their motion-energy model of direction selectivity out of exactly such pairs, and they reappear in Week 38.

<Prove title="Energy kept by one component" task="Show that the first singular value satisfies $\sigma_1 = \max_{\lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert = 1}\lVert A\mathbf{v}\rVert$. Then argue that $\sigma_1^2 / \sum_i\sigma_i^2 = 1$ exactly when A has rank one.">

$\lVert A\mathbf{v}\rVert^2 = \mathbf{v}^\top A^\top A\mathbf{v}$ is a Rayleigh quotient of the symmetric matrix $A^\top A$, whose largest eigenvalue is $\sigma_1^2$ (Day 1). By last week's Forge it is at most $\sigma_1^2$, with equality at $\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v}_1$. So $\sigma_1$ is the largest stretch $A$ applies to any unit vector, also called the **spectral norm** of A.

$\sigma_1^2 / \sum_i\sigma_i^2 = 1$ means $\sigma_i = 0$ for every $i \gt 1$, so $A = \sigma_1\mathbf{u}_1\mathbf{v}_1^\top$, a rank-one matrix. Conversely a rank-one matrix has exactly one nonzero singular value.

</Prove>

<li>Keeping the top k singular components gives the best rank-k approximation.</li>

<li>For low-rank signals plus noise, truncation denoises; too many components let noise back.</li>
