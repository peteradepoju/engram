<Step n={1} title="The idea" minutes={20} />

You have $T$ observations of $p$ variables in a $T \times p$ matrix $X$: trials by neurons, or spikes by voltage samples. PCA looks for a new set of perpendicular axes, ordered so that the first captures as much of the data's variance as possible, the second as much of what remains, and so on. If the first few axes capture most of the variance, the data are well described in fewer dimensions.

<Figure caption="Turn the axis by dragging its handle. Variance along the axis plus mean squared residual is always the same, so the axis of greatest variance is also the one closest to the data." credit="Interactive figure by Engram.">
  <PcaProjection />
</Figure>

The figure shows the key fact. For a unit vector $\mathbf{u}$ and a centred data point $\mathbf{x}$, Pythagoras (Week 4) splits $\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert^2$ into the squared projection $(\mathbf{u}^\top\mathbf{x})^2$ and the squared residual. Averaged over the data, the total is fixed, so **maximising the variance along u is the same as minimising the mean squared distance from the data to the line**. PCA is both the best summary and the best fit.

<Step n={2} title="Two routes to PCA" minutes={25} />

<Derivation title="PCA through the covariance matrix and through the SVD">
  <div>

Centre the data: subtract each column's mean to get $X_c$. The covariance matrix is $\Sigma = X_c^\top X_c / (T - 1)$ (Week 6, Day 5).

  </div>
  <div>

The variance along unit vector $\mathbf{u}$ is the quadratic form $\mathbf{u}^\top\Sigma\mathbf{u}$. By the Rayleigh quotient (Week 6 Forge) it is largest when $\mathbf{u}$ is the top eigenvector of $\Sigma$, and the variance there is the top eigenvalue. Restricting to directions perpendicular to the first, the best is the second eigenvector, and so on. **The principal components are the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix, and the eigenvalues are the variances along them.**

  </div>
  <div>

Now take the SVD of the centred data, $X_c = U S V^\top$ (we write $S$ for the singular values to avoid clashing with $\Sigma$). Then

$$
\Sigma = \frac{X_c^\top X_c}{T - 1} = V\,\frac{S^2}{T - 1}\,V^\top
$$

  </div>
  <div>

This is the spectral decomposition of $\Sigma$. So **the principal components are the right singular vectors of $X_c$**, the variance along component $i$ is $s_i^2/(T - 1)$, and the projections of the data onto the components (the **scores**) are $X_cV = US$.

  </div>
</Derivation>

In practice the SVD route is preferred: it never forms $X_c^\top X_c$, so it is more accurate (Day 1), and it works well when there are more variables than observations.

  import numpy as np
  def pca(X, k):
      mu = X.mean(axis=0)
      U, s, Vt = np.linalg.svd(X - mu, full_matrices=False)

Two numbers summarise how well $k$ components describe the data. The **variance explained** by component $i$ is its share of the total, $\lambda_i / \sum_j\lambda_j$, and the cumulative variance explained adds these up. A plot of the variances against component number is a **scree plot** (after the rubble at the foot of a cliff: you look for where the cliff ends).

<Step n={4} title="Spike sorting with PCA" minutes={30} />

An extracellular electrode records spikes from several nearby neurons. Each neuron's spikes have a characteristic shape, set by its size, type and distance from the electrode. Assigning each spike to a neuron, **spike sorting**, was one of the first jobs PCA did in neuroscience: the waveforms are 32-dimensional, but the differences between neurons live in a few directions.

The two principal components separate the three neurons about twice as well as the trough alone. That answers the prediction. PC1 looks like an average spike shape and mostly measures size; PC2 picks up differences in width and recovery. Each PC pools information from all 32 samples, which averages away the independent noise. Modern spike sorters such as Kilosort still use low-dimensional projections like these, on probes with hundreds of channels (Week 73).

PCA finds where the variance is. Whether that is where the interesting structure is depends on your data, and is the question to ask every time.

<Prove title="Variance explained adds up" task="Show that the total variance of the data, $\sum_j \operatorname{Var}(x_j)$ summed over the p original variables, equals the sum of the variances of all p principal components.">

The total variance is the sum of the diagonal of $\Sigma$, which is its trace. The trace equals the sum of the eigenvalues (Week 6, Day 2), and the eigenvalues are the variances of the principal components.

So PCA does not create or destroy variance; it only redistributes it, piling as much as possible into the first components. That is why "variance explained" percentages add up to 100%.

</Prove>

<Key title="Before you go">
<ul>
<li>PCA finds perpendicular directions of greatest variance: the eigenvectors of the covariance.</li>
<li>Compute it as the SVD of the centred data; variances are $s_i^2/(T - 1)$.</li>
<li>The best variance-capturing axes are also the best reconstruction axes.</li>
<li>PCA pools many noisy measurements into a few informative ones, as in spike sorting.</li>
</ul>
</Key>
