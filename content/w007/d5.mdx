<Step n={1} title="The spectrum" minutes={20} />

The **eigenspectrum** of a population is the list of covariance eigenvalues from largest to smallest: the variance along PC1, PC2, and so on. It contains everything PCA knows about dimensionality. Plotted on log-log axes, a power law $\lambda_n \propto n^{-\alpha}$ is a straight line with slope $-\alpha$. Large $\alpha$ means a few dimensions dominate; small $\alpha$ means variance is spread over many.

<Step n={2} title="Noise inflates dimensionality" minutes={25} />

Suppose each recorded response is signal plus independent noise, $\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{s} + \boldsymbol\epsilon$, with noise variance $\sigma^2$ in every neuron and no noise correlations. Because signal and noise are independent, the covariances add:

$$
\Sigma_r = \Sigma_s + \sigma^2 I
$$

Adding $\sigma^2 I$ adds $\sigma^2$ to every eigenvalue (Week 6, Day 1). Large eigenvalues hardly change, but the small ones, which should fall towards zero, are lifted onto a floor at $\sigma^2$. With 100 neurons, a floor of 100 small components can hold more total variance than the signal itself, and every dimensionality estimate goes up. Real noise is also correlated and finite samples add their own errors, so the effect in data is messier but just as real.

<Step n={3} title="Cross-validated PCA" minutes={35} />

If you show every stimulus twice, the signal is the same on both presentations but the noise is independent. Stringer and colleagues (2019), today's paper, used this to measure only the reliable, stimulus-driven variance along each direction.

<Derivation title="Why the cross-covariance removes noise">
  <div>

Project both presentations onto a unit vector $\mathbf{u}$: $a = \mathbf{u}^\top(\mathbf{s} + \boldsymbol\epsilon_1)$ and $b = \mathbf{u}^\top(\mathbf{s} + \boldsymbol\epsilon_2)$, all centred.

  </div>
  <div>

Average their product over stimuli. The signal-signal term gives the signal variance along $\mathbf{u}$. Every term involving noise averages to zero, because each noise is independent of the signal and the two noises are independent of each other:

$$
\mathbb{E}[ab] = \mathbb{E}[(\mathbf{u}^\top\mathbf{s})^2] + 0 + 0 + 0 = \mathbf{u}^\top\Sigma_s\mathbf{u}
$$

  </div>
  <div>

Compare $\mathbb{E}[a^2] = \mathbf{u}^\top\Sigma_s\mathbf{u} + \sigma^2$ from one presentation alone: squaring one presentation keeps the noise, multiplying two different presentations cancels it.

  </div>
  <div>

To avoid fitting the directions $\mathbf{u}$ to the same noise you then measure, find them on one half of the stimuli (from the first presentation) and evaluate the cross-covariance on the other half.

  </div>
</Derivation>

<Step n={4} title="Power laws" minutes={25} />

Stringer and colleagues recorded about 10,000 neurons in mouse V1 and found that the cross-validated spectrum for natural images followed a power law with exponent close to 1. They then proved a striking result: for a representation of a $d$-dimensional set of stimuli to be smooth (nearby stimuli giving nearby responses), the exponent must exceed $1 + 2/d$. For natural images $d$ is large, so the threshold is just above 1. Their interpretation: visual cortex is as high-dimensional as it can be without becoming so sensitive to tiny input changes that it falls apart, a property they linked to the vulnerability of deep networks to adversarial examples.

Ordinary PCA would have reported an exponent around 0.5, a much flatter and higher-dimensional code than the truth. Cross-validation recovers a value close to 1. When you read a dimensionality claim in a paper, ask three questions: was noise separated from signal, was the spectrum or the participation ratio or a variance threshold used, and was it trial-averaged? The answers can change "3 dimensions" into "hundreds".

<Prove title="Squared projections keep the noise" task="With $a = x + \epsilon_1$ and $b = x + \epsilon_2$, where x, $\epsilon_1$ and $\epsilon_2$ are independent with mean zero and $\operatorname{Var}(\epsilon_i) = \sigma^2$, compute $\mathbb{E}[a^2]$ and $\mathbb{E}[ab]$. Why is the cross-validated estimate unbiased but noisier?">

$\mathbb{E}[a^2] = \mathbb{E}[x^2] + 2\mathbb{E}[x\epsilon_1] + \mathbb{E}[\epsilon_1^2] = \operatorname{Var}(x) + \sigma^2$, since $\mathbb{E}[x\epsilon_1] = \mathbb{E}[x]\mathbb{E}[\epsilon_1] = 0$.

$\mathbb{E}[ab] = \mathbb{E}[x^2] + \mathbb{E}[x\epsilon_2] + \mathbb{E}[\epsilon_1 x] + \mathbb{E}[\epsilon_1\epsilon_2] = \operatorname{Var}(x)$, since all three cross terms factor into products of zero means.

So the average of $ab$ is an unbiased estimate of the signal variance. But each product $ab$ still contains the random terms $x\epsilon_2$, $\epsilon_1 x$ and $\epsilon_1\epsilon_2$; they average to zero only over many stimuli, so with finite data the estimate scatters, and can even come out negative for components with little signal. That is the scatter you saw in the tail.

</Prove>

<Key title="Before you go">
<ul>
<li>Noise adds a floor to the spectrum and inflates every dimensionality estimate.</li>
<li>Cross-validated PCA multiplies projections of independent repeats, keeping only reliable variance.</li>
<li>Fit power laws on log-log axes; the exponent says how fast variance falls off.</li>
<li>Always ask how a dimensionality estimate handled noise, averaging and the choice of summary.</li>
</ul>
