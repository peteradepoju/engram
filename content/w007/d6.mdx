The 90% threshold asks for many components, because Poisson noise spreads a thin layer of variance over every direction. The scree plot shows a clear break after three. A threshold, a participation ratio and an eyeballed elbow give three different answers, so we need a principled way to choose.

Each extra component kept from half A brings in more of A's own noise, which half B does not share. Up to the true dimensionality the gain in signal outweighs that, and beyond it the noise wins, so the error turns upward. The minimum, at three components, is the same answer you would get from the hidden signals you deleted from the simulation. Cunningham and Yu describe this kind of held-out evaluation as the standard way to choose dimensionality.

Throwing away 77 of 80 dimensions improves decoding. The raw decoder measures distance in all 80 directions, including the 77 that contain only noise, and that noise pushes test trials towards the wrong centroids. Projecting onto the signal subspace keeps the differences between conditions and discards most of the noise: the same lesson as Day 2's denoising and Week 5's ridge regression. (One caution: the components came from trial averages that included the test trials. For a fully honest estimate, compute the PCs from training trials only; try it and see how little changes.)

<Step n={3} title="Derivation set" minutes={10} />

<Prove title="1. Scores and the SVD" task="For centred data $X_c = USV^\top$, show that the scores $X_cV$ equal $US$, that the scores on different components are uncorrelated, and that the variance of the scores on component i is $s_i^2/(T - 1)$.">

$X_cV = USV^\top V = US$, since $V^\top V = I$.

The covariance of the scores is $\frac{1}{T - 1}(US)^\top(US) = \frac{1}{T - 1}SU^\top US = \frac{S^2}{T - 1}$, a diagonal matrix (the scores are centred because $X_c$ is). Off-diagonal zeros mean scores on different components are uncorrelated; the diagonal gives the variances $s_i^2/(T - 1)$. PCA is a rotation of the data that removes all correlations between the new axes.

</Prove>

<Prove title="2. Rotations in the PC plane" task="Suppose the two leading latent signals are $z_1 = e(t)\cos(\omega t + \phi)$ and $z_2 = e(t)\sin(\omega t + \phi)$. Show that the vector $(z_1, z_2)$ evolves as a rotation scaled by $e(t)$, and give the linear dynamical system it would satisfy if $e$ were constant. Which eigenvalues does that system have?">

$(z_1, z_2) = e(t)\,(\cos\psi, \sin\psi)$ with $\psi = \omega t + \phi$: a point at distance $e(t)$ from the origin at angle $\psi$, turning at rate $\omega$. So the trajectory rotates with a radius that grows and shrinks.

With constant $e$, differentiate: $\dot{z}_1 = -\omega z_2$ and $\dot{z}_2 = \omega z_1$, that is $\dot{\mathbf{z}} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -\omega \\ \omega & 0 \end{pmatrix}\mathbf{z}$. This matrix has trace 0 and determinant $\omega^2$, so its eigenvalues are $\pm i\omega$ (Week 6, Day 2): a centre. Churchland and colleagues fitted exactly such antisymmetric dynamics to the top PCs of motor cortex with their jPCA method.

</Prove>
