<Step n={1} title="Rates of change" minutes={25} />

A firing rate is itself a rate of change: spikes per second. A neuron's membrane potential rises at so many millivolts per millisecond. A synaptic weight changes by so much per learning trial. In each case we have a quantity $y$ that depends on another, $x$, and ask how much $y$ changes per unit change of $x$.

Over a gap $h$ the **average rate of change** is the slope of the straight line, the **secant**, joining two points on the curve:

$$
\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} = \frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h}
$$

For a straight line this is the same everywhere. For a curve it depends on where you are and on how wide the gap is.

<Step n={2} title="The derivative as a limit" minutes={25} />

The **derivative** of $f$ at $x$ is what the secant slope approaches as the gap shrinks to nothing:

$$
f'(x) = \frac{df}{dx} = \lim_{h \to 0}\frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h}
$$

It is the slope of the **tangent** line, the best straight-line approximation of the curve near $x$:

$$
f(x + h) \approx f(x) + f'(x)\,h \qquad \text{for small } h
$$

That approximation is the most useful idea in this whole week. It says that, zoomed in far enough, every smooth function looks linear, and its derivative is the slope of that line.

<Derivation title="The derivative of x squared from the definition">
  <div>

Write out the difference quotient for $f(x) = x^2$:

$$
\frac{(x + h)^2 - x^2}{h} = \frac{x^2 + 2xh + h^2 - x^2}{h} = 2x + h
$$

  </div>
  <div>

As $h \to 0$ the leftover $h$ vanishes, so $f'(x) = 2x$. The secant slope was off by exactly $h$: the error shrinks in proportion to the gap.

  </div>
  <div>

The same steps for $x^n$, using the binomial expansion $(x + h)^n = x^n + nx^{n-1}h + \dots$, give the **power rule** $\frac{d}{dx}x^n = nx^{n-1}$. Tomorrow you will collect the other rules.

  </div>
</Derivation>

<Step n={3} title="Numerical derivatives" minutes={25} />

You can always estimate a derivative numerically by choosing a small $h$. The **forward difference** uses $x$ and $x + h$. The **central difference** uses points on both sides:

$$
f'(x) \approx \frac{f(x + h) - f(x - h)}{2h}
$$

The central version is far more accurate: its error shrinks like $h^2$ rather than $h$ (you will see why with Taylor series in Week 9). Numerical derivatives are how you check the analytic gradients you derive, a habit worth starting now.

<Step n={4} title="Slopes, tuning and discrimination" minutes={30} />

A Gaussian orientation tuning curve is

$$
f(\theta) = b + A\exp\left(-\frac{(\theta - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)
$$

with baseline $b$, amplitude $A$, preferred orientation $\mu$ and width $\sigma$. Using the rules you will meet tomorrow (the chain rule and $\frac{d}{dx}e^x = e^x$), its derivative is

$$
f'(\theta) = -A\,\frac{\theta - \mu}{\sigma^2}\exp\left(-\frac{(\theta - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)
$$

It is zero at the peak, $\theta = \mu$, and largest in size on the flanks.

Why does the slope matter? Suppose spike counts in a window of $T$ seconds are Poisson (Week 15), so the variance equals the mean, $Tf(\theta)$. A change of stimulus by $\delta\theta$ shifts the mean count by about $Tf'(\theta)\,\delta\theta$ (the tangent-line approximation). The change is detectable when it is comparable to the noise, a standard deviation of $\sqrt{Tf(\theta)}$. The ratio of the two, squared, per unit $\delta\theta^2$, is the neuron's **Fisher information**:

$$
I(\theta) = \frac{T\,f'(\theta)^2}{f(\theta)}
$$

and the smallest reliably detectable change is roughly $\delta\theta \approx 1/\sqrt{I(\theta)}$. You will meet Fisher information properly in Week 36. Today, notice that the derivative is in the numerator.

The neuron carries no Fisher information at its peak, where small changes in orientation barely change its rate, and the most on its flanks. To tell 60° from 62° you should use this neuron's firing around 60°, where its rate is changing fast. That answers the prediction. Notice, though, that its threshold there is about 7.5°: one neuron counting spikes for half a second cannot reliably tell 60° from 62°. Fisher information from independent neurons adds up, so a population of a hundred similar neurons would bring the threshold under a degree, which is why the brain reads populations (Week 36). Butts and Goldman (2006), today's paper, showed when this slope-based answer holds and when, with more noise or coarser tasks, the peak becomes informative again.

<Prove title="Derivative of 1/x from the definition" task="Using the limit definition, show that the derivative of $f(x) = 1/x$ is $-1/x^2$ for $x \neq 0$.">

The difference quotient is

$$
\frac{1}{h}\left(\frac{1}{x + h} - \frac{1}{x}\right) = \frac{1}{h}\cdot\frac{x - (x + h)}{x(x + h)} = \frac{-1}{x(x + h)}
$$

As $h \to 0$ this tends to $-1/x^2$. So $f'(x) = -1/x^2$, which is also the power rule with $n = -1$: $-1 \cdot x^{-2}$.

</Prove>

<li>Zoomed in, a smooth function is linear: $f(x + h) \approx f(x) + f'(x)h$.</li>

<li>A neuron discriminates best where its tuning curve is steepest, not at its peak.</li>
