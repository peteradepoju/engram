<Step n={1} title="The basic rules" minutes={25} />

Each rule follows from the limit definition (try proving one or two). With $c$ a constant:

| Function | Derivative |
|---|---|
| $c$ | $0$ |
| $c\,f(x)$ | $c\,f'(x)$ |
| $f(x) + g(x)$ | $f'(x) + g'(x)$ |
| $x^n$ | $nx^{n-1}$ |
| $e^x$ | $e^x$ |
| $e^{ax}$ | $ae^{ax}$ |
| $\ln x$ | $1/x$ |
| $\sin x$, $\cos x$ | $\cos x$, $-\sin x$ |

The exponential is the function that equals its own derivative: its rate of growth is proportional to its size. That is why it describes populations, radioactive decay, the charging of a membrane (Week 10) and the exponential nonlinearity of the LNP model (Week 35).

<Step n={2} title="Products and quotients" minutes={20} />

For a product of two functions,

$$
(fg)' = f'g + fg'
$$

Picture a rectangle with sides $f$ and $g$: nudging both sides grows the area by a strip along each side, $f'g$ and $fg'$ (the tiny corner piece vanishes in the limit). For a quotient,

$$
\left(\frac{f}{g}\right)' = \frac{f'g - fg'}{g^2}
$$

<Prove title="The derivative of the sigmoid" task="Using the quotient rule on $\sigma(x) = \dfrac{1}{1 + e^{-x}}$, show that $\sigma'(x) = \sigma(x)\,(1 - \sigma(x))$. What is its largest value?">

Here $f = 1$ and $g = 1 + e^{-x}$, so $f' = 0$ and $g' = -e^{-x}$ (the rule for $e^{ax}$ with $a = -1$). The quotient rule gives

$$
\sigma'(x) = \frac{0 - 1\cdot(-e^{-x})}{(1 + e^{-x})^2} = \frac{e^{-x}}{(1 + e^{-x})^2} = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-x}}\cdot\frac{e^{-x}}{1 + e^{-x}}
$$

The first factor is $\sigma(x)$. The second is $\frac{(1 + e^{-x}) - 1}{1 + e^{-x}} = 1 - \sigma(x)$. So $\sigma' = \sigma(1 - \sigma)$.

The product $s(1 - s)$ of a number between 0 and 1 with its complement is largest when $s = \frac{1}{2}$, giving $\frac{1}{4}$. So the sigmoid's slope never exceeds 0.25, reached at $x = 0$. This neat form also makes it cheap to compute: once you have $\sigma(x)$, the derivative is one multiplication away.

</Prove>

Every model neuron, real or artificial, passes a weighted sum through a nonlinearity. Its derivative matters because, as you will see on Day 5, every learning signal that passes back through a unit is multiplied by it.

- **Sigmoid** and **tanh** saturate: for large positive or negative inputs their output barely changes, so their derivative falls to nearly zero. A saturated unit stops learning.
- The **rectified linear unit** (ReLU), $\max(0, x)$, has derivative 0 below threshold and exactly 1 above. It does not saturate for positive inputs, which Glorot, Bordes and Bengio (2011), today's paper, argued made deep networks easier to train. At $x = 0$ the derivative is undefined; in practice it is set to 0.
- **Softplus**, $\ln(1 + e^x)$, is a smooth ReLU. Its derivative is the sigmoid.
- The **exponential** is used for firing rates in statistical models of neurons because it is always positive and makes the maths of Poisson likelihoods simple.

The sigmoid's slope peaks at 0.25, as you proved. That answers the prediction, and it has a consequence you will see tomorrow: a learning signal passed back through ten sigmoid units is multiplied by at most $0.25^{10}$, less than one in a million. Tanh does better, with a peak slope of 1, and ReLU passes the signal through unchanged wherever units are active.

<Step n={4} title="Contrast response functions" minutes={25} />

Neurons in visual cortex respond to the contrast $c$ of a grating (from 0, blank, to 1, full black and white) following the **Naka-Rushton function**, first used for responses in the fish retina:

$$
r(c) = R_{\max}\frac{c^n}{c^n + c_{50}^n}
$$

$R_{\max}$ is the maximum response, $c_{50}$ the contrast giving half of it, and $n$ (often 2 to 3) sets the steepness. It is a sigmoid on a logarithmic contrast axis, and a building block of divisive normalisation, the operation many cortical circuits appear to share (Week 143).

On a linear contrast axis the slope peaks somewhat below $c_{50}$. (On a logarithmic axis, where psychophysicists usually plot contrast, the peak is exactly at $c_{50}$.) Either way, the neuron is most useful for discriminating contrasts in a band around its half-saturation point and nearly useless near full contrast, where it saturates. Contrast adaptation shifts $c_{50}$ towards the contrasts an animal has recently seen, keeping neurons in their sensitive range.

<ul>
<li>A few rules (sum, power, exponential, log, product, quotient) differentiate most functions.</li>
<li>$\sigma' = \sigma(1 - \sigma)$, which never exceeds 0.25.</li>
<li>Saturating nonlinearities have vanishing derivatives; ReLU's is 1 wherever it is active.</li>
<li>A contrast response is most sensitive near its half-saturation contrast.</li>
