<Step n={1} title="The chain rule" minutes={25} />

If $y = f(u)$ and $u = g(x)$, then $y = f(g(x))$ and

$$
\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy}{du}\cdot\frac{du}{dx} = f'(g(x))\,g'(x)
$$

<Derivation title="Why it works: follow a nudge">
  <div>

Nudge the input by a small $h$. By the tangent-line approximation (Day 1), $u$ changes by about $g'(x)\,h$:

$$
g(x + h) \approx g(x) + g'(x)\,h
$$

  </div>
  <div>

That change in $u$ is itself a small nudge to $f$, so $y$ changes by about $f'(u)$ times it:

$$
f(g(x + h)) \approx f(g(x)) + f'(g(x))\,g'(x)\,h
$$

  </div>
  <div>

Comparing with $f(g(x + h)) \approx f(g(x)) + \frac{dy}{dx}h$ gives $\frac{dy}{dx} = f'(g(x))\,g'(x)$. Each stage scales a nudge by its own derivative, and the scale factors multiply.

  </div>
</Derivation>

You have already used the chain rule: the derivative of $e^{ax}$ is $ae^{ax}$ (outer function $e^u$, inner $u = ax$), and yesterday's gain factor in the activation figure came from it too. The derivative of the Gaussian tuning curve on Day 1 had outer function $e^u$ and inner function $u = -(\theta - \mu)^2/2\sigma^2$, whose derivative is $-(\theta - \mu)/\sigma^2$.

<Step n={2} title="Model neurons" minutes={25} />

A sigmoid model neuron with one input computes $r = \sigma(wx + b)$. There are three quantities you might nudge: the input $x$, the weight $w$ and the bias $b$. With $u = wx + b$:

$$
\frac{\partial r}{\partial x} = \sigma'(u)\,w, \qquad \frac{\partial r}{\partial w} = \sigma'(u)\,x, \qquad \frac{\partial r}{\partial b} = \sigma'(u)
$$

(The curly $\partial$ means we vary one quantity and hold the others fixed: a partial derivative, Day 4.) The first says how sensitive the neuron is to its input. The second is what learning needs: how the output would change if the synapse changed. Notice that it is proportional to the input $x$, so a synapse whose input is silent gets no learning signal, much like Hebbian plasticity (Week 42).

<Step n={3} title="Long chains: vanishing and exploding" minutes={30} />

For a chain of $L$ stages, $h_L = f_L(\cdots f_2(f_1(h_0)))$, the chain rule applied repeatedly gives

$$
\frac{dh_L}{dh_0} = f_L'(h_{L-1})\cdots f_2'(h_1)\,f_1'(h_0) = \prod_{l=1}^{L} f_l'(h_{l-1})
$$

A product of many numbers is dangerous. If the factors are typically less than 1, the product shrinks exponentially; if greater than 1, it grows exponentially. In a deep network the stages are layers; in a recurrent network they are time steps. Bengio, Simard and Frasconi (1994), today's paper, showed that this makes it very hard for a recurrent network to learn that something which happened many steps ago matters now.

Through 20 sigmoid stages a nudge at the start is multiplied by less than $10^{-12}$, well beyond the $0.25^{20} \approx 10^{-12}$ bound because the sigmoid's slope is below its maximum away from zero. That answers the prediction. Linear stages with weight 1.2 multiply it by about 38 after 20 steps and 0.8 shrinks it to about 0.01: the eigenvalue story of Week 6, now for learning signals. ReLU passes it through exactly, as long as the units stay active.

<Step n={4} title="Working backwards" minutes={25} />

In a long chain you can multiply the local derivatives in either order. For learning, the useful order is **backwards**, from the output to the input: start with 1 at the output, and at each stage multiply by the local derivative. Every intermediate product is then the derivative of the output with respect to that stage, which you will need on Day 5 to update every weight in a network at once.

Take a chain that ends in a squared error: $u = wx$, $r = \sigma(u)$, $L = \frac{1}{2}(r - y)^2$, with a target $y$.

The gradient is negative, so increasing the weight lowers the error, and one step in the direction opposite to the gradient does exactly that. This is gradient descent (Week 12) on a single synapse. On Day 5 the same backwards walk runs through a whole network.

<Prove title="The derivative of a Gaussian by the chain rule" task="Use the chain rule to differentiate $g(\theta) = \exp\left(-\dfrac{(\theta - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)$, and check that it gives the tuning-curve slope from Day 1.">

Let $u = -\dfrac{(\theta - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}$, so $g = e^u$. The outer derivative is $\dfrac{dg}{du} = e^u$.

For the inner one, apply the chain rule again: $(\theta - \mu)^2$ has outer function $v^2$ and inner $v = \theta - \mu$, so its derivative is $2(\theta - \mu)\cdot 1$. Hence $\dfrac{du}{d\theta} = -\dfrac{2(\theta - \mu)}{2\sigma^2} = -\dfrac{\theta - \mu}{\sigma^2}$.

Multiplying: $g'(\theta) = -\dfrac{\theta - \mu}{\sigma^2}\exp\left(-\dfrac{(\theta - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)$. With the amplitude $A$ in front, this is the Day 1 formula.

</Prove>

<Key title="Before you go">
<ul>
<li>The chain rule multiplies the derivatives of the stages of a composition.</li>
<li>A neuron's weight gradient is the slope of its nonlinearity times its input.</li>
<li>Through many stages the product of derivatives vanishes or explodes exponentially.</li>
<li>Multiplying local derivatives from the output backwards gives the gradient at every stage.</li>
</ul>
</Key>
