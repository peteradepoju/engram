<Step n={1} title="Partial derivatives" minutes={25} />

For a function of several variables, $f(w_1, w_2, \dots, w_n)$, the **partial derivative** with respect to $w_i$ is the ordinary derivative in $w_i$ with every other variable held fixed:

$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial w_i} = \lim_{h \to 0}\frac{f(\dots, w_i + h, \dots) - f(\dots, w_i, \dots)}{h}
$$

For example, with $f(w_1, w_2) = w_1^2 + 3w_1w_2$: treating $w_2$ as a constant gives $\partial f/\partial w_1 = 2w_1 + 3w_2$, and treating $w_1$ as a constant gives $\partial f/\partial w_2 = 3w_1$.

The squared error of a linear neuron with two weights, fitted to data $(x_{t1}, x_{t2}, y_t)$, is a function of the weights:

$$
L(w_1, w_2) = \frac{1}{2T}\sum_t (w_1x_{t1} + w_2x_{t2} - y_t)^2
$$

By the chain rule, $\partial L/\partial w_1 = \frac{1}{T}\sum_t (w_1x_{t1} + w_2x_{t2} - y_t)\,x_{t1}$: the average error times the input on that synapse.

<Step n={2} title="The gradient" minutes={25} />

The **gradient** stacks the partial derivatives into a vector:

$$
\nabla f = \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial w_1}, \dots, \frac{\partial f}{\partial w_n}\right)
$$

The tangent-line approximation of Day 1 becomes a tangent-plane approximation: for a small step $\mathbf{h}$,

$$
f(\mathbf{w} + \mathbf{h}) \approx f(\mathbf{w}) + \nabla f(\mathbf{w}) \cdot \mathbf{h}
$$

So the rate of change in a direction given by a unit vector $\mathbf{u}$, the **directional derivative**, is $\nabla f \cdot \mathbf{u}$.

<Prove title="The gradient is the direction of steepest increase" task="Show that, among all unit vectors $\mathbf{u}$, the directional derivative $\nabla f \cdot \mathbf{u}$ is largest when $\mathbf{u}$ points along $\nabla f$, and that moving perpendicular to $\nabla f$ leaves f unchanged to first order.">

By the geometry of the dot product (Week 4), $\nabla f \cdot \mathbf{u} = \lVert\nabla f\rVert\,\lVert\mathbf{u}\rVert\cos\theta = \lVert\nabla f\rVert\cos\theta$, where $\theta$ is the angle between them.

This is largest when $\cos\theta = 1$, that is when $\mathbf{u} = \nabla f / \lVert\nabla f\rVert$, and then the rate of increase is $\lVert\nabla f\rVert$. It is most negative, the steepest descent, when $\mathbf{u}$ points along $-\nabla f$.

When $\mathbf{u}$ is perpendicular to $\nabla f$, $\cos\theta = 0$, so $f$ does not change to first order. Directions of no change trace out the contour lines, so the gradient is perpendicular to the contours.

</Prove>

The gradient of the squared error is $\frac{1}{T}X^\top(X\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y})$. Setting it to zero gives $X^\top X\mathbf{w} = X^\top\mathbf{y}$, the normal equations of Week 5, now derived by calculus instead of geometry. Stepping against it, $\mathbf{w} \leftarrow \mathbf{w} - \eta\nabla L$, is the delta rule of Widrow and Hoff and the simplest form of gradient descent (Week 12).

<Step n={3} title="A neuron's most sensitive direction" minutes={30} />

Now take the gradient with respect to the **stimulus** rather than the weights. For the linear-nonlinear neuron $r = \sigma(\mathbf{w} \cdot \mathbf{x})$, the chain rule gives

$$
\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\, r = \sigma'(\mathbf{w} \cdot \mathbf{x})\,\mathbf{w}
$$

The gradient always points along the receptive field $\mathbf{w}$, scaled by the slope of the nonlinearity. By the proof above, the small change of stimulus that raises firing most, for a fixed size of change, is a change along $\mathbf{w}$. That answers the prediction: change pixels in proportion to their weights. This is the logic behind **adversarial examples** for deep networks (small, carefully shaped changes that flip a decision) and behind finding images that maximally drive a neuron (Week 100).

<Step n={4} title="Populations add up" minutes={25} />

Day 1 gave one Poisson neuron's Fisher information, $I_i(\theta) = Tf_i'(\theta)^2/f_i(\theta)$. For a population of independent neurons, Fisher information **adds**: $I(\theta) = \sum_i I_i(\theta)$. Seung and Sompolinsky (1993), today's paper, used this to show how precisely a population can encode a stimulus, and that an optimal decoder reaches the bound $\delta\theta \approx 1/\sqrt{I}$ for large populations.

Every fourfold increase in neurons halves the threshold: $1/\sqrt{N}$ scaling, because independent informations add. With a few hundred neurons the population can resolve fractions of a degree, finer than any single neuron. In real cortex, noise correlations limit this gain, a central topic of Week 119.
