<Step n={2} title="Weekly challenge" minutes={60} />

**The experiment.** 50 orientation-tuned neurons respond to gratings within 4° of 45°, and a monkey must report whether each is clockwise or anticlockwise of 45° (Week 4's discrimination idea, now as a binary choice). You will decode the choice with **logistic regression**: $p = \sigma(\mathbf{w} \cdot \mathbf{z})$ is the predicted probability of "clockwise", where $\mathbf{z}$ is the z-scored population response with a 1 appended for the bias. The loss is the cross-entropy

$$
L(\mathbf{w}) = -\frac{1}{T}\sum_t\left[y_t\ln p_t + (1 - y_t)\ln(1 - p_t)\right]
$$

Look at the weights. The largest belong to neurons preferring roughly 20° and 80°, one to one and a half tuning widths either side of 45°, with opposite signs: neurons whose tuning curves are steep at 45° (Day 1), so their rates change most across the judgement. Neurons preferring 45° itself, which fire most, get much smaller weights. (The weights are noisy, because neighbouring neurons carry overlapping information and the decoder can share weight between them in many ways.)

Through ten sigmoid layers the gradient reaching layer 1 is about a millionth of what layer 10 receives, so the first layers barely learn. Tanh loses a factor of a few; ReLU with weights scaled by $\sqrt{2/n}$ keeps the gradient roughly constant, because on average half the units are active, each passing the gradient with slope 1, and the factor of 2 makes up for the other half. This scaling, from He and colleagues (2015), is now the default initialisation for ReLU networks (Week 82).

**Part C: feedback alignment.** Backpropagation sends errors back to the hidden layer through $\mathbf{w}_2$, the transpose of the forward output weights. The brain would need a separate pathway with exactly matching weights, the "weight transport problem". Lillicrap and colleagues (2016) tried sending errors back through a **fixed random** vector $\mathbf{B}$ instead. Run the next cell to set up Day 5's tuning-curve task.

The network learns with random feedback. The trick is that the forward weights adapt so that the random feedback becomes useful: the learned $\mathbf{w}_2$ ends up pointing partly along $\mathbf{B}$, and the hidden layer's updates then roughly follow the true gradient. Exact symmetric feedback turns out not to be necessary, which removed one of the strongest arguments that the brain could not do anything like backpropagation.

<Prove title="1. The gradient of the cross-entropy" task="For one example with $p = \sigma(u)$, $u = \mathbf{w} \cdot \mathbf{z}$, and $\ell = -[y\ln p + (1 - y)\ln(1 - p)]$, show that $\partial\ell/\partial\mathbf{w} = (p - y)\,\mathbf{z}$.">

By the chain rule, $\dfrac{\partial\ell}{\partial\mathbf{w}} = \dfrac{d\ell}{dp}\cdot\dfrac{dp}{du}\cdot\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial\mathbf{w}}$.

$\dfrac{d\ell}{dp} = -\dfrac{y}{p} + \dfrac{1 - y}{1 - p} = \dfrac{p - y}{p(1 - p)}$. From Day 2, $\dfrac{dp}{du} = p(1 - p)$. And $\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial\mathbf{w}} = \mathbf{z}$.

The $p(1 - p)$ factors cancel, leaving $(p - y)\,\mathbf{z}$. Averaging over examples gives $\frac{1}{T}Z^\top(\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{y})$. The cancellation is why the cross-entropy pairs so well with a sigmoid: the saturating slope that made squared error learn slowly disappears from the gradient.

</Prove>

<Prove title="2. He initialisation" task="Suppose a ReLU layer's pre-activations $z_j = \sum_i W_{ij}h_i$ have independent weights with mean 0 and variance $v$, and the inputs $h_i$ are independent of them. Assume each input's pre-activation is symmetric about zero. Show that keeping the mean square of activity the same from layer to layer requires $v = 2/n$.">

$\operatorname{Var}(z_j) = \sum_i\operatorname{Var}(W_{ij})\,\mathbb{E}[h_i^2] = n\,v\,\mathbb{E}[h^2]$, since the weights have mean zero and are independent of the inputs.

The next layer's input is $h' = \max(0, z)$. For $z$ symmetric about zero, half its probability is below zero, so $\mathbb{E}[h'^2] = \frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}[z^2] = \frac{1}{2}n\,v\,\mathbb{E}[h^2]$.

For this to equal $\mathbb{E}[h^2]$ we need $\frac{1}{2}nv = 1$, so $v = 2/n$, a standard deviation of $\sqrt{2/n}$. The same argument run backwards keeps gradient sizes constant, which is what you saw in Part B.

</Prove>
