<Step n={1} title="Fixed points" minutes={20} />

For a system $\dot{\mathbf{x}} = F(\mathbf{x})$, a **fixed point** $\mathbf{x}^*$ satisfies $F(\mathbf{x}^*) = \mathbf{0}$: if the system starts there, it stays. Whether it returns after a small push depends on the Jacobian $J = \partial F/\partial\mathbf{x}$ at $\mathbf{x}^*$ (Day 2). If every eigenvalue of $J$ has negative real part, small perturbations decay and the fixed point is **stable**; if any has positive real part it is **unstable**. A theorem (Hartman-Grobman) makes this precise: unless an eigenvalue has zero real part, the nonlinear system near $\mathbf{x}^*$ behaves like its linearisation. Everything from Week 6 now applies locally: nodes, spirals and saddles.

The Wilson-Cowan circuit of Week 6, with its sigmoids put back in, is

$$
\tau_E\dot{E} = -E + S(w_{EE}E - w_{EI}I + h_E), \qquad \tau_I\dot{I} = -I + S(w_{IE}E - w_{II}I + h_I)
$$

where $S$ is a sigmoid and $h_E$, $h_I$ are external drives.

<Step n={2} title="Newton's method" minutes={30} />

To solve $F(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{0}$, start with a guess and replace $F$ by its linearisation: $F(\mathbf{x} + \boldsymbol\delta) \approx F(\mathbf{x}) + J\boldsymbol\delta = \mathbf{0}$. Solving for the step gives **Newton's method**:

$$
\mathbf{x} \leftarrow \mathbf{x} - J(\mathbf{x})^{-1}F(\mathbf{x})
$$

(computed with `np.linalg.solve`, never by inverting). Near a solution, Newton's method roughly doubles the number of correct digits each step. Far from one it can wander, so for finding all fixed points the usual approach is to start it from a grid of guesses and keep the distinct answers.

<PyCell code={String.raw`

  import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
  def S(x): return 1 / (1 + np.exp(-(x - 4)))
  wEE, wEI, wIE, wII, hE, hI, tauI = 18.0, 10.0, 10.0, 2.0, 0.0, 0.0, 2.0
  def F(z):
      E, I = z
      return np.array([-E + S(wEE * E - wEI * I + hE), (-I + S(wIE * E - wII * I + hI)) / tauI])
  def jacobian(z, h=1e-6):
      J = np.zeros((2, 2))
      for j in range(2):
          e = np.zeros(2); e[j] = h
          J[:, j] = (F(z + e) - F(z - e)) / (2 * h)
      return J
  print(F(np.array([0.5, 0.5])))

  def newton(z, steps=50):
      z = z.astype(float).copy()
      for _ in range(steps):
          z = z - np.linalg.solve(jacobian(z), F(z))
      return z

  fixed_points = []
  for E0 in np.linspace(0, 1, 6):
      for I0 in np.linspace(0, 1, 6):
          z = newton(np.array([E0, I0]))
          if np.linalg.norm(F(z)) < 1e-10 and all(np.linalg.norm(z - q) > 1e-6 for q in fixed_points):
              fixed_points.append(z)

  def classify(z):
      re = np.linalg.eigvals(jacobian(z)).real
      if np.all(re < 0):
          return "stable"
      if re.min() < 0 < re.max():
          return "saddle"
      return "unstable"

      print(z.round(4), classify(z), np.linalg.eigvals(jacobian(z)).round(3))

There are two stable states, one with almost no activity and one with high activity, separated by saddles and an unstable point. The circuit is **bistable**: a brief input can flip it from the low state to the high one, and it stays there after the input ends. That is a memory, and bistable circuits like this are one proposed basis of persistent activity in working memory (Week 46).

<Step n={3} title="Phase planes and bistability" minutes={30} />

In two dimensions you can see everything. The **nullclines** are the curves where one variable stops changing: $\dot{E} = 0$ and $\dot{I} = 0$. Fixed points are where they cross. Away from the nullclines the flow is fixed in sign, so the nullclines divide the plane into regions where E and I each rise or fall.

Try two experiments in the figure. First set $w_{EE}$ near 18 and the drive near 0: five crossings, the bistable circuit you just found. Then lower $w_{EE}$ to 12 and raise the drive from 0 to 3: a single fixed point that is a stable spiral at low drive becomes an unstable spiral, and trajectories settle onto a closed loop instead, a sustained **oscillation**. The first change, in which fixed points collide and vanish in pairs, is a saddle-node bifurcation; the second, in which a complex pair of eigenvalues crosses the imaginary axis, is a Hopf bifurcation, Week 6's E-I story in its full nonlinear form.

<Step n={4} title="The paradoxical effect" minutes={25} />

Consider a simpler circuit with threshold-linear neurons, both firing, so their steady rates satisfy $\mathbf{r} = W\mathbf{r} + \mathbf{h}$ with

$$
W = \begin{pmatrix} w_{EE} & -w_{EI} \\ w_{IE} & -w_{II} \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{r} = (I - W)^{-1}\mathbf{h}
$$

If $w_{EE} \gt 1$, the excitatory population on its own would be unstable: its recurrent excitation alone exceeds its leak. The circuit can still be stable overall if inhibition is strong enough. Such a circuit is called **inhibition stabilised**. Tsodyks, Skaggs, Sejnowski and McNaughton (1997), today's paper, showed that it responds to extra drive onto its inhibitory cells in a surprising way.

In the weak circuit, extra drive to the inhibitory cells makes them fire more, as you would expect. In the inhibition-stabilised circuit they fire *less*. That answers the prediction: it depends on the circuit, and the direction tells you which kind of circuit you have.

<Prove title="Why the response is paradoxical" task="Using $\mathbf{r} = (I - W)^{-1}\mathbf{h}$, show that $\partial r_I/\partial h_I = (1 - w_{EE})/\Delta$, where $\Delta = \det(I - W)$, and explain why it is negative exactly when $w_{EE} \gt 1$ in a stable circuit.">

$I - W = \begin{pmatrix} 1 - w_{EE} & w_{EI} \\ -w_{IE} & 1 + w_{II} \end{pmatrix}$, with determinant $\Delta = (1 - w_{EE})(1 + w_{II}) + w_{EI}w_{IE}$.

The 2 by 2 inverse (Week 5) is $\frac{1}{\Delta}\begin{pmatrix} 1 + w_{II} & -w_{EI} \\ w_{IE} & 1 - w_{EE} \end{pmatrix}$, so $r_I = \frac{1}{\Delta}(w_{IE}h_E + (1 - w_{EE})h_I)$ and $\partial r_I/\partial h_I = (1 - w_{EE})/\Delta$.

The circuit's dynamics are governed by $W - I$, whose determinant equals $\det(I - W) = \Delta$ for a 2 by 2 matrix. Stability needs a positive determinant (Week 6, Day 2), so $\Delta \gt 0$. The sign of the response is then the sign of $1 - w_{EE}$: negative exactly when the excitatory subnetwork alone would be unstable.

The intuition: the extra drive raises inhibition, which lowers excitation, which removes so much of the excitatory input to the inhibitory cells that their rate falls below where it started. Experiments using optogenetics have found this signature in mouse visual cortex, suggesting that cortex operates as an inhibition-stabilised network (Week 107).

</Prove>
