<Step n={1} title="Second derivatives and the Hessian" minutes={20} />

The **Hessian** of $f(\mathbf{w})$ is the matrix of second partial derivatives,

$$
H_{ij} = \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial w_i\,\partial w_j}
$$

For smooth functions the order of differentiation does not matter, so $H$ is **symmetric** (Week 6's spectral theorem applies: real eigenvalues, perpendicular eigenvectors). The Hessian is the Jacobian of the gradient. The second-order Taylor expansion is

$$
f(\mathbf{w} + \mathbf{h}) \approx f(\mathbf{w}) + \nabla f(\mathbf{w})\cdot\mathbf{h} + \tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{h}^\top H(\mathbf{w})\,\mathbf{h}
$$

The last term is a quadratic form (Week 6, Day 5). Along a unit direction $\mathbf{u}$, the curvature is $\mathbf{u}^\top H\mathbf{u}$, which lies between the smallest and largest eigenvalues of $H$.

<Step n={2} title="Classifying critical points" minutes={25} />

At a **critical point** the gradient is zero, so the expansion becomes $f(\mathbf{w} + \mathbf{h}) \approx f(\mathbf{w}) + \frac{1}{2}\mathbf{h}^\top H\mathbf{h}$, and the eigenvalues of $H$ decide what kind of point it is:

- all positive: a **minimum** (the function curves up in every direction);
- all negative: a **maximum**;
- some of each sign: a **saddle point**, up in some directions and down in others;
- some zero: the test is inconclusive, and higher-order terms decide.

Dauphin and colleagues (2014), today's paper, argued that in the very high-dimensional losses of neural networks almost all critical points with high loss are saddles: for a point to be a minimum, every one of millions of eigenvalues must be positive, which is very unlikely by chance.

<Prove title="A positive definite Hessian means a strict minimum" task="Suppose $\nabla f(\mathbf{w}^*) = \mathbf{0}$ and every eigenvalue of $H(\mathbf{w}^*)$ is at least $\lambda_{\min} \gt 0$. Use the second-order expansion to show that $f(\mathbf{w}^* + \mathbf{h}) \gt f(\mathbf{w}^*)$ for every small nonzero $\mathbf{h}$.">

The expansion gives $f(\mathbf{w}^* + \mathbf{h}) - f(\mathbf{w}^*) = \frac{1}{2}\mathbf{h}^\top H\mathbf{h} + R(\mathbf{h})$, where the remainder $R$ is of order $\lVert\mathbf{h}\rVert^3$.

By the Rayleigh quotient (Week 6 Forge), $\mathbf{h}^\top H\mathbf{h} \geq \lambda_{\min}\lVert\mathbf{h}\rVert^2$. So the difference is at least $\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{\min}\lVert\mathbf{h}\rVert^2 - C\lVert\mathbf{h}\rVert^3$ for some constant $C$, which is positive once $\lVert\mathbf{h}\rVert \lt \lambda_{\min}/(2C)$.

So every nearby point is higher: $\mathbf{w}^*$ is a strict local minimum. The smallest eigenvalue sets how sharply the bowl rises in its flattest direction.

</Prove>

<Step n={3} title="Curvature and gradient descent" minutes={30} />

Near a minimum, a loss is approximately the quadratic $\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{w}^\top H\mathbf{w}$ (taking the minimum at the origin), whose gradient is $H\mathbf{w}$. Gradient descent with learning rate $\eta$ then reads $\mathbf{w} \leftarrow (I - \eta H)\mathbf{w}$, a linear map. In the eigenvector basis of $H$, each component is multiplied by $1 - \eta\lambda_i$ every step (Week 6, Day 3).

- For stability every $|1 - \eta\lambda_i|$ must be below 1, so $\eta \lt 2/\lambda_{\max}$: the **sharpest** direction limits the learning rate.
- The **flattest** direction then shrinks by $1 - \eta\lambda_{\min} \geq 1 - 2\lambda_{\min}/\lambda_{\max}$ per step, so the number of steps needed grows in proportion to the **condition number** $\kappa = \lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}$.

A round bowl takes a single step; condition number 10 takes about 70 steps and condition number 100 about 700. Steps grow in proportion to the condition number, so the answer to the prediction is about 100 times as many. This is why correlated inputs slow learning (tomorrow's figure), and why optimisers such as Adam and batch normalisation, which effectively rescale directions, help so much (Weeks 13 and 82).

<Step n={4} title="Newton's method" minutes={30} />

Instead of a fixed small step, jump straight to the minimum of the second-order expansion. Setting the gradient of $f + \nabla f\cdot\mathbf{h} + \frac{1}{2}\mathbf{h}^\top H\mathbf{h}$ to zero gives $H\mathbf{h} = -\nabla f$, so

$$
\mathbf{w} \leftarrow \mathbf{w} - H^{-1}\nabla f
$$

This is yesterday's Newton's method applied to the equation $\nabla f = \mathbf{0}$. On an exact quadratic it lands on the minimum in one step, whatever the condition number, because $H^{-1}$ undoes the stretching. On other functions it converges very fast near a minimum. Its costs are forming and solving with an $n \times n$ Hessian, prohibitive for millions of parameters, and that near a saddle it heads straight for the saddle, since it seeks any point where the gradient is zero (the problem Dauphin and colleagues addressed). For models with a few hundred parameters, such as the statistical models of neurons in Week 35, it is the method of choice.

Newton's method settles to the best loss within three or four steps; gradient descent, slowed by the correlated inputs, is still visibly short after a hundred. The Hessian of the cross-entropy, $Z^\top\operatorname{diag}(p(1 - p))Z/T$, is positive semidefinite for every $\mathbf{w}$ (it is a sum of $p(1-p)\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}^\top$ terms), so the loss is **convex**: it has no saddles or spurious minima, only one bowl. Logistic regression and the Poisson models of Week 35 share this property, which is a large part of why they are so widely used.

<ul>
<li>The Hessian is the symmetric matrix of second derivatives; it gives the local curvature.</li>
<li>Its eigenvalues classify critical points; in high dimensions most bad critical points are saddles.</li>
<li>The sharpest curvature caps the learning rate; the condition number sets how slow descent is.</li>
<li>Newton's method uses the Hessian to jump to the minimum of the local quadratic.</li>
