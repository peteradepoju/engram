<Step n={1} title="The basic identities" minutes={25} />

We write $\nabla_{\mathbf{w}}f$ for the gradient of a scalar $f$ with respect to the vector $\mathbf{w}$, a vector of the same shape as $\mathbf{w}$. Three identities cover most of what you need. Each can be checked entry by entry.

$$
\nabla_{\mathbf{w}}(\mathbf{a}^\top\mathbf{w}) = \mathbf{a}, \qquad \nabla_{\mathbf{w}}(\mathbf{w}^\top A\mathbf{w}) = (A + A^\top)\mathbf{w}, \qquad \nabla_{\mathbf{w}}\tfrac{1}{2}\lVert X\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y}\rVert^2 = X^\top(X\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y})
$$

For symmetric $A$ the middle one is $2A\mathbf{w}$, the vector version of $\frac{d}{dw}aw^2 = 2aw$. The third follows from the first two by expanding $\lVert X\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y}\rVert^2 = \mathbf{w}^\top X^\top X\mathbf{w} - 2\mathbf{y}^\top X\mathbf{w} + \mathbf{y}^\top\mathbf{y}$. Adding a ridge penalty $\frac{\lambda}{2}\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert^2$ adds $\lambda\mathbf{w}$, and setting the total to zero gives $(X^\top X + \lambda I)\mathbf{w} = X^\top\mathbf{y}$: Week 5's ridge solution in one line.

<Prove title="The gradient of a quadratic form" task="Show that $\dfrac{\partial}{\partial w_k}\left(\mathbf{w}^\top A\mathbf{w}\right) = \left((A + A^\top)\mathbf{w}\right)_k$.">

Write the quadratic form out: $\mathbf{w}^\top A\mathbf{w} = \sum_i\sum_j w_iA_{ij}w_j$.

Differentiating with respect to $w_k$, the terms with $i = k$ give $\sum_j A_{kj}w_j$ and the terms with $j = k$ give $\sum_i w_iA_{ik}$ (the single term with $i = j = k$, $A_{kk}w_k^2$, contributes $2A_{kk}w_k$, split between the two sums).

So the derivative is $\sum_j A_{kj}w_j + \sum_i A_{ik}w_i = (A\mathbf{w})_k + (A^\top\mathbf{w})_k$. Stacking over $k$ gives $(A + A^\top)\mathbf{w}$.

</Prove>

<Step n={2} title="Gradients with respect to matrices" minutes={20} />

For a layer with weight matrix $W$, the loss gradient with respect to $W$ is a matrix of the same shape, with entry $(i, j)$ equal to $\partial L/\partial W_{ij}$. For $\hat{\mathbf{y}} = W\mathbf{x}$ and $L = \frac{1}{2}\lVert W\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{y}\rVert^2$, output $i$ depends on $W_{ij}$ through $\hat{y}_i = \sum_j W_{ij}x_j$, so $\partial L/\partial W_{ij} = (\hat{y}_i - y_i)\,x_j$. All together:

$$
\nabla_W L = (W\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{y})\,\mathbf{x}^\top
$$

an **outer product** of the error at the output with the input. Each synapse changes in proportion to (error at its postsynaptic neuron) × (activity of its presynaptic neuron). With the error replaced by the output itself, this is Hebb's rule; with the error as written, it is the delta rule. Summed over a batch, it is the $X^\top\bar{Z}$ you wrote in last week's backward pass.

<Step n={3} title="Fitting a Poisson neuron" minutes={35} />

The **Poisson generalised linear model** (GLM) describes a neuron's spike count in each time bin as Poisson with rate $\lambda_t = \exp(\mathbf{x}_t\cdot\mathbf{w})$, where $\mathbf{x}_t$ holds the stimulus features at time $t$ (plus a 1 for the baseline). It is the statistical form of the linear-nonlinear-Poisson model you will build next week and study in depth in Week 35. Its log-likelihood, up to a constant, is

$$
\ell(\mathbf{w}) = \sum_t\left[y_t\,\mathbf{x}_t\cdot\mathbf{w} - \exp(\mathbf{x}_t\cdot\mathbf{w})\right]
$$

<Derivation title="The gradient and Hessian of the Poisson log-likelihood">
  <div>

The first term is linear in $\mathbf{w}$, with gradient $\sum_t y_t\mathbf{x}_t = X^\top\mathbf{y}$.

  </div>
  <div>

The second term, by the chain rule, has gradient $\sum_t\exp(\mathbf{x}_t\cdot\mathbf{w})\,\mathbf{x}_t = X^\top\boldsymbol\lambda$. So

$$
\nabla\ell = X^\top(\mathbf{y} - \boldsymbol\lambda)
$$

the same "input times error" form as squared error and logistic regression.

  </div>
  <div>

Differentiating again, $H = -X^\top\operatorname{diag}(\boldsymbol\lambda)X$, which is negative semidefinite: the log-likelihood is concave, with a single peak and no false maxima, so gradient ascent or Newton's method always finds the best fit.

  </div>
</Derivation>

<Step n={4} title="Oja's rule" minutes={25} />

A linear neuron $y = \mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}$ learning by Hebb's rule, $\Delta\mathbf{w} = \eta\,y\,\mathbf{x}$, strengthens its weights without limit. Oja (1982), today's paper, normalised the weights after each update, $\mathbf{w} \leftarrow (\mathbf{w} + \eta y\mathbf{x})/\lVert\mathbf{w} + \eta y\mathbf{x}\rVert$, and expanded this to first order in the small learning rate $\eta$ (a Taylor expansion, Day 1). With $\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert = 1$ the result is

$$
\Delta\mathbf{w} = \eta\,y\,(\mathbf{x} - y\,\mathbf{w})
$$

a Hebbian term $y\mathbf{x}$ and a decay $-y^2\mathbf{w}$, both computable at the synapse from its own input, the neuron's output and its own weight. Averaged over inputs with covariance $C$ (and mean zero), the expected update is $\eta(C\mathbf{w} - (\mathbf{w}^\top C\mathbf{w})\mathbf{w})$, which is zero exactly when $C\mathbf{w}$ is parallel to $\mathbf{w}$: when $\mathbf{w}$ is an eigenvector of $C$. Only the top eigenvector is stable.
