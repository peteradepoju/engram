  def S(x): return 1 / (1 + np.exp(-(x - 4)))
  def F(z, hE, wEE=12.0):
      E, I = z
      return np.array([-E + S(wEE * E - 10 * I + hE), (-I + S(10 * E - 2 * I)) / 2])
  def jac(z, hE, h=1e-6):
      J = np.zeros((2, 2))
      for j in range(2):
          e = np.zeros(2); e[j] = h
          J[:, j] = (F(z + e, hE) - F(z - e, hE)) / (2 * h)
      return J
  def newton(z, hE, steps=50):
      for _ in range(steps):
          z = z - np.linalg.solve(jac(z, hE), F(z, hE))
      return z

**Part B: error bars from curvature.** A neuron's spike counts to 24 motion directions, 5 repeats each, follow a Poisson GLM with $\lambda = \exp(w_0 + w_1\cos\theta + w_2\sin\theta)$, a smooth direction tuning curve. The **inverse of the negative Hessian** of the log-likelihood at its maximum estimates the covariance of the fitted weights, so the square roots of its diagonal are their standard errors. A 95% interval is the estimate plus or minus 1.96 standard errors.

The curvature of the likelihood at its peak is the observed Fisher information: a sharply curved peak means the data determine the weights well, and a flat one means they do not. Checking coverage by simulation, as you just did, is the honest way to test any error bar before trusting it (Week 59).

Near the saddle the Hessian has eigenvalues 1 and −1. Along the downhill direction each step multiplies $y$ by $1 + 0.1 = 1.1$, so climbing from $\epsilon$ to 0.5 takes about $\ln(0.5/\epsilon)/\ln 1.1$ steps: each factor of 1000 in $\epsilon$ adds $\ln 1000/\ln 1.1 \approx 72$ steps. Gradient descent always escapes eventually, but it can linger for a long time on a plateau with a tiny gradient, which is what Dauphin and colleagues argued slows training in high dimensions. Noise, as in stochastic gradient descent (Week 12), kicks the system off the saddle faster.

<Prove title="1. The Hessian of the Poisson log-likelihood" task="With $\ell(\mathbf{w}) = \sum_t[y_t\,\mathbf{x}_t\cdot\mathbf{w} - \exp(\mathbf{x}_t\cdot\mathbf{w})]$, show that $H = -\sum_t\lambda_t\mathbf{x}_t\mathbf{x}_t^\top$ and that it is negative semidefinite.">

The gradient is $\sum_t(y_t - \lambda_t)\mathbf{x}_t$ with $\lambda_t = \exp(\mathbf{x}_t\cdot\mathbf{w})$. Differentiating component $i$ with respect to $w_j$, only $\lambda_t$ depends on $\mathbf{w}$, and $\partial\lambda_t/\partial w_j = \lambda_t x_{tj}$ by the chain rule. So $H_{ij} = -\sum_t\lambda_t x_{ti}x_{tj}$, that is $H = -\sum_t\lambda_t\mathbf{x}_t\mathbf{x}_t^\top = -X^\top\operatorname{diag}(\boldsymbol\lambda)X$.

For any $\mathbf{v}$, $\mathbf{v}^\top H\mathbf{v} = -\sum_t\lambda_t(\mathbf{x}_t\cdot\mathbf{v})^2 \leq 0$, since every $\lambda_t \gt 0$. So $H$ is negative semidefinite and $\ell$ is concave: any point where the gradient vanishes is the global maximum.

</Prove>

<Prove title="2. Oja's rule from a Taylor expansion" task="Starting from $\mathbf{w}' = (\mathbf{w} + \eta y\mathbf{x})/\lVert\mathbf{w} + \eta y\mathbf{x}\rVert$ with $\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert = 1$ and $y = \mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x}$, expand to first order in $\eta$ and show that $\mathbf{w}' \approx \mathbf{w} + \eta y(\mathbf{x} - y\mathbf{w})$.">

The squared length is $\lVert\mathbf{w} + \eta y\mathbf{x}\rVert^2 = 1 + 2\eta y\,\mathbf{w}\cdot\mathbf{x} + O(\eta^2) = 1 + 2\eta y^2 + O(\eta^2)$.

Using the Taylor expansion $(1 + u)^{-1/2} \approx 1 - u/2$, the reciprocal of the length is $1 - \eta y^2 + O(\eta^2)$.

Multiplying, $\mathbf{w}' \approx (\mathbf{w} + \eta y\mathbf{x})(1 - \eta y^2) = \mathbf{w} + \eta y\mathbf{x} - \eta y^2\mathbf{w} + O(\eta^2) = \mathbf{w} + \eta y(\mathbf{x} - y\mathbf{w})$. The non-local normalisation, which needs every weight at once, has become a local decay term $-\eta y^2 w_i$ at each synapse.

</Prove>
