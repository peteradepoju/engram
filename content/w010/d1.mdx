<Step n={1} title="Accumulation" minutes={20} />

If a neuron fires at a constant 20 spikes per second for 0.5 s, it fires on average $20 \times 0.5 = 10$ spikes: rate times time. When the rate changes, chop time into short pieces of width $\Delta t$ in which the rate is nearly constant, and add up rate × duration over the pieces. In the limit of very thin pieces the sum becomes the **definite integral**

$$
\int_a^b r(t)\,dt = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0}\sum_k r(t_k)\,\Delta t
$$

Geometrically it is the area between the curve and the time axis (counting area below the axis as negative). The $dt$ reminds you of the thin widths, and the units multiply: Hz times seconds gives a pure number of spikes. Adrian and Zotterman (1926), today's paper, were the first to record a single sensory neuron and show that the stimulus is encoded in this rate, which a downstream neuron effectively integrates.

<Step n={2} title="Riemann sums and numerical integration" minutes={30} />

The sum in the definition is a **Riemann sum**. How you choose the height of each rectangle matters for accuracy:

- **left edge**: $r(t_k)$ at the start of each piece; error proportional to $\Delta t$;
- **midpoint**: $r$ at the centre of each piece; error proportional to $\Delta t^2$, because errors on the two halves of each piece nearly cancel (the same symmetry as Week 8's central difference);
- **trapezoid**: the average of the two ends, $\frac{1}{2}(r(t_k) + r(t_{k+1}))\Delta t$; also error proportional to $\Delta t^2$.

  def left(f, a, b, n):
      t = np.linspace(a, b, n + 1)[:-1]
      return np.sum(f(t)) * (b - a) / n

  def midpoint(f, a, b, n):
      dt = (b - a) / n
      t = a + dt * (np.arange(n) + 0.5)
      return np.sum(f(t)) * dt

  def trapezoid(f, a, b, n):
      t = np.linspace(a, b, n + 1)
      y = f(t)
      return (np.sum(y) - 0.5 * (y[0] + y[-1])) * (b - a) / n

  exact = np.e - 1
  errs = {name: [abs(rule(np.exp, 0, 1, n) - exact) for n in [1, 10, 100]] for name, rule in [("left", left), ("midpoint", midpoint), ("trapezoid", trapezoid)]}

The midpoint rule is about twice as accurate as the trapezoid rule, and both are far better than left edges. Libraries offer still better methods (Simpson's rule, adaptive quadrature in `scipy.integrate`), but for evenly sampled data such as binned spike rates the trapezoid rule is the usual choice.

<Step n={3} title="Expected spike counts" minutes={30} />

The receptor fires about 29 spikes in the second, 28 of them after the stimulus starts. That answers the prediction. Its peak rate of 65 Hz would suggest far more, but the peak lasts only a moment; its adapted rate of 25 Hz, applied to the 0.8 s of stimulus, would suggest 20, missing the transient. Only the integral gets it right. Integrals of exponentials appear everywhere in neuroscience, and $\int_0^\infty Ae^{-s/\tau}ds = A\tau$ is worth memorising: an exponentially decaying response contributes its height times its time constant.

<Step n={4} title="The fundamental theorem" minutes={25} />

Define the **cumulative** count $N(t) = \int_0^t r(s)\,ds$, the expected number of spikes up to time $t$. The **fundamental theorem of calculus** says

$$
\frac{dN}{dt} = r(t), \qquad \int_a^b r(t)\,dt = N(b) - N(a)
$$

The rate of growth of the accumulated total is the rate itself: integration and differentiation undo each other. Any function whose derivative is $r$ is an **antiderivative** of $r$, and the second equation says that to evaluate an integral you need only an antiderivative evaluated at the two ends. That is how the closed form above was found: $-\tau e^{-s/\tau}$ is an antiderivative of $e^{-s/\tau}$.

`np.cumsum` and `np.diff` are the discrete versions of integrating and differentiating, and they undo each other exactly. The cumulative curve is steepest just after the stimulus starts, where the rate is highest: its slope is the rate.

<Prove title="The area under an exponential" task="Show that $\int_0^T Ae^{-t/\tau}\,dt = A\tau(1 - e^{-T/\tau})$, and that it tends to $A\tau$ as $T \to \infty$. Then explain why a synaptic current of peak $A$ and decay time constant $\tau$ delivers a charge of about $A\tau$.">

The derivative of $-\tau e^{-t/\tau}$ is $-\tau\cdot(-\frac{1}{\tau})e^{-t/\tau} = e^{-t/\tau}$ (chain rule), so $-A\tau e^{-t/\tau}$ is an antiderivative of the integrand.

By the fundamental theorem, $\int_0^T Ae^{-t/\tau}\,dt = [-A\tau e^{-t/\tau}]_0^T = -A\tau e^{-T/\tau} + A\tau = A\tau(1 - e^{-T/\tau})$. As $T$ grows, $e^{-T/\tau} \to 0$, leaving $A\tau$.

Charge is the integral of current over time. An exponentially decaying current therefore delivers peak × time constant in total: doubling either the peak or the decay time doubles the charge, a quick calculation used constantly when comparing synapses.

</Prove>

<Key title="Before you go">
<ul>
<li>An integral accumulates a rate into a total: rate × time, summed over thin slices.</li>
<li>Midpoint and trapezoid sums are much more accurate than left-edge sums.</li>
<li>A neuron's expected spike count is the integral of its rate; a peak or an average alone can mislead.</li>
<li>Integration and differentiation are inverses, as cumsum and diff are.</li>
</ul>
