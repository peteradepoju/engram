<Step n={1} title="The LNP model" minutes={20} />

With stimulus $s(t)$, the three stages are

$$
g(t) = \sum_{k=0}^{L-1} h[k]\,s(t - k), \qquad \lambda(t) = f(g(t)), \qquad y(t) \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda(t)\Delta t)
$$

- **L**: the generator signal $g$ is a causal convolution of the stimulus with the filter $h$ (Day 3), which says how much the stimulus $k$ bins ago matters now. A retinal ganglion cell's temporal filter is typically **biphasic**: a positive lobe a few tens of milliseconds back and a negative lobe further back, so the cell responds to changes in light rather than to steady light.
- **N**: a static nonlinearity $f$ (exponential, softplus, or rectified power) turns the generator signal into a non-negative rate, with a threshold below which the cell rarely fires.
- **P**: spikes are drawn at that rate, independently in each bin.

With $f = \exp$, this is exactly the Poisson GLM you fitted in Week 9: the L stage is the linear predictor $\mathbf{x}_t\cdot\mathbf{w}$, with the recent stimulus history as features.

<Step n={3} title="Recovering the filter" minutes={30} />

The **spike-triggered average** (STA) averages the stimulus history before every spike:

$$
\text{STA}[k] = \frac{\sum_t y(t)\,s(t - k)}{\sum_t y(t)}
$$

In Week 5 you saw that for a linear neuron with correlated stimuli the STA is distorted by the stimulus covariance. For **Gaussian white noise** something remarkable holds: whatever the nonlinearity (as long as it is not symmetric), the STA is proportional to the true filter. Chichilnisky (2001), this week's deep read, showed how to use this to characterise primate retinal ganglion cells.

<Prove title="Why the STA recovers the filter (Stein's lemma)" task="In one dimension, let $s$ be a standard Gaussian with density $\phi(s) = e^{-s^2/2}/\sqrt{2\pi}$, and let the rate be $f(hs)$. Use integration by parts and $\phi'(s) = -s\phi(s)$ to show that $\mathbb{E}[s\,f(hs)] = h\,\mathbb{E}[f'(hs)]$. Explain why this makes the STA proportional to $h$.">

$\mathbb{E}[s\,f(hs)] = \int s\,\phi(s)\,f(hs)\,ds = -\int\phi'(s)\,f(hs)\,ds$, using $s\phi(s) = -\phi'(s)$.

Integrate by parts: $-[\phi f]_{-\infty}^{\infty} + \int\phi(s)\,\frac{d}{ds}f(hs)\,ds$. The boundary term vanishes because $\phi$ decays to zero. By the chain rule, $\frac{d}{ds}f(hs) = h\,f'(hs)$, so the result is $h\int\phi(s)f'(hs)\,ds = h\,\mathbb{E}[f'(hs)]$.

With many stimulus dimensions the same argument, one coordinate at a time, gives $\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{s}\,f(\mathbf{h}\cdot\mathbf{s})] = \mathbf{h}\,\mathbb{E}[f'(\mathbf{h}\cdot\mathbf{s})]$. The STA is this expectation divided by the mean rate, a scalar, so it points along $\mathbf{h}$. The nonlinearity only changes its length. (If $\mathbb{E}[f']$ is zero, as for a symmetric nonlinearity like $f(g) = g^2$, the STA vanishes and you need the spike-triggered covariance instead.)

</Prove>

The STA has the true filter's shape almost exactly, despite the exponential nonlinearity. That answers the prediction, and it is the reason white noise became the standard stimulus for characterising sensory neurons. The price is that natural stimuli are not white, and for them the STA is biased (Week 5), which is why the GLM of Week 9, fitted by maximum likelihood, is used in practice.
