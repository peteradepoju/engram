<Step n={1} title="Convolution in two dimensions" minutes={25} />

For an image $X$ and a small kernel $K$ of size $h \times w$, the operation used in image processing and in neural networks is

$$
Y[i, j] = \sum_{a=0}^{h-1}\sum_{b=0}^{w-1} K[a, b]\,X[i + a, j + b]
$$

Strictly this is **cross-correlation**: true convolution flips the kernel first, $K[-a, -b]$, as in yesterday's one-dimensional formula. For symmetric kernels the two agree, and since networks learn their kernels the flip makes no difference, so deep learning calls this convolution anyway.

Each output $Y[i, j]$ is the dot product (Week 4) of the kernel with one patch of the image: exactly a linear model neuron whose receptive field is $K$, placed at position $(i, j)$. The output map is the response of a whole sheet of identical neurons tiling visual space.

<Step n={2} title="Implementing it" minutes={25} />

With no padding (**valid** mode), an $H \times W$ image and an $h \times w$ kernel give an output of size $(H - h + 1) \times (W - w + 1)$. Padding the image with zeros around its border keeps the output the same size as the input (**same** mode). A **stride** of $s$ evaluates only every $s$th position, shrinking the output by a factor of $s$ in each direction, a common way to reduce resolution in deep networks.

<Step n={3} title="Receptive fields as kernels" minutes={30} />

Two kernels model the early visual system remarkably well.

- **Difference of Gaussians** (centre-surround): a narrow positive Gaussian minus a broad negative one. Like retinal ganglion cells and LGN neurons, it responds to spots and edges but not to uniform fields.
- **Gabor**: a sinusoidal grating under a Gaussian window, $G(x, y) = \exp\left(-\frac{x'^2 + y'^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)\cos\left(\frac{2\pi y'}{\ell}\right)$ in coordinates $(x', y')$ rotated to the preferred orientation. It matches the receptive fields of V1 simple cells, which Hubel and Wiesel found respond to bars and edges at particular orientations (Week 4).

<Step n={4} title="Equivariance, pooling and convolutional networks" minutes={25} />

Because the same kernel is applied at every position, shifting the image shifts the output map by the same amount: convolution is **translation equivariant**. A single output unit at a fixed position, though, sees a different part of the image after the shift, and its response can change completely. Taking the **maximum** over a small neighbourhood of positions (max pooling) gives a response that barely changes for small shifts: approximate **invariance**. Hubel and Wiesel's complex cells, which respond to an oriented bar anywhere within their receptive field, behave this way.

The simple-cell unit at a fixed position flips from positive to strongly negative when the bar moves three pixels, because the bar now falls on an inhibitory stripe of its receptive field. That answers the prediction. The pooled, complex-cell-like response does not change at all.

This is the architecture Fukushima proposed in 1980 (today's paper): layers of **S-cells** that convolve with shared kernels, alternating with layers of **C-cells** that pool over position, repeated so that each stage combines the features of the last over a larger region with more tolerance to position. A modern convolutional network has the same structure, convolution, then a nonlinearity such as ReLU, then pooling, with one difference that changed everything: its kernels are learned by backpropagation (Week 8) rather than designed or learned locally. Trained on object recognition, such networks turn out to develop Gabor-like kernels in their first layer and to predict neural responses along the ventral visual stream (Weeks 84 and 133).

<Prove title="Convolution is translation equivariant" task="Let $X'[i, j] = X[i, j - d]$ be the image shifted right by d columns (ignoring the border). Show that $(X' \star K)[i, j] = (X \star K)[i, j - d]$, where $\star$ is today's cross-correlation.">

By definition, $(X' \star K)[i, j] = \sum_{a, b}K[a, b]\,X'[i + a, j + b] = \sum_{a, b}K[a, b]\,X[i + a, j + b - d]$.

The right-hand side is the definition of $(X \star K)$ evaluated at position $(i, j - d)$, since replacing $j$ with $j - d$ in $\sum_{a, b}K[a, b]X[i + a, j + b]$ gives exactly that sum.

So shifting the input shifts the output by the same amount: the operation commutes with translation. The proof uses only the fact that the same $K$ is used at every position, which is why weight sharing is what makes convolutional layers equivariant.

</Prove>
