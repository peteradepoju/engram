<Step n={1} title="Sinusoids" minutes={25} />

A sinusoid is

$$
x(t) = A\cos(2\pi ft + \phi)
$$

- $A$ is the **amplitude**, half the distance from trough to peak;
- $f$ is the **frequency** in cycles per second (hertz); the **period** is $1/f$ and the **angular frequency** is $\omega = 2\pi f$ radians per second;
- $\phi$ is the **phase**, which shifts the wave in time: a phase of $\phi$ moves the peaks earlier by $\phi/(2\pi f)$ seconds.

Brain rhythms are named by frequency band. The boundaries vary between papers, but typical ones are delta (1 to 4 Hz), theta (4 to 8), alpha (8 to 12), beta (13 to 30) and gamma (30 to 100). Berger (1929), today's paper, discovered the alpha rhythm in the human EEG: it is strongest with the eyes closed and is suppressed when they open.

<Step n={2} title="Complex exponentials" minutes={30} />

Sines and cosines are awkward to multiply and shift. Complex numbers make them easy. **Euler's formula** says

$$
e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta
$$

so $e^{i\theta}$ is the point at angle $\theta$ on the unit circle in the complex plane. A rotating point $e^{i2\pi ft}$ goes round the circle $f$ times per second, and its real part is the cosine. A sinusoid is the real part of a rotating complex number,

$$
A\cos(2\pi ft + \phi) = \operatorname{Re}\left[A e^{i\phi}\,e^{i2\pi ft}\right]
$$

and the fixed complex number $c = Ae^{i\phi}$, called a **phasor**, holds both the amplitude (its length) and the phase (its angle). Multiplying complex numbers multiplies lengths and adds angles, so scaling and delaying a sinusoid is a single multiplication. That is why the Fourier transform is written with complex numbers.

<Step n={3} title="Amplitude and phase" minutes={20} />

A sinusoid can also be written as a cosine plus a sine at the same frequency, $a\cos\omega t + b\sin\omega t$. The two forms describe the same thing:

<Prove title="From cosine and sine to amplitude and phase" task="Show that $a\cos\omega t + b\sin\omega t = A\cos(\omega t - \phi)$ with $A = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$ and $\phi = \operatorname{atan2}(b, a)$, using the complex number $a + ib$.">

Write $a + ib$ in polar form: $a + ib = Ae^{i\phi}$ with $A = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$ and $\phi$ its angle, so $a = A\cos\phi$ and $b = A\sin\phi$.

Then $a\cos\omega t + b\sin\omega t = A(\cos\phi\cos\omega t + \sin\phi\sin\omega t) = A\cos(\omega t - \phi)$, by the cosine difference formula.

Equivalently, $\operatorname{Re}[(a - ib)e^{i\omega t}] = \operatorname{Re}[(a - ib)(\cos\omega t + i\sin\omega t)] = a\cos\omega t + b\sin\omega t$: the coefficients of the cosine and sine are the real and (negated) imaginary parts of one phasor. The function `atan2` picks the correct quadrant, which plain `arctan(b/a)` does not.

</Prove>

<Step n={4} title="Measuring a buried rhythm" minutes={30} />

Here is the key idea of the whole week. Sample a signal at $N$ times $t_n$ covering a whole number of cycles. Then $\cos\omega t_n$ and $\sin\omega t_n$ are **orthogonal** vectors (their dot product is zero) and each has squared length $N/2$ (tomorrow's proof). So, as for any orthogonal basis (Week 4), the coefficients of a signal along them are found by **projection**:

$$
a = \frac{2}{N}\sum_n x(t_n)\cos\omega t_n, \qquad b = \frac{2}{N}\sum_n x(t_n)\sin\omega t_n
$$

Noise at other frequencies, and random noise, is nearly orthogonal to both, so it contributes little to these sums: projecting onto a frequency averages away everything else. The error shrinks like $\sigma\sqrt{2/N}$.

A rhythm five times smaller than the noise, invisible by eye, comes out with its amplitude and phase close to the truth, because 4000 samples average the noise down by a factor of about 45 (the expected error, $5\sqrt{2/4000} \approx 0.11$). That answers the prediction. Doing this projection at every frequency at once is the discrete Fourier transform (Day 3).
