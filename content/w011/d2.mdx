<Step n={1} title="Harmonics" minutes={20} />

A signal is **periodic** with period $T$ if it repeats every $T$ seconds. Its **fundamental** frequency is $f_0 = 1/T$, and the **harmonics** are the whole-number multiples $kf_0$. The **Fourier series** of a periodic signal is

$$
x(t) = \frac{a_0}{2} + \sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left[a_k\cos(2\pi kf_0t) + b_k\sin(2\pi kf_0t)\right]
$$

or, in complex form, $x(t) = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}c_k e^{i2\pi kf_0t}$. Only harmonics appear, because only sinusoids that complete a whole number of cycles in time $T$ repeat with period $T$.

<Step n={2} title="Orthogonality" minutes={25} />

Over one period, define the inner product of two signals as $\langle f, g\rangle = \int_0^T f(t)g(t)\,dt$, the continuous version of the dot product (Week 4), or for samples, $\sum_n f(t_n)g(t_n)$.

<Prove title="Harmonics are orthogonal" task="For whole numbers $j, k \geq 1$ and $\omega_0 = 2\pi/T$, show that $\int_0^T\cos(j\omega_0t)\cos(k\omega_0t)\,dt$ is 0 if $j \neq k$ and $T/2$ if $j = k$, and that $\int_0^T\cos(j\omega_0t)\sin(k\omega_0t)\,dt = 0$ for all $j, k$.">

Use the product-to-sum identity $\cos A\cos B = \frac{1}{2}[\cos(A - B) + \cos(A + B)]$.

For $j \neq k$, both $(j - k)\omega_0t$ and $(j + k)\omega_0t$ complete a whole, nonzero number of cycles in time $T$, and the integral of a cosine over whole cycles is zero. So the integral is 0.

For $j = k$, $\cos^2(k\omega_0t) = \frac{1}{2}[1 + \cos(2k\omega_0t)]$, whose integral over the period is $\frac{1}{2}T + 0 = T/2$.

For cosine times sine, $\cos A\sin B = \frac{1}{2}[\sin(A + B) - \sin(A - B)]$, and every sine integrates to zero over whole cycles (including $\sin 0 = 0$ when $j = k$). The same steps work for sums over $N$ equally spaced samples, with $T/2$ replaced by $N/2$.

</Prove>

Every off-diagonal entry is zero and every diagonal entry is $N/2$. The harmonics are an orthogonal basis, so, as in Week 4, the coefficient of a signal along each one is its projection divided by the squared length:

$$
a_k = \frac{2}{N}\sum_n x(t_n)\cos(kt_n), \qquad b_k = \frac{2}{N}\sum_n x(t_n)\sin(kt_n)
$$

This is yesterday's formula, applied to every harmonic. The constant term is $a_0/2$, the mean.

The square wave contains only **odd** harmonics, with amplitudes falling as $1/k$: a 10 Hz square wave has power at 10, 30, 50 Hz and so on. That answers the prediction. The symmetry is the reason: the square wave is unchanged if you shift it by half a period and flip its sign, which every odd harmonic also does and every even harmonic does not. The regular spike train, ten spikes per period, has power only at every tenth harmonic, but equally at all of them: sharp events contain all frequencies. Brain signals that contain sharp features, such as spikes leaking into a field potential, therefore spread power to high frequencies, a problem you will meet again with real data.

<Step n={4} title="Partial sums and energy" minutes={30} />

Keeping the first $K$ harmonics gives the **partial sum**, the best approximation using those frequencies in the least-squares sense (a projection onto their span, Week 5). Near a jump, partial sums overshoot by about 9% of the jump however many harmonics you keep, a ripple that gets narrower but not smaller: the **Gibbs phenomenon**. It is why sharp filters create ringing artefacts (Day 5).

**Parseval's theorem** says that the power of the signal is shared among its harmonics:

$$
\frac{1}{T}\int_0^T x(t)^2\,dt = \frac{a_0^2}{4} + \frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(a_k^2 + b_k^2\right)
$$

Each harmonic contributes half its squared amplitude, the mean square of a sinusoid. This is Pythagoras for an orthogonal basis (Week 4) and the reason power spectra (Day 4) add up to the variance.

Quadrupling the number of harmonics leaves the peak at about 1.18: the jump is from −1 to +1, a size of 2, and 9% of 2 is 0.18. Meanwhile Parseval's sum accounts for essentially all of the square wave's power. The power in the harmonics beyond $K$ is exactly the mean squared error of the partial sum, which is why the least-squares error falls steadily even though the peak overshoot never does.

<ul>
<li>A periodic signal is a sum of sinusoids at harmonics of its fundamental frequency.</li>
<li>Harmonics are orthogonal, so each coefficient is a projection.</li>
<li>Partial sums are least-squares approximations; near jumps they overshoot by about 9%.</li>
<li>Parseval's theorem shares the signal's power among the harmonics.</li>
