<Step n={1} title="The power spectral density" minutes={25} />

Parseval's theorem (Day 2) shares a signal's variance among frequencies. The **power spectral density** (PSD) $S(f)$ makes this precise: $S(f)\,\Delta f$ is the variance contributed by the frequencies between $f$ and $f + \Delta f$, so

$$
\operatorname{Var}(x) = \int_0^{f_s/2}S(f)\,df
$$

Its units are signal units squared per hertz (for EEG, µV²/Hz). From the DFT of $N$ samples at rate $f_s$, the simplest estimate is the **periodogram**

$$
\hat{S}(f_k) = \frac{2}{f_sN}|X_k|^2
$$

for $0 \lt k \lt N/2$ (the factor 2 adds in the mirror-image negative frequencies; it is left out at $k = 0$ and $k = N/2$).

  import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
  rng = np.random.default_rng(2020)
  fs = 250

  def aperiodic(n, chi, rng):
      """Noise with power proportional to 1/f^chi, made by shaping white noise in the frequency domain."""
      X = np.fft.rfft(rng.normal(size=n)); f = np.fft.rfftfreq(n, 1 / fs)
      scale = np.zeros_like(f); scale[1:] = f[1:] ** (-chi / 2)
      y = np.fft.irfft(X * scale, n)
      return y / y.std()

  def alpha_bursts(n, amp, rng):
      """10 Hz bursts with random timing, as alpha appears in real EEG."""
      t = np.arange(n) / fs
      env = np.convolve((rng.random(n) < 0.004).astype(float), np.hanning(int(0.8 * fs)), "same")
      return amp * np.clip(env, 0, 1) * np.sin(2 * np.pi * 10 * t + rng.uniform(0, 2 * np.pi))

<Step n={2} title="Why periodograms are noisy" minutes={25} />

The periodogram is an extraordinarily jagged estimate. Each $X_k$ of a noise signal is a sum of many random terms, so (by the central limit theorem, Week 15) its real and imaginary parts are roughly Gaussian, and $|X_k|^2$ has a chi-squared distribution with 2 degrees of freedom: an exponential distribution, whose standard deviation equals its mean. Every bin is uncertain by 100% of its own value.

Longer recordings do not help. Ten times more data gives ten times more frequency bins, each just as uncertain: the resolution improves but the noise in each estimate does not. That answers the prediction: the spectrum looks just as jagged, only finer. The cure is to trade resolution for reliability by averaging.

<Step n={3} title="Welch's method and windows" minutes={30} />

**Welch's method** cuts the recording into segments (often half-overlapping), computes a periodogram of each, and averages them. With $M$ independent segments the standard deviation of each bin falls by $\sqrt{M}$; the price is that each segment is shorter, so the frequency resolution is $1/(\text{segment length})$.

Before taking each FFT, the segment is multiplied by a **window** that tapers smoothly to zero at both ends, such as the Hann window $w_n = \frac{1}{2}(1 - \cos(2\pi n/(L - 1)))$. Cutting a segment out abruptly is the same as multiplying by a rectangle, whose sharp edges spread power from any strong frequency into distant bins (**spectral leakage**, the Gibbs effect of Day 2 in the frequency domain). The window costs a little resolution and removes most of the leakage; the PSD is then normalised by $\sum w_n^2$ instead of $L$ so that it still integrates to the variance.

Fifty-seven half-overlapping segments make the spectrum two hundred times less jagged. The resolution is now about 0.5 Hz instead of 0.017 Hz, which is plenty to see an alpha peak. Removing each segment's mean also removes power below about half a hertz, which is why the Welch spectrum integrates to slightly less than the full variance of a signal with a strong 1/f background.

<Step n={4} title="Rhythms above the background" minutes={25} />

On log-log axes the 1/f background is a straight line, $\log S = c - \chi\log f$, with **aperiodic exponent** $\chi$. Rhythms are bumps above that line. Donoghue and colleagues (2020), this week's deep read, showed that measuring "alpha power" as the raw power in the 8 to 12 Hz band confuses two things: a genuine change in the rhythm, and a change in the background's offset or exponent, which shifts the power in every band. The fix is to fit the background first and measure rhythms relative to it.

Both conditions have the same background, with exponents near the true 1.5. Alpha rises about 2.2 log units (more than a hundredfold) above it with eyes closed and less than one with eyes open: Berger's observation, made quantitative. The specparam method of Donoghue and colleagues fits the background and Gaussian peaks together, which is more robust than this two-step version, especially when peaks overlap or the background bends (a "knee").
