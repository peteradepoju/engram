<Step n={1} title="The convolution theorem" minutes={25} />

For sequences of length $N$, define **circular** convolution, $(x \circledast h)_n = \sum_m x_m\,h_{(n - m) \bmod N}$, which wraps around the ends. The convolution theorem says

$$
\text{DFT}(x \circledast h)_k = X_k\,H_k
$$

Convolution in time is multiplication in frequency. Each complex sinusoid passes through a linear, shift-invariant system unchanged in shape, merely multiplied by the complex number $H_k$: scaled by $|H_k|$ and shifted in phase by $\arg H_k$. That function of frequency is the system's **transfer function** or **frequency response**.

<Prove title="The convolution theorem" task="Starting from $Y_k = \sum_n(x \circledast h)_n e^{-i2\pi kn/N}$, show that $Y_k = X_kH_k$.">

Substitute the definition and swap the order of summation:

$Y_k = \sum_m x_m\sum_n h_{(n - m) \bmod N}\,e^{-i2\pi kn/N}$.

In the inner sum put $j = (n - m) \bmod N$. As $n$ runs over a full period, so does $j$, and because $e^{-i2\pi k\cdot/N}$ has period $N$, $e^{-i2\pi kn/N} = e^{-i2\pi km/N}e^{-i2\pi kj/N}$. So the inner sum is $e^{-i2\pi km/N}\sum_j h_je^{-i2\pi kj/N} = e^{-i2\pi km/N}H_k$.

Then $Y_k = H_k\sum_m x_me^{-i2\pi km/N} = H_kX_k$. Ordinary (non-circular) convolution can be computed the same way after padding both sequences with zeros to at least the length of the full result, which is how fast convolution works.

</Prove>

<Step n={2} title="The membrane as a low-pass filter" minutes={25} />

For $\tau\dot{V} = -V + RI$, try a complex sinusoidal input $RI = e^{i\omega t}$ and a response of the same form, $V = H\,e^{i\omega t}$. Substituting, $i\omega\tau H = -H + 1$, so

$$
H(\omega) = \frac{1}{1 + i\omega\tau}, \qquad |H| = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + (\omega\tau)^2}}, \qquad \arg H = -\arctan(\omega\tau)
$$

Below the **cut-off** frequency $f_c = 1/(2\pi\tau)$ (8 Hz for $\tau$ = 20 ms) the gain is close to 1; above it, the gain falls in proportion to $1/f$ and the output lags by up to a quarter cycle. This is the Fourier transform of Week 10's exponential kernel.

At 2 Hz the membrane passes almost the whole input; at 100 Hz the oscillation is about 12 times smaller. That answers the prediction. Neurons are low-pass filters of their input currents, which is one reason fast gamma-band input is attenuated unless it is strong or synchronised.

<Step n={3} title="Band-pass filters" minutes={25} />

To isolate a rhythm, keep only a band of frequencies. The simplest band-pass filter multiplies the DFT by a mask that is 1 inside the band and 0 outside, then transforms back. Sharp edges in the frequency domain are themselves a Gibbs problem: the filter's impulse response becomes long and oscillatory, so the filtered signal rings, and any burst of activity is smeared in time. Smooth edges trade a less sharp band for a shorter, better-behaved filter. Filtering also makes *anything* in the band look like an oscillation: white noise filtered at 6 to 10 Hz looks exactly like a theta rhythm, so filtering cannot tell you whether a rhythm is present. Check the power spectrum (Day 4) first.

<Step n={4} title="Phase and spike locking" minutes={30} />

To ask *when* within a rhythm something happens, you need the **instantaneous phase** at every moment. The **analytic signal** $z(t) = x(t) + i\,\mathcal{H}[x](t)$ pairs each sinusoidal component with its quarter-cycle-shifted partner, so that a cosine $\cos\omega t$ becomes the rotating phasor $e^{i\omega t}$. It is computed with the DFT by deleting the negative frequencies and doubling the positive ones. Its angle is the instantaneous phase and its magnitude the instantaneous amplitude (the envelope).

For a neuron's spikes, collect the phase $\phi_j$ of the rhythm at each spike. If spikes prefer a phase, the unit vectors $e^{i\phi_j}$ point mostly one way. Their average,

$$
\bar{z} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_j e^{i\phi_j}
$$

has length $R$ between 0 (no locking) and 1 (perfect locking) and angle equal to the preferred phase. O'Keefe and Recce (1993), today's paper, used theta phase measured this way to discover that hippocampal place cells fire at progressively earlier phases as an animal crosses each place field.

The spikes cluster near the trough, and the locking disappears when each spike is jittered independently by up to a cycle, which keeps the number of spikes and their rough timing but destroys their relation to the rhythm. A control like this is much better than comparing $R$ with zero, because a finite number of spikes always gives some $R$ by chance. (Shifting the whole spike train by one random offset would not work here: this simulated theta is perfectly regular, so a shift just moves every spike to a new phase together. With real, irregular rhythms a large circular shift is the standard control.)
