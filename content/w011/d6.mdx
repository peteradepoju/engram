**The recording.** Twenty seconds of local field potential from rat hippocampus, sampled at 1 kHz, while the animal runs laps of a track, one lap every 4 s. The LFP has a 1/f² background, a theta rhythm whose frequency wanders between about 6 and 10 Hz, and gamma activity at 60 Hz whose strength waxes and wanes. A place cell with a field in the middle of the track was recorded at the same time. Run the cell to make the data and the tools from this week.

<PyCell code={String.raw`
  rng = np.random.default_rng(1993)
  fs, T = 1000, 20
  N = fs * T
  t = np.arange(N) / fs

  def aperiodic(n, chi):
      X = np.fft.rfft(rng.normal(size=n)); f = np.fft.rfftfreq(n, 1 / fs)
      s = np.zeros_like(f); s[1:] = f[1:] ** (-chi / 2)
      y = np.fft.irfft(X * s, n); return y / y.std()

  _f_inst = 8 + np.convolve(rng.normal(size=N), np.ones(500) / 500, "same") * 15      # wandering theta frequency
  _theta_phase = np.angle(np.exp(1j * np.cumsum(2 * np.pi * _f_inst / fs)))
  _gamma = 0.25 * (1 + 0.8 * np.cos(_theta_phase)) * np.cos(2 * np.pi * 60 * t)
  lfp = np.cos(_theta_phase) + _gamma + 0.8 * aperiodic(N, 2.0)

  position = (t % 4) / 4                                   # 0 to 1 along the track on each lap
  _field = np.exp(-(position - 0.5) ** 2 / (2 * 0.12 ** 2))
  _through = np.clip((position - 0.2) / 0.6, 0, 1)
  _pref = 2 * np.pi * (0.9 - 0.7 * _through)               # hidden: phase falls by 0.7 cycles across the field
  spikes = np.nonzero(rng.random(N) < 0.04 * _field * np.exp(2 * np.cos(_theta_phase - _pref)) / 2.3)[0]

  def welch(x, fs, nperseg):
      w = np.hanning(nperseg); out = []
      for s in range(0, len(x) - nperseg + 1, nperseg // 2):
          seg = x[s:s + nperseg] - x[s:s + nperseg].mean()
          p = np.abs(np.fft.rfft(w * seg)) ** 2 / (fs * np.sum(w ** 2)); p[1:-1] *= 2; out.append(p)
      return np.fft.rfftfreq(nperseg, 1 / fs), np.mean(out, axis=0)

  def bandpass(x, fs, lo, hi, edge=1.0):
      X = np.fft.rfft(x); f = np.fft.rfftfreq(len(x), 1 / fs)
      d = np.maximum(lo - f, 0) + np.maximum(f - hi, 0)
      return np.fft.irfft(X * np.exp(-d ** 2 / (2 * edge ** 2)), len(x))

  def analytic_signal(x):
      X = np.fft.fft(x); h = np.zeros(len(x)); h[0] = 1; h[1:len(x) // 2] = 2
      if len(x) % 2 == 0: h[len(x) // 2] = 1
      return np.fft.ifft(X * h)

  print(len(spikes), "spikes from the place cell")
  plt.figure(figsize=(7, 2.5)); plt.plot(t[:2000], lfp[:2000], lw=0.7); plt.xlabel("time (s)"); plt.title("two seconds of LFP"); plt.show()

**Part B: does gamma ride on theta?** Phase-amplitude coupling asks whether the amplitude of a fast rhythm depends on the phase of a slow one. A standard measure, the **modulation index** of Tort and colleagues (2010), bins theta phase into 18 bins, averages the gamma envelope in each, normalises the averages into a distribution $P$ over bins, and measures how far it is from uniform: $\text{MI} = (\ln 18 - H(P))/\ln 18$, where $H(P) = -\sum P\ln P$ is the entropy (Week 20). MI is 0 for a flat distribution and grows as gamma concentrates at particular phases.

Gamma is strongest at the peak of theta, and the real coupling is far above every shifted control. Here the circular shift works as a control because the theta rhythm wanders in frequency, so shifting the envelope by a second or more breaks its relation to theta phase. Phase-amplitude coupling is easy to produce artefactually (sharp, non-sinusoidal waveforms create harmonics that mimic it), so real studies check waveform shape too.

The spikes start late in the theta cycle as the animal enters the field and move earlier as it crosses, by about 0.7 of a cycle in total, O'Keefe and Recce's phase precession. Plotting the phases twice, stacked, is the standard way to show a circular variable without the wrap-around hiding the trend. Ordinary linear regression would fail here, because phase wraps from 360° back to 0°; the circular fit is immune to where the wrap falls.

<Prove title="1. Why the FFT works" task="For even N, split the DFT sum $X_k = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x_ne^{-i2\pi kn/N}$ into even and odd n, and show that $X_k = E_k + w_kO_k$ and $X_{k + N/2} = E_k - w_kO_k$, where E and O are the DFTs of length N/2 of the even and odd samples and $w_k = e^{-i2\pi k/N}$. Count the operations.">

Write $n = 2m$ for even and $n = 2m + 1$ for odd indices:

$X_k = \sum_m x_{2m}e^{-i2\pi k(2m)/N} + \sum_m x_{2m+1}e^{-i2\pi k(2m + 1)/N} = \sum_m x_{2m}e^{-i2\pi km/(N/2)} + w_k\sum_m x_{2m+1}e^{-i2\pi km/(N/2)} = E_k + w_kO_k$.

E and O have period N/2 in k, and $w_{k + N/2} = e^{-i\pi}w_k = -w_k$, so $X_{k + N/2} = E_k - w_kO_k$.

Each level of splitting costs about N operations to combine, and halving N repeatedly takes $\log_2N$ levels, giving about $N\log_2N$ in total instead of $N^2$.

</Prove>

<Prove title="2. The membrane at its cut-off" task="For $H(\omega) = 1/(1 + i\omega\tau)$, show that at the cut-off $\omega = 1/\tau$ the gain is $1/\sqrt{2}$ and the phase lag is 45°, and that far above it the gain falls by a factor of 2 for every doubling of frequency.">

At $\omega\tau = 1$, $H = 1/(1 + i)$, with $|1 + i| = \sqrt{2}$ and angle 45°, so $|H| = 1/\sqrt{2}$ and $\arg H = -45°$. The power gain $|H|^2$ is one half, which is why the cut-off is also called the half-power point.

For $\omega\tau \gg 1$, $|H| \approx 1/(\omega\tau)$: doubling $\omega$ halves the gain, and the lag approaches 90°. On log-log axes the gain curve is flat below the cut-off and a line of slope −1 above it.

</Prove>
