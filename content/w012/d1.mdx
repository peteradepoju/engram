<Step n={1} title="Loss functions" minutes={25} />

An **optimisation problem** has parameters $\boldsymbol\theta$ and an objective, the **loss** $L(\boldsymbol\theta)$, to be minimised:

$$
\boldsymbol\theta^* = \arg\min_{\boldsymbol\theta} L(\boldsymbol\theta)
$$

For fitting a model $f(x;\boldsymbol\theta)$ to data $(x_t, y_t)$, the two standard losses come from assumptions about the noise.

- **Mean squared error**, $L = \frac{1}{T}\sum_t(y_t - f(x_t;\boldsymbol\theta))^2$. Minimising it is the same as maximising the likelihood if the noise is Gaussian with constant variance (Week 16).
- **Negative log-likelihood**, $L = -\frac{1}{T}\sum_t\log p(y_t \mid x_t, \boldsymbol\theta)$. For spike counts with a Poisson model of rate $\lambda_t$, this is $\frac{1}{T}\sum_t(\lambda_t - y_t\log\lambda_t)$ plus a constant (Week 9's GLM). It weights errors according to the variance a Poisson neuron really has: large where the rate is high, small where it is low.

The choice of loss is a modelling decision: it says what kinds of error you care about and what noise you believe in.

<Step n={2} title="Landscapes and minima" minutes={20} />

The loss as a function of the parameters is a **landscape**. A **global minimum** is the lowest point anywhere; a **local minimum** is lower than everything nearby but not necessarily lowest overall; a **plateau** is a flat region where the slope gives no guidance; and in many dimensions there are also saddles (Week 9). A **local** method, which only looks at the slope where it stands, can stop in any local minimum.

For one or two parameters you can simply evaluate the loss on a grid and look. With $d$ parameters and 20 grid values each, that is $20^d$ evaluations: 400 for two, eight thousand for three, and more than $10^{13}$ for ten. A network with a million weights is out of the question. Li and colleagues (2018), today's paper, showed how to take honest two-dimensional slices through such enormous landscapes, and found that architectural choices change them from chaotic to smooth.

The landscape has a deep valley at 7.3 Hz surrounded by many shallow dips, about one per hertz. Each is a frequency where a sinusoid happens to line up with a stretch of the data, spaced by the recording's frequency resolution of 1/T = 1 Hz (Week 11). Only the searches that started next to the true frequency found it. That answers the prediction. The remedy is to use what you know about the problem: here, look at the power spectrum first and start the search at its peak, which is exactly what spectral analysis is for.

<Prove title="Squared error is Gaussian likelihood" task="Suppose $y_t = f(x_t;\boldsymbol\theta) + \epsilon_t$ with independent Gaussian noise of mean 0 and fixed variance $\sigma^2$. Show that maximising the likelihood of the data over $\boldsymbol\theta$ is the same as minimising the sum of squared errors.">

Each $y_t$ has density $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\exp\left(-\frac{(y_t - f(x_t;\boldsymbol\theta))^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)$. By independence the likelihood is the product, so the log-likelihood is

$\ell(\boldsymbol\theta) = -\frac{T}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^2) - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_t(y_t - f(x_t;\boldsymbol\theta))^2$.

The first term does not depend on $\boldsymbol\theta$, and $\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}$ is a positive constant. So maximising $\ell$ means minimising $\sum_t(y_t - f(x_t;\boldsymbol\theta))^2$. Least squares is maximum likelihood under constant-variance Gaussian noise, which is why it is the wrong choice for spike counts, whose variance grows with their mean.

<Key title="Before you go">
<ul>
<li>Fitting is optimisation: choose parameters to minimise a loss.</li>
<li>Squared error assumes Gaussian noise; Poisson likelihood suits spike counts.</li>
<li>Grid search is hopeless in many dimensions; local search follows the slope and can be trapped.</li>
<li>Knowing the problem, as with starting a frequency fit at the spectral peak, beats blind search.</li>
</ul>
