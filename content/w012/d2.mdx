<Step n={1} title="The algorithm" minutes={20} />

Starting from $\boldsymbol\theta_0$, **gradient descent** repeats

$$
\boldsymbol\theta_{k+1} = \boldsymbol\theta_k - \eta\,\nabla L(\boldsymbol\theta_k)
$$

with **learning rate** (step size) $\eta \gt 0$. The gradient points uphill (Week 8), so each step goes downhill. By the tangent-plane approximation, for small $\eta$ the loss falls by about $\eta\lVert\nabla L\rVert^2$, so it decreases as long as the gradient is not zero and the step is small enough. It stops making progress where the gradient vanishes: at a minimum, a saddle or a plateau. In practice you stop after a fixed number of steps, or when the gradient or the change in loss falls below a tolerance.

<Step n={2} title="Convergence on a quadratic" minutes={30} />

Near any smooth minimum the loss is approximately quadratic (Week 9's second-order Taylor expansion), so the quadratic tells you how gradient descent behaves at the end of every run.

<Derivation title="Gradient descent on a quadratic, mode by mode">
  <div>

Take $L(\mathbf{w}) = \frac{1}{2}\mathbf{w}^\top H\mathbf{w}$ with symmetric positive definite $H$, whose eigenvalues run from $\mu$ (smallest) to $\Lambda$ (largest). The gradient is $H\mathbf{w}$, so a step is $\mathbf{w}_{k+1} = (I - \eta H)\mathbf{w}_k$.

  </div>
  <div>

In the eigenvector basis of $H$ (Week 6) each component evolves on its own: the component along eigenvector $i$ is multiplied by $1 - \eta\lambda_i$ every step.

  </div>
  <div>

Every component shrinks if and only if $|1 - \eta\lambda_i| \lt 1$ for all $i$, that is

$$
0 \lt \eta \lt \frac{2}{\Lambda}
$$

  </div>
  <div>

The slowest component sets the speed. The worst factor, $\max_i|1 - \eta\lambda_i|$, is smallest when the two extremes balance, $1 - \eta\mu = -(1 - \eta\Lambda)$, giving $\eta^* = \frac{2}{\Lambda + \mu}$ and a contraction per step of

$$
\rho^* = \frac{\Lambda - \mu}{\Lambda + \mu} = \frac{\kappa - 1}{\kappa + 1}, \qquad \kappa = \frac{\Lambda}{\mu}
$$

  </div>
</Derivation>

So the error shrinks geometrically, by a factor $\rho$ per step (called **linear convergence**), and the number of steps needed to gain a factor of 10 grows roughly in proportion to the condition number $\kappa$. For $\kappa = 10$, $\rho^* = 9/11 \approx 0.82$: each step removes only about 18% of the remaining error. That answers the prediction.

<Step n={3} title="Choosing the step size" minutes={25} />

In real problems you do not know $\Lambda$ and $\mu$. Practical strategies:

- **Try a sweep** of learning rates spaced by factors of 3 to 10, and keep the largest that reliably decreases the loss. The best is usually close to the largest stable one.
- **Normalise the inputs** (z-score each feature, Week 3): correlated or badly scaled inputs are the commonest cause of a large condition number, as the loss-surface figure of Week 9 showed.
- **Line search**: at each step, try a step and halve it until the loss decreases enough (backtracking). Robust, but each step costs several loss evaluations.
- **Use curvature**: Newton's method (Week 9) rescales by the Hessian and removes the dependence on $\kappa$; the adaptive methods of next week approximate this cheaply.

Bottou, Curtis and Nocedal (2018), this week's deep read, give the general version of today's analysis: for a loss that is $\mu$-strongly convex and has $\Lambda$-Lipschitz gradient, gradient descent with step $1/\Lambda$ converges linearly at rate $1 - \mu/\Lambda$, and without strong convexity the guarantee weakens to the loss gap shrinking like $1/k$.

<Step n={4} title="Fitting a tuning curve" minutes={30} />

Gradient descent works on any differentiable loss, not only quadratics. Fit all four parameters of the von Mises tuning curve from Day 1 (preferred direction, width, baseline, amplitude) by minimising the Poisson negative log-likelihood. Width, baseline and amplitude must be positive, so optimise their logarithms: a common trick that turns a constrained problem into an unconstrained one.

<PyCell code={String.raw`
  rng = np.random.default_rng(12)
  theta = np.repeat(np.linspace(0, 2 * np.pi, 16, endpoint=False), 10)
  def von_mises(theta, mu, kappa, b, A):
      return b + A * np.exp(kappa * (np.cos(theta - mu) - 1))
  counts = rng.poisson(von_mises(theta, 1.0, 2.0, 5.0, 30.0))

  def nll(p):
      mu, log_k, log_b, log_A = p
      lam = von_mises(theta, mu, np.exp(log_k), np.exp(log_b), np.exp(log_A))
      return np.mean(lam - counts * np.log(lam))

  def numerical_grad(f, p, h=1e-6):
      g = np.zeros_like(p)
      for i in range(len(p)):
          e = np.zeros_like(p); e[i] = h
          g[i] = (f(p + e) - f(p - e)) / (2 * h)
      return g
  print("NLL at a rough guess:", round(nll(np.array([0.0, 0.0, 1.0, 2.0])), 3))

From a poor start, four thousand small steps recover all four parameters. Notice how the loss falls quickly at first and then slowly: the early steps fix the parameters the loss is most sensitive to (high curvature), and the late steps creep along the flat directions (low curvature), the condition-number story again. In practice you would supply the analytic gradient (Week 9) rather than a numerical one, and use a better optimiser (Week 13).

<Prove title="Descent on a one-dimensional quadratic" task="For $L(w) = \frac{\lambda}{2}w^2$ with $\lambda \gt 0$, show that gradient descent gives $w_k = (1 - \eta\lambda)^kw_0$. For which $\eta$ does it converge, when does it oscillate, and which $\eta$ is best?">

The gradient is $\lambda w$, so $w_{k+1} = w_k - \eta\lambda w_k = (1 - \eta\lambda)w_k$, and by induction $w_k = (1 - \eta\lambda)^kw_0$.

It converges exactly when $|1 - \eta\lambda| \lt 1$, that is $0 \lt \eta \lt 2/\lambda$. For $\eta \lt 1/\lambda$ the factor is positive and $w$ approaches 0 from one side; for $1/\lambda \lt \eta \lt 2/\lambda$ it is negative and $w$ alternates in sign while shrinking (oscillation); beyond $2/\lambda$ it grows.

The best is $\eta = 1/\lambda$, which makes the factor zero: one step lands exactly on the minimum. That is Newton's method in one dimension (Week 9), which uses the curvature to pick the step.

</Prove>

<Key title="Before you go">
<ul>
<li>Gradient descent steps against the gradient with a learning rate η.</li>
<li>On a quadratic, each eigen-direction shrinks by $1 - \eta\lambda_i$: stable only for $\eta \lt 2/\Lambda$.</li>
<li>The best rate is $(\kappa - 1)/(\kappa + 1)$ per step, so ill-conditioned problems are slow.</li>
<li>Gradient descent fits nonlinear models too; reparameterise to handle positivity.</li>
</ul>
