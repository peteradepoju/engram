<Step n={1} title="The definition" minutes={25} />

A function $f$ is **convex** if the chord between any two points on its graph lies on or above the graph:

$$
f\left((1 - t)\mathbf{a} + t\mathbf{b}\right) \leq (1 - t)f(\mathbf{a}) + tf(\mathbf{b}) \quad\text{for all } \mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b} \text{ and } 0 \leq t \leq 1
$$

It is **strictly convex** if the inequality is strict whenever $\mathbf{a} \neq \mathbf{b}$ and $0 \lt t \lt 1$, and **strongly convex** if it curves up at least as fast as some quadratic $\frac{\mu}{2}\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert^2$. Convex functions include $x^2$, $e^x$, $|x|$, softplus and every linear function; $\sin x$ and the double well are not.

<Prove title="For convex functions, local means global" task="Suppose f is convex and $\mathbf{w}^*$ is a local minimum: $f(\mathbf{w}^*) \leq f(\mathbf{w})$ for all $\mathbf{w}$ within some distance r of $\mathbf{w}^*$. Show that $f(\mathbf{w}^*) \leq f(\mathbf{b})$ for every $\mathbf{b}$, however far away.">

Suppose instead that some $\mathbf{b}$ had $f(\mathbf{b}) \lt f(\mathbf{w}^*)$. Take a point on the segment towards it, $\mathbf{w}_t = (1 - t)\mathbf{w}^* + t\mathbf{b}$, with $t \gt 0$ small enough that $\mathbf{w}_t$ is within distance r of $\mathbf{w}^*$.

By convexity, $f(\mathbf{w}_t) \leq (1 - t)f(\mathbf{w}^*) + tf(\mathbf{b}) \lt (1 - t)f(\mathbf{w}^*) + tf(\mathbf{w}^*) = f(\mathbf{w}^*)$.

So a point arbitrarily close to $\mathbf{w}^*$ has a lower value, contradicting that $\mathbf{w}^*$ is a local minimum. Hence no such $\mathbf{b}$ exists. For a convex loss there are no false valleys, and any method that only goes downhill, such as gradient descent, cannot get trapped. If f is strictly convex the minimum is also unique.

</Prove>

<Step n={2} title="Three tests" minutes={25} />

For smooth functions, three conditions are equivalent ways to say "convex":

1. **Chords**: the definition above.
2. **Tangents**: the tangent plane at every point lies on or below the graph, $f(\mathbf{b}) \geq f(\mathbf{a}) + \nabla f(\mathbf{a})\cdot(\mathbf{b} - \mathbf{a})$. A consequence: wherever $\nabla f = \mathbf{0}$, the function is at its global minimum.
3. **Curvature**: the Hessian is positive semidefinite everywhere, all its eigenvalues at least zero (Week 9). In one dimension, $f'' \geq 0$.

Convexity is also preserved by useful operations: a sum of convex functions is convex, a convex function of a linear (affine) function of $\mathbf{w}$ is convex, and the maximum of convex functions is convex. So a loss built as a sum, over data points, of convex functions of $\mathbf{x}_t\cdot\mathbf{w}$ is convex, which covers most of the statistical models in this course.

<Step n={3} title="Convex losses in neuroscience" minutes={30} />

The Hessian test is the practical one. You derived three Hessians in Week 9:

- least squares: $X^\top X/T$, positive semidefinite because $\mathbf{v}^\top X^\top X\mathbf{v} = \lVert X\mathbf{v}\rVert^2 \geq 0$;
- logistic regression: $Z^\top\operatorname{diag}(p(1 - p))Z/T$, a weighted version of the same;
- Poisson GLM (negative log-likelihood): $X^\top\operatorname{diag}(\boldsymbol\lambda)X$, the same again.

Adding a ridge penalty adds $\lambda I$ to each, making them strongly convex with a unique minimum. Paninski (2004), today's paper, proved the general result behind the success of encoding models: for a linear filter followed by any nonlinearity that is convex and log-concave (the exponential, softplus and others), the point-process likelihood is concave in the filter, so maximum likelihood has no local optima.

At every point the Hessians have no negative eigenvalues. Convexity can be lost, though, in ways that matter in practice: fitting the preferred direction or width of a tuning curve inside a nonlinear function (Day 1's frequency problem) breaks it, which is why encoding models are usually written so that the unknowns enter linearly before the nonlinearity.

<Step n={4} title="Why networks are not convex" minutes={25} />

A network with hidden units has a built-in symmetry. Swap two hidden units, exchanging their incoming weights and their outgoing weights together, and the network computes exactly the same function, so the loss is unchanged. If the loss has a minimum at one setting $\mathbf{w}_A$, it has an equally good one at the swapped setting $\mathbf{w}_B$. If the loss were convex, the midpoint $\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{w}_A + \mathbf{w}_B)$ would be at least as good. But at the midpoint the two hidden units have identical weights and so compute the same thing, wasting half the network.

The two settings fit equally well; the midpoint is much worse, because its two hidden units have become clones. That answers the prediction, and it proves the loss is not convex: a convex function cannot be higher at the midpoint than at both ends. With $n$ hidden units in a layer there are $n!$ equivalent minima created by permutations alone, so network training cannot have a unique answer. Remarkably, it does not need one: many different minima generalise equally well.

<Key title="Before you go">
<ul>
<li>Convex functions have chords above, tangents below and positive semidefinite Hessians.</li>
<li>For convex losses every local minimum is global, so gradient descent finds the best fit.</li>
<li>Least squares, logistic and Poisson GLM losses are convex; ridge makes them strongly convex.</li>
<li>Network losses are not convex: hidden-unit symmetry gives equal minima with worse points between.</li>
