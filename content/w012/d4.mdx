<Step n={1} title="Noisy gradients" minutes={25} />

Most losses are averages over data points, $L(\mathbf{w}) = \frac{1}{T}\sum_t\ell_t(\mathbf{w})$, so the gradient is an average too. A **minibatch** $\mathcal{B}$ of $B$ examples chosen at random gives the estimate

$$
\mathbf{g}_{\mathcal{B}} = \frac{1}{B}\sum_{t \in \mathcal{B}}\nabla\ell_t(\mathbf{w})
$$

and **stochastic gradient descent** (SGD) steps along it: $\mathbf{w} \leftarrow \mathbf{w} - \eta\,\mathbf{g}_{\mathcal{B}}$. Each step costs $B$ gradient evaluations instead of $T$.

<Prove title="The minibatch gradient is unbiased, with variance proportional to 1/B" task="If the B examples are drawn independently and uniformly at random from the T data points, show that $\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{g}_{\mathcal{B}}] = \nabla L$ and that the variance of each component is $\sigma^2/B$, where $\sigma^2$ is the variance of that component of $\nabla\ell_t$ over data points.">

Each drawn example's gradient $\nabla\ell_{t_j}$ is a random vector whose expectation is the average over all data points, $\frac{1}{T}\sum_t\nabla\ell_t = \nabla L$. The minibatch gradient is the average of $B$ such vectors, and the expectation of an average is the average of the expectations, so $\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{g}_{\mathcal{B}}] = \nabla L$: on average SGD goes in the right direction.

The $B$ draws are independent, so for each component the variance of their sum is $B\sigma^2$, and dividing the sum by $B$ divides the variance by $B^2$, giving $\sigma^2/B$ (Week 15). Doubling the batch halves the variance but doubles the cost: the standard deviation of the noise only falls as $1/\sqrt{B}$.

</Prove>

<Step n={2} title="Implementing SGD" minutes={25} />

In practice the data are shuffled once per **epoch** (one pass through the dataset), then split into consecutive minibatches, so every example is used exactly once per epoch. This is called sampling without replacement and usually works a little better than drawing each batch independently.

  import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

Five exact gradient steps barely start the descent, while thousands of cheap noisy steps reach a loss thousands of times closer to the best. That answers the prediction: option B wins easily. Single-example steps (batch 1) are noisier than batches of 10 to 100, so they settle at a higher floor. In practice the best batch is set by hardware: GPUs process a batch of 100 almost as fast as a batch of 1, so moderate batches give most of the speed with less noise.

<Step n={4} title="The noise floor and learning-rate decay" minutes={25} />

Even at the exact minimum, individual examples' gradients are not zero; only their average is. So constant-step SGD never settles: it keeps being kicked around the minimum, and the size of the kicks, and of the resulting **noise floor**, is proportional to $\eta$ (and to $1/B$). Robbins and Monro (1951), today's paper, showed that SGD converges exactly if the step sizes $\eta_k$ satisfy

$$
\sum_k\eta_k = \infty, \qquad \sum_k\eta_k^2 \lt \infty
$$

The first condition lets the iterates travel as far as needed; the second makes the accumulated noise finite, so it averages out. Steps proportional to $1/k$ satisfy both. In deep learning, schedules that decay the learning rate during training (step decay, cosine decay) serve the same purpose.

With a constant step, SGD soon stops improving and jitters at a floor that rises about threefold when the learning rate doubles. Shrinking the step as learning proceeds lets the noise average away, and the excess loss falls hundreds of times further. A large step early on and a small step later is the general recipe.
