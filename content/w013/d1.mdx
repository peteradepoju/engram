<Step n={1} title="The heavy ball" minutes={25} />

Polyak's **heavy-ball** method (1964), today's paper, keeps a velocity $\mathbf{v}$ and updates

$$
\mathbf{v}_{k+1} = \beta\,\mathbf{v}_k - \eta\,\nabla L(\mathbf{w}_k), \qquad \mathbf{w}_{k+1} = \mathbf{w}_k + \mathbf{v}_{k+1}
$$

with **momentum** $0 \leq \beta \lt 1$. With $\beta = 0$ it is gradient descent. Think of a ball rolling on the loss surface with friction: $-\eta\nabla L$ is the push of the slope and $\beta$ is how much of its speed survives each step (1 − β is the friction). A common variant, Nesterov momentum, evaluates the gradient at the look-ahead point $\mathbf{w}_k + \beta\mathbf{v}_k$ and is slightly more stable.

<Step n={2} title="Momentum as averaging" minutes={20} />

Unrolling the velocity update shows what momentum does:

$$
\mathbf{v}_{k+1} = -\eta\left(\nabla L_k + \beta\nabla L_{k-1} + \beta^2\nabla L_{k-2} + \dots\right)
$$

The step is an **exponentially weighted sum of past gradients**, with weights $1, \beta, \beta^2, \dots$ that fade over about $1/(1 - \beta)$ steps. Two consequences follow:

- Along a direction where the gradient is steady, the sum approaches $\frac{1}{1 - \beta}$ times one gradient: the effective step grows to $\eta/(1 - \beta)$, ten times larger for $\beta = 0.9$.
- Along a direction where the gradient alternates in sign (zigzagging across a valley), the terms cancel and the effective step shrinks.

So momentum acts like a large step where the landscape is consistent and a small step where it is not: exactly what a long valley needs. It also averages away some of the noise in stochastic gradients, like the low-pass filter of Week 11.

<Step n={3} title="How much faster?" minutes={35} />

On a quadratic with curvatures between $\mu$ and $\Lambda$ (condition number $\kappa = \Lambda/\mu$), Polyak showed that the best choices

$$
\eta^* = \frac{4}{(\sqrt{\Lambda} + \sqrt{\mu})^2}, \qquad \beta^* = \left(\frac{\sqrt{\kappa} - 1}{\sqrt{\kappa} + 1}\right)^2
$$

give an error that shrinks per step by about $\frac{\sqrt{\kappa} - 1}{\sqrt{\kappa} + 1}$, compared with $\frac{\kappa - 1}{\kappa + 1}$ for gradient descent (Week 12). The number of steps now grows like $\sqrt{\kappa}$ instead of $\kappa$: for $\kappa = 100$, ten times fewer.

<Step n={4} title="Momentum in practice" minutes={25} />

Real losses are not quadratic and you do not know $\Lambda$ and $\mu$, so in practice $\beta$ is simply set to 0.9 (sometimes 0.99), and the learning rate is reduced to compensate for the larger effective step. With stochastic gradients, momentum also smooths the noise. A side effect is **overshoot**: a fast ball can roll past the minimum and up the other side, so the loss is not guaranteed to fall every step.

Momentum gets further. You might expect the same learning rate, now multiplied up to ten times by momentum, to be unstable along the steep direction (the shared offset of the raw counts). It is not, because along that direction the gradient keeps changing sign and momentum's averaging cancels it, while along the shallow directions the full speed-up applies. Notice the wobble early on, the ball overshooting before it settles. (Try scaling the learning rate down by 1 − β instead: the gain mostly disappears, which shows the speed-up comes from the larger effective step in the shallow directions.)

<Prove title="The steady velocity" task="Suppose the gradient is a constant vector $\mathbf{g}$. Show that the heavy-ball velocity converges to $-\frac{\eta}{1 - \beta}\mathbf{g}$, and find how many steps it takes to get within $1/e$ of that value.">

The velocity obeys $\mathbf{v}_{k+1} = \beta\mathbf{v}_k - \eta\mathbf{g}$. Its fixed point $\mathbf{v}^*$ satisfies $\mathbf{v}^* = \beta\mathbf{v}^* - \eta\mathbf{g}$, so $\mathbf{v}^* = -\frac{\eta}{1 - \beta}\mathbf{g}$.

The deviation $\mathbf{d}_k = \mathbf{v}_k - \mathbf{v}^*$ obeys $\mathbf{d}_{k+1} = \beta\mathbf{d}_k$, so $\mathbf{d}_k = \beta^k\mathbf{d}_0$: it shrinks geometrically. It falls to $1/e$ of its starting size when $\beta^k = e^{-1}$, that is $k = -1/\ln\beta \approx \frac{1}{1 - \beta}$ for $\beta$ close to 1. So $\beta = 0.9$ remembers about the last 10 gradients and $\beta = 0.99$ about the last 100, and the effective learning rate is $\eta/(1 - \beta)$.

</Prove>

<Key title="Before you go">
<ul>
<li>Momentum keeps a velocity that averages past gradients with weights $\beta^j$.</li>
<li>Consistent directions speed up to an effective step $\eta/(1 - \beta)$; oscillating ones are damped.</li>
<li>On quadratics the steps needed fall from order κ to order √κ.</li>
<li>In practice β = 0.9 with a correspondingly smaller learning rate is the standard choice.</li>
</ul>
