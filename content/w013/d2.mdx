<Step n={1} title="Per-parameter steps" minutes={25} />

Suppose parameter $i$ has typically seen gradients of size $s_i$. Gradient descent moves it by about $\eta s_i$ per step: parameters with big gradients race and ones with small gradients crawl, regardless of how far each is from its best value. The adaptive idea is to divide each parameter's step by an estimate of $s_i$.

- **AdaGrad** (Duchi, Hazan and Singer, 2011) divides by the square root of the *sum* of all past squared gradients, $\sqrt{G_i}$ with $G_i = \sum_k g_{i,k}^2$. Rarely updated parameters keep large steps, which suits sparse features, but the sum only grows, so every step eventually shrinks towards zero.
- **RMSProp** (Hinton, in lecture notes) replaces the sum with an exponential moving average, $v_i \leftarrow \rho v_i + (1 - \rho)g_i^2$, and steps by $-\eta\,g_i/(\sqrt{v_i} + \epsilon)$. The step no longer shrinks for ever; it tracks the recent scale of the gradient.

The small constant $\epsilon$ (for example $10^{-8}$) prevents division by zero.

<Step n={2} title="Adam" minutes={30} />

**Adam** (Kingma and Ba, 2015, today's paper) combines momentum with RMSProp. With $\beta_1 = 0.9$, $\beta_2 = 0.999$ and step counter $k$:

$$
\mathbf{m} \leftarrow \beta_1\mathbf{m} + (1 - \beta_1)\mathbf{g}, \qquad \mathbf{v} \leftarrow \beta_2\mathbf{v} + (1 - \beta_2)\mathbf{g}^2
$$

$$
\hat{\mathbf{m}} = \frac{\mathbf{m}}{1 - \beta_1^k}, \quad \hat{\mathbf{v}} = \frac{\mathbf{v}}{1 - \beta_2^k}, \qquad \mathbf{w} \leftarrow \mathbf{w} - \eta\,\frac{\hat{\mathbf{m}}}{\sqrt{\hat{\mathbf{v}}} + \epsilon}
$$

(squares and square roots taken entry by entry). $\mathbf{m}$ estimates the mean gradient, $\mathbf{v}$ its mean square. Both start at zero, so early on they are biased towards zero; dividing by $1 - \beta^k$ corrects that, which matters most in the first few hundred steps, since $\beta_2 = 0.999$ takes about a thousand steps to forget its initial zero.

  import numpy as np
  import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

  def adam(grad, x0, lr, steps, b1=0.9, b2=0.999, eps=1e-8):
      w = np.array(x0, dtype=float)
      m, v = np.zeros_like(w), np.zeros_like(w)
      history = [w.copy()]
      for k in range(1, steps + 1):
          g = grad(w)
          m = b1 * m + (1 - b1) * g
          v = b2 * v + (1 - b2) * g ** 2
          m_hat = m / (1 - b1 ** k)
          v_hat = v / (1 - b2 ** k)
          w = w - lr * m_hat / (np.sqrt(v_hat) + eps)
          history.append(w.copy())
      return np.array(history)

  print("first step:", adam(lambda w: np.array([1000.0, -0.001, 3.0]), np.zeros(3), 0.01, 1)[1])
`} />

On the first step, $\hat{\mathbf{m}} = \mathbf{g}$ and $\hat{\mathbf{v}} = \mathbf{g}^2$, so every parameter moves by exactly the learning rate, whichever way its gradient points and however large it is. Adam's learning rate is therefore a step size in parameter units, which makes it much easier to choose than a gradient-descent learning rate.

<Step n={3} title="Scale invariance" minutes={25} />

Because Adam divides the averaged gradient by its own root mean square, multiplying the loss by a constant $c$ multiplies both by $c$ and leaves the step unchanged (apart from the tiny $\epsilon$). Gradient descent's step, by contrast, grows by $c$. The same holds per parameter: rescaling one input feature changes its gradient's size but not Adam's step for its weight by much. This is the main practical reason Adam works with default settings on so many problems.

<Prove title="Adam and the sign of the gradient" task="Suppose a parameter's gradient is the same value g at every step. Show that Adam's bias-corrected step is exactly $-\eta\,g/(|g| + \epsilon) \approx -\eta\,\operatorname{sign}(g)$ at every step. What does this suggest Adam does when gradients are noisy?">

With a constant gradient, $m_k = (1 - \beta_1)\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\beta_1^j g = (1 - \beta_1^k)g$, so $\hat{m}_k = g$. Likewise $v_k = (1 - \beta_2^k)g^2$, so $\hat{v}_k = g^2$ and $\sqrt{\hat{v}_k} = |g|$.

The step is therefore $-\eta g/(|g| + \epsilon) \approx -\eta\operatorname{sign}(g)$: a fixed-size step in the direction of descent, whatever the gradient's magnitude.

With noisy gradients of mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma$, $\hat{m}$ estimates $\mu$ while $\sqrt{\hat{v}}$ estimates $\sqrt{\mu^2 + \sigma^2}$. The step is then roughly $\eta\,\mu/\sqrt{\mu^2 + \sigma^2}$: close to $\eta$ when the signal dominates, and much smaller when the noise does. Adam automatically takes cautious steps for parameters whose gradients are mostly noise.

</Prove>

<Key title="Before you go">
<ul>
<li>Adaptive methods divide each parameter's step by the root mean square of its recent gradients.</li>
<li>Adam combines momentum with RMSProp and corrects both averages for their start at zero.</li>
<li>Adam's steps are nearly invariant to the scale of the loss and of individual parameters.</li>
<li>It works well across a wide range of learning rates, which is why it is the default.</li>
</ul>
