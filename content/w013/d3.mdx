<Step n={1} title="The tangency picture" minutes={25} />

Suppose you want to maximise $f(\mathbf{x})$ but only among points that satisfy $g(\mathbf{x}) = 0$. Walk along the constraint curve. As long as the curve cuts across the contours of $f$, walking one way increases $f$, so you are not yet at the best point. You stop only where the constraint curve runs *along* a contour of $f$, touching it without crossing.

At that point the two curves are tangent, so their normals are parallel. The normal to a contour is the gradient, so

$$
\nabla f(\mathbf{x}^*) = \lambda\,\nabla g(\mathbf{x}^*)
$$

for some number $\lambda$, the **Lagrange multiplier**. Together with $g(\mathbf{x}^*) = 0$ this gives as many equations as unknowns.

<Step n={2} title="The Lagrangian, and PCA again" minutes={25} />

The condition is neatly packaged in the **Lagrangian**

$$
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x}, \lambda) = f(\mathbf{x}) - \lambda\,g(\mathbf{x}).
$$

Setting $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\mathbf{x} = 0$ gives $\nabla f = \lambda\nabla g$; setting $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\lambda = 0$ gives back the constraint. A constrained problem in $n$ variables becomes an unconstrained stationary point in $n + 1$.

The multiplier has a meaning: $\lambda = \mathrm{d}f^*/\mathrm{d}c$ when the constraint is $g(\mathbf{x}) = c$. It is the price of the constraint, how much the best value would improve if the limit were loosened by one unit.

<Derivation title="PCA from a Lagrangian">

<div>

Maximise the variance of the projection, $\mathbf{u}^\top\Sigma\mathbf{u}$, subject to $\mathbf{u}^\top\mathbf{u} = 1$. The Lagrangian is

$$
\mathcal{L} = \mathbf{u}^\top\Sigma\mathbf{u} - \lambda(\mathbf{u}^\top\mathbf{u} - 1).
$$

</div>

<div>

The gradient with respect to $\mathbf{u}$ is $2\Sigma\mathbf{u} - 2\lambda\mathbf{u}$. Setting it to zero:

$$
\Sigma\mathbf{u} = \lambda\mathbf{u}.
$$

Every stationary point is an eigenvector, and the multiplier is its eigenvalue.

</div>

<div>

At such a point the objective is $\mathbf{u}^\top\Sigma\mathbf{u} = \lambda\mathbf{u}^\top\mathbf{u} = \lambda$. The maximum is therefore the eigenvector with the largest eigenvalue: the first principal component of Week 7, now derived in three lines.

</div>

</Derivation>

Step then project: each update multiplies $\mathbf{u}$ by $I + 0.2\Sigma$ and rescales, which is exactly the power iteration of Week 6 applied to a shifted matrix. Projecting onto the constraint after every step is a general recipe you will use again tomorrow.

<Step n={3} title="The best unbiased decoder" minutes={30} />

A population of $N$ neurons responds to a stimulus $\theta$ with mean rates $\mathbf{f}(\theta)$ and noise covariance $C$. A linear readout estimates small changes in $\theta$ from $\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{r}$. To be **unbiased**, the readout must move by exactly one unit when $\theta$ moves by one: $\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{f}' = 1$. Among such readouts we want the least noisy, so we minimise the variance $\mathbf{w}^\top C\mathbf{w}$.

<Derivation title="The minimum-variance unbiased linear decoder">

<div>

$$
\mathcal{L} = \mathbf{w}^\top C\mathbf{w} - \lambda(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{f}' - 1).
$$

Setting the gradient to zero, $2C\mathbf{w} = \lambda\mathbf{f}'$, so $\mathbf{w} = \tfrac{\lambda}{2}C^{-1}\mathbf{f}'$.

</div>

<div>

The constraint fixes the scale: $\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\,\mathbf{f}'^\top C^{-1}\mathbf{f}' = 1$, so

$$
\mathbf{w}^* = \frac{C^{-1}\mathbf{f}'}{\mathbf{f}'^\top C^{-1}\mathbf{f}'}, \qquad \operatorname{Var} = \frac{1}{\mathbf{f}'^\top C^{-1}\mathbf{f}'}.
$$

</div>

<div>

The denominator $\mathbf{f}'^\top C^{-1}\mathbf{f}'$ is the linear Fisher information of the population, which you will meet properly in Week 17. The best unbiased linear readout achieves a variance of one over it.

</div>

</Derivation>

Taking the correlations into account cuts the variance by about 16% here. The gain depends on the structure of the noise: correlations along $\mathbf{f}'$ itself, called differential correlations (Moreno-Bote and colleagues, 2014), cannot be removed by any readout and set a hard limit on what a population can encode.

<Step n={4} title="Laughlin's fly" minutes={25} />

In 1981 Simon Laughlin measured the contrasts a blowfly sees in its natural habitat and, separately, the response curve of its large monopolar cells, the neurons just after the photoreceptors. The two matched: the response curve was, to within the scatter of the data, the cumulative distribution of natural contrasts.

The reason is a constrained optimisation. A neuron with a limited range of responses (say a fixed number of distinguishable levels) carries the most information when every level is used equally often, because the uniform distribution has the highest entropy on a bounded range (Week 20). If the response is $r = F(x)$, with $F$ the cumulative distribution of the input, then $r$ is uniformly distributed: this is **histogram equalisation**. The curve is steep where inputs are common, spreading them over many levels, and flat where they are rare. That answers the prediction.

The linear curve wastes its top levels on rare large contrasts and crowds common small ones into a few levels, giving 2.76 bits; the CDF curve reaches the maximum of 3.32 bits. The same idea, applied to whole images and populations, becomes the efficient coding hypothesis (Barlow, 1961) that returns in Weeks 39 and 111.

<Prove title="Maximum entropy on a finite set" task="Use a Lagrange multiplier to show that among probability vectors $(p_1, \dots, p_n)$ with $\sum_i p_i = 1$, the entropy $H = -\sum_i p_i\log p_i$ is stationary only at the uniform distribution.">

The Lagrangian is $\mathcal{L} = -\sum_i p_i\log p_i - \lambda\left(\sum_i p_i - 1\right)$.

Differentiating with respect to $p_i$: $-\log p_i - 1 - \lambda = 0$, so $p_i = e^{-1-\lambda}$, the same for every $i$.

The constraint then gives $n\,e^{-1-\lambda} = 1$, so $p_i = 1/n$. Since $H$ is concave this stationary point is the maximum, with $H = \log n$ (3.32 bits for ten levels, as in the exercise).

</Prove>
