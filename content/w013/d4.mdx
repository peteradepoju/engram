<Step n={1} title="Inequality constraints" minutes={25} />

Minimise $f(\mathbf{x})$ subject to $g_j(\mathbf{x}) \le 0$ for each $j$. At the solution each constraint is either **active** ($g_j = 0$, pressing against its limit) or **inactive** ($g_j \lt 0$, irrelevant locally). An inactive constraint behaves as if it were absent; an active one behaves like yesterday's equality, but with a sign: the constraint may only push the solution back inside, never pull it out.

These ideas are collected in the **Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) conditions**. At a local minimum (under mild regularity conditions) there are multipliers $\mu_j$ with

$$
\nabla f + \sum_j \mu_j\nabla g_j = \mathbf{0}, \qquad \mu_j \ge 0, \qquad \mu_j\,g_j(\mathbf{x}) = 0.
$$

The last condition, **complementary slackness**, says each multiplier is zero unless its constraint is active. For convex problems the KKT conditions are not only necessary but sufficient.

<Callout kind="history">

William Karush derived these conditions in his 1939 master's thesis at Chicago; Harold Kuhn and Albert Tucker published them independently in 1951, and for decades they bore only the later names.

</Callout>

<Step n={2} title="Non-negative weights" minutes={25} />

The simplest inequality is $x_i \ge 0$. Projected gradient descent handles it by taking an ordinary gradient step and then **projecting** back onto the allowed set, which here just means setting negative entries to zero: $\mathbf{x} \leftarrow \max(\mathbf{x} - \eta\nabla f, 0)$. For non-negative least squares, KKT says that at the solution every positive weight has zero gradient and every zero weight has a gradient that is non-negative (pushing it further down would help, but it is not allowed).

<Step n={3} title="Why L1 gives zeros" minutes={25} />

Penalised regression, $\min_\mathbf{w} \tfrac{1}{2}\lVert A\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y}\rVert^2 + \lambda R(\mathbf{w})$, is the Lagrangian of a constrained problem: minimise the error subject to $R(\mathbf{w}) \le c$. With the **L2** penalty $R = \lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert_2^2$ (ridge regression, Week 5) the allowed region is a disc. With the **L1** penalty $R = \lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert_1 = \sum_i |w_i|$ (the lasso; Tibshirani, 1996) it is a diamond, with corners on the axes.

The solution is where the expanding error contours first touch the region. A disc is smooth, so the touching point is almost never on an axis. A diamond's corners stick out, so the contours very often touch first at a corner, where some coordinates are exactly zero.

In one dimension the L1-penalised problem $\min_w \tfrac{1}{2}(w - z)^2 + \tau|w|$ has the closed-form answer

$$
w^* = S_\tau(z) = \operatorname{sign}(z)\max(|z| - \tau, 0),
$$

the **soft-thresholding** operator: shrink towards zero by $\tau$, and set to exactly zero anything smaller than $\tau$.

<Derivation title="Soft thresholding">

<div>

For $w > 0$ the objective is $\tfrac{1}{2}(w - z)^2 + \tau w$, minimised at $w = z - \tau$, which is valid (positive) only if $z > \tau$. Similarly, for $w \lt 0$ the minimum is $w = z + \tau$, valid only if $z \lt -\tau$.

</div>

<div>

If $|z| \le \tau$, neither branch is valid, and the minimum is at the kink $w = 0$. Here the absolute value has no derivative, but every slope between $-\tau$ and $\tau$ is a valid subgradient, and $0 \in (0 - z) + [-\tau, \tau]$ holds exactly when $|z| \le \tau$.

</div>

<div>

Together: $w^* = \operatorname{sign}(z)\max(|z| - \tau, 0)$. The kink in $|w|$ is what makes zero an attractive answer, which a smooth penalty like $w^2$ can never do.

</div>

</Derivation>

<Step n={4} title="ISTA and sparse recovery" minutes={20} />

For many weights, the **iterative shrinkage-thresholding algorithm** (ISTA) alternates a gradient step on the smooth error with soft thresholding:

$$
\mathbf{w} \leftarrow S_{\lambda/L}\!\left(\mathbf{w} - \tfrac{1}{L}A^\top(A\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y})\right),
$$

with $L$ the largest eigenvalue of $A^\top A$. It is projected gradient descent with the projection replaced by the L1 penalty's own proximal step, and it converges for convex problems at the rate of plain gradient descent (a momentum version, FISTA, is faster).

With fewer measurements than unknowns, ISTA finds the five true inputs, with every weight within about 0.04 of its true value (a few others come out tiny but non-zero). The minimum-norm solution spreads the signal over more than half of the inputs and gets individual weights wrong by up to 0.67. That answers the prediction: when the truth is sparse, the L1 penalty can recover it from far fewer measurements than unknowns. This is the foundation of **compressed sensing** (Candès, Romberg and Tao; Donoho, 2006), and of connectivity inference from limited recordings.

<Step n={5} title="Sparse coding in visual cortex" minutes={10} />

Olshausen and Field (1996, this week's deep read) asked what basis functions would let natural image patches be represented with few active units at a time. They minimised reconstruction error plus a sparsity penalty on the coefficients, inferring the coefficients for each patch (much as ISTA does) and slowly adapting the basis. The learned basis functions were localised, oriented and bandpass, strikingly like the receptive fields of simple cells in V1. Sparsity alone, applied to natural images, was enough to produce them.

<Prove title="KKT for non-negative least squares" task="For $\min_{\mathbf{x} \ge 0} f(\mathbf{x})$ with $f$ convex and differentiable, show that the KKT conditions reduce to: $\partial f/\partial x_i = 0$ wherever $x_i > 0$, and $\partial f/\partial x_i \ge 0$ wherever $x_i = 0$.">

Write the constraints as $g_i(\mathbf{x}) = -x_i \le 0$, so $\nabla g_i = -\mathbf{e}_i$.

Stationarity gives $\nabla f - \sum_i\mu_i\mathbf{e}_i = 0$, that is $\partial f/\partial x_i = \mu_i$ for each $i$.

Dual feasibility, $\mu_i \ge 0$, gives $\partial f/\partial x_i \ge 0$ everywhere. Complementary slackness, $\mu_i x_i = 0$, gives $\mu_i = 0$ (and so $\partial f/\partial x_i = 0$) wherever $x_i > 0$. Because $f$ is convex, these conditions are also sufficient for a minimum, which is what the exercise checked.

</Prove>
