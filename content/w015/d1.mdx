<Step n={1} title="The rules of probability" minutes={20} />

An experiment, such as one presentation of a stimulus, has a set of possible **outcomes**, its **sample space** $\Omega$. An **event** is a set of outcomes, such as "the neuron fired at least 5 spikes". A probability assigns to each event $A$ a number $P(A)$ that obeys three rules (Kolmogorov, 1933):

1. $P(A) \ge 0$ for every event.
2. $P(\Omega) = 1$: something happens.
3. If $A$ and $B$ cannot both happen, $P(A \text{ or } B) = P(A) + P(B)$ (and likewise for any countable list of such events).

Everything else follows. The complement rule $P(\text{not } A) = 1 - P(A)$ comes from rules 2 and 3, and for events that can overlap, $P(A \text{ or } B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \text{ and } B)$, since otherwise the overlap is counted twice.

What probability *means* is a separate question. The **frequentist** reading is the long-run fraction of repetitions in which the event happens; the **Bayesian** reading is a degree of belief, which can apply to a single event such as "this neuron is tuned to motion" (Week 16). The rules are the same either way.

<Step n={2} title="Random variables, PMFs and CDFs" minutes={30} />

A **random variable** $X$ is a rule that assigns a number to each outcome: the spike count, the reaction time, the label a network predicts. A **discrete** random variable takes values in a list (counts 0, 1, 2, ...), and is described by its **probability mass function** (PMF)

$$
p(k) = P(X = k), \qquad \sum_k p(k) = 1,
$$

or equivalently by its **cumulative distribution function** (CDF), $F(x) = P(X \le x)$, which rises in steps from 0 to 1. Probabilities of ranges come straight from the CDF: $P(a \lt X \le b) = F(b) - F(a)$. The **quantile** at level $q$ is the smallest $x$ with $F(x) \ge q$; the median is the quantile at 0.5.

Even the most likely count, 4, turns up on only about one trial in five. Getting some other number is four times as likely, which answers the prediction. The mean is a summary of a distribution, not a typical outcome, and a single trial tells you little about it. Tolhurst, Movshon and Dean (1983, today's paper) measured exactly this spread in visual cortex and found it is the rule, not the exception.

<Step n={3} title="Two neurons: joint distributions and independence" minutes={30} />

For two random variables, the **joint PMF** $p(a, b) = P(X = a \text{ and } Y = b)$ gives the probability of every pair of values. Summing over one variable gives the **marginal** distribution of the other:

$$
p_X(a) = \sum_b p(a, b), \qquad p_Y(b) = \sum_a p(a, b).
$$

$X$ and $Y$ are **independent** if $p(a, b) = p_X(a)\,p_Y(b)$ for every pair: knowing one tells you nothing about the other. Neurons recorded together are rarely independent. A common reason is a shared fluctuation, such as the animal's arousal or attention, that scales both neurons' responses up or down together from trial to trial.

With a shared gain both neurons tend to be silent together, or busy together, more often than independence predicts: here both are silent on 0.86% of trials, against 0.32% if they were independent. With a fixed gain the joint table matches the product of its marginals up to sampling noise. Fluctuations shared across a population are now known to be a large part of cortical variability, and you will measure them properly as noise correlations (Week 16) and partition them on Day 3.

<Step n={4} title="The law of large numbers" minutes={25} />

If $X_1, X_2, \dots, X_n$ are independent draws from the same distribution with mean $\mu$, their average $\bar{X}_n$ gets closer to $\mu$ as $n$ grows: this is the **law of large numbers**, the bridge between probability as a rule and probability as a long-run frequency. How close? The typical error, the **standard error**, is $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$, where $\sigma$ is the standard deviation of one draw (tomorrow you will prove it). Quadrupling the data only halves the error.

<Prove title="The inclusion-exclusion rule" task="Using only the three rules of probability, show that $P(A \text{ or } B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \text{ and } B)$.">

Split $A \text{ or } B$ into three pieces that cannot overlap: $A$ without $B$, $B$ without $A$, and $A$ and $B$ together. By rule 3,

$$
P(A \text{ or } B) = P(A \text{ only}) + P(B \text{ only}) + P(A \text{ and } B).
$$

Likewise $A$ splits into "$A$ only" and "$A$ and $B$", so $P(A \text{ only}) = P(A) - P(A \text{ and } B)$, and similarly for $B$.

Substituting gives $P(A) + P(B) - P(A \text{ and } B)$. The subtraction removes the overlap, which would otherwise be counted twice.

</Prove>
