<Step n={1} title="Expectation" minutes={25} />

The **expectation** (mean) of a discrete random variable is the probability-weighted average of its values,

$$
\mathbb{E}[X] = \sum_k k\,p(k),
$$

and more generally $\mathbb{E}[g(X)] = \sum_k g(k)\,p(k)$ for any function $g$. It is where a histogram of many draws would balance.

Its most useful property is **linearity**: for any random variables $X$ and $Y$ and constants $a$ and $b$,

$$
\mathbb{E}[aX + bY] = a\,\mathbb{E}[X] + b\,\mathbb{E}[Y].
$$

This holds whether or not $X$ and $Y$ are independent, which is what makes it so powerful: the expected total spike count of a population is the sum of the neurons' expected counts, however they are correlated.

<Step n={2} title="Variance and sums" minutes={25} />

The **variance** measures spread around the mean:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}\left[(X - \mu)^2\right] = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - \mu^2,
$$

and the **standard deviation** $\sigma = \sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(X)}$ is in the units of $X$. Scaling multiplies the variance by the square, $\operatorname{Var}(aX) = a^2\operatorname{Var}(X)$, and shifting leaves it unchanged. For a sum,

$$
\operatorname{Var}(X + Y) = \operatorname{Var}(X) + \operatorname{Var}(Y) + 2\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y),
$$

where the **covariance** $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = \mathbb{E}[(X - \mu_X)(Y - \mu_Y)]$ is zero for independent variables (though zero covariance does not imply independence).

<Derivation title="The standard error of a mean">

<div>

Let $X_1, \dots, X_n$ be independent, each with variance $\sigma^2$, and $\bar{X} = \tfrac{1}{n}\sum_i X_i$.

</div>

<div>

Independence makes every covariance zero, so the variance of the sum is the sum of the variances, $n\sigma^2$.

</div>

<div>

Dividing by $n$ multiplies the variance by $1/n^2$:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(\bar{X}) = \frac{n\sigma^2}{n^2} = \frac{\sigma^2}{n}, \qquad \operatorname{sd}(\bar{X}) = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}.
$$

If the draws are positively correlated, the covariance terms do not vanish and the error falls more slowly, the reason correlated noise limits the benefit of pooling neurons.

</div>

</Derivation>

<Step n={3} title="Bernoulli and binomial" minutes={20} />

A **Bernoulli** random variable is 1 with probability $p$ and 0 otherwise: one release site that does or does not release. Its mean is $p$ and, since $X^2 = X$, its variance is $p - p^2 = p(1 - p)$.

The **binomial** distribution counts successes in $n$ independent Bernoulli trials:

$$
P(K = k) = \binom{n}{k}p^k(1 - p)^{n - k}, \qquad \mathbb{E}[K] = np, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(K) = np(1 - p).
$$

The mean and variance follow from linearity and independence with no algebra: $K$ is a sum of $n$ Bernoullis. Note that the variance $np(1 - p)$ is largest at $p = \tfrac{1}{2}$ and vanishes at $p = 0$ and $p = 1$, when every trial gives the same answer.

<Step n={4} title="Quantal analysis" minutes={35} />

If each of $n$ sites releases with probability $p$ and each quantum produces a response of size $q$, the response is $V = qK$ with $K$ binomial, so

$$
\mathbb{E}[V] = npq, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(V) = q^2np(1 - p) = q\,\mathbb{E}[V] - \frac{\mathbb{E}[V]^2}{n}.
$$

The last form eliminates $p$. As release probability changes (with calcium, or with plasticity), the points (mean, variance) trace a **parabola** through the origin with initial slope $q$ and curvature $-1/n$. That answers the prediction: the variance rises and then falls, peaking at half the maximum response. Fitting the parabola recovers the unseen quantities $n$ and $q$, a method called **variance-mean analysis** (Clements and Silver, 2000).

The fit recovers both hidden numbers to within about 10%. The estimate of $q$ comes out slightly high, and you can say why: variability in the size of each quantum adds variance, multiplying the slope by $1 + \mathrm{CV}^2$ (here 1.04). Real analyses correct for it.

When $p$ is small and $n$ large, the binomial becomes the Poisson distribution with mean $m = np$ (tomorrow), and the probability of a failure is $e^{-m}$. Del Castillo and Katz used this to estimate the mean number of quanta from the failures alone, $m = -\ln P(\text{failure})$, and checked it against the mean response divided by the size of a spontaneous quantum. The two agreed, which was the decisive evidence for quantal release.

<Prove title="The variance-mean parabola" task="For $V = qK$ with $K$ binomial($n$, $p$), show that $\operatorname{Var}(V) = q\,\mathbb{E}[V] - \mathbb{E}[V]^2/n$, and find the mean response at which the variance is largest.">

From the binomial moments, $\mathbb{E}[V] = qnp$ and $\operatorname{Var}(V) = q^2np(1 - p) = q^2np - q^2np^2$.

The first term is $q\cdot qnp = q\,\mathbb{E}[V]$. The second is $q^2n^2p^2/n = \mathbb{E}[V]^2/n$. Hence $\operatorname{Var}(V) = q\mathbb{E}[V] - \mathbb{E}[V]^2/n$.

As a function of $\mu = \mathbb{E}[V]$, this parabola has derivative $q - 2\mu/n$, which is zero at $\mu = nq/2$: half the maximum response $nq$, reached at $p = \tfrac{1}{2}$.

</Prove>
