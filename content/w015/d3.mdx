<Step n={1} title="The Poisson limit" minutes={25} />

Divide a window into $n$ tiny bins, each holding a spike with probability $p = \lambda/n$, independently. The count is binomial($n$, $\lambda/n$). As the bins shrink ($n \to \infty$ with $\lambda$ fixed), it converges to the **Poisson distribution**

$$
P(K = k) = \frac{\lambda^k e^{-\lambda}}{k!}, \qquad k = 0, 1, 2, \dots
$$

<Derivation title="From binomial to Poisson">

<div>

Write the binomial probability with $p = \lambda/n$:

$$
\binom{n}{k}\left(\frac{\lambda}{n}\right)^k\left(1 - \frac{\lambda}{n}\right)^{n - k} = \frac{\lambda^k}{k!}\cdot\frac{n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)}{n^k}\cdot\left(1 - \frac{\lambda}{n}\right)^{n}\cdot\left(1 - \frac{\lambda}{n}\right)^{-k}.
$$

</div>

<div>

For fixed $k$, as $n \to \infty$: the second factor is a product of $k$ terms each tending to 1; the third tends to $e^{-\lambda}$ (the definition of the exponential, Week 8); the fourth tends to 1.

</div>

<div>

What remains is $\lambda^k e^{-\lambda}/k!$. The binomial mean $np = \lambda$ and variance $np(1 - p) = \lambda(1 - \lambda/n)$ tend to $\lambda$ as well, so a Poisson variable has **mean equal to variance**, $\lambda$.

</div>

</Derivation>

This is why the Poisson distribution turns up wherever many independent opportunities each rarely succeed: vesicles from many sites at low release probability (yesterday), photons absorbed from a dim light, and, roughly, spikes.

<Step n={2} title="Intervals and memorylessness" minutes={25} />

In a **Poisson process** of rate $r$, the count in any window of length $t$ is Poisson($rt$), and counts in non-overlapping windows are independent. The probability of waiting longer than $t$ for the next event is the probability of no events in $t$:

$$
P(\text{wait} > t) = e^{-rt},
$$

so intervals are **exponential** with mean $1/r$ and standard deviation $1/r$, giving a coefficient of variation of 1. The exponential is **memoryless**: $P(\text{wait} > s + t \mid \text{wait} > s) = e^{-r(s+t)}/e^{-rs} = e^{-rt}$. Having waited already tells you nothing; the **hazard**, the instantaneous chance of an event given none so far, is constant at $r$. That answers the prediction: exactly as likely. Real neurons have a refractory period and are not memoryless at short times, which a **gamma** distribution of intervals, a sum of several exponential stages, captures better.

For gamma intervals, a neuron that has waited a while is "due": its hazard rises with time, so a long further wait becomes less likely, and its coefficient of variation is $1/\sqrt{4} = 0.5$, more regular than Poisson.

<Step n={3} title="Superposition" minutes={15} />

If $K_1, \dots, K_m$ are independent Poisson counts with means $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_m$, their sum is Poisson with mean $\sum_i\lambda_i$. Pooling the spikes of many independent Poisson neurons gives a Poisson process with the summed rate, which is why the input to a cortical neuron from thousands of synapses is often modelled as Poisson even if each input is not. (The sum of many independent, sparse, non-Poisson processes also tends towards Poisson, a result due to Palm and Khinchin.)

<Step n={4} title="Over-dispersion" minutes={40} />

Recorded in an awake animal, cortical spike counts usually have Fano factors above 1, and the excess grows with the mean, just as Tolhurst and colleagues saw. A Poisson process cannot do this, but a Poisson process whose rate itself varies from trial to trial can. Let the rate on each trial be $g\,f(\theta)$, where $f(\theta)$ is the tuning curve and $g$ a gain with mean 1 and variance $\sigma_g^2$, shared by all spikes in the trial. By the **law of total variance** (Week 16),

$$
\operatorname{Var}(K) = \underbrace{\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{Var}(K \mid g)]}_{\text{Poisson part, } \mu} + \underbrace{\operatorname{Var}(\mathbb{E}[K \mid g])}_{\text{gain part, } \sigma_g^2\mu^2} = \mu + \sigma_g^2\mu^2.
$$

Goris, Movshon and Simoncelli (2014) fitted this **modulated Poisson** model to neurons in visual cortex and found that gain fluctuations account for much of the excess variability, more so in higher visual areas.

At low rates the counts look Poisson, because the $\mu$ term dominates; at the preferred orientation the gain term takes over and the Fano factor rises towards 3. A single Fano factor for a neuron is therefore not very meaningful: it depends on the stimulus. The model also predicts that gain fluctuations shared between neurons produce correlated variability, the dependence you saw on Day 1.

<Prove title="The mean of a Poisson variable" task="Show directly from the PMF that $\mathbb{E}[K] = \lambda$ for $K \sim$ Poisson($\lambda$). Then show that $\mathbb{E}[K(K-1)] = \lambda^2$ and deduce that $\operatorname{Var}(K) = \lambda$.">

$\mathbb{E}[K] = \sum_{k \ge 0} k\,\frac{\lambda^k e^{-\lambda}}{k!} = \lambda e^{-\lambda}\sum_{k \ge 1}\frac{\lambda^{k-1}}{(k-1)!} = \lambda e^{-\lambda}e^{\lambda} = \lambda$, using the series for $e^\lambda$.

In the same way, $\mathbb{E}[K(K-1)] = \lambda^2 e^{-\lambda}\sum_{k \ge 2}\frac{\lambda^{k-2}}{(k-2)!} = \lambda^2$.

Then $\mathbb{E}[K^2] = \lambda^2 + \lambda$, so $\operatorname{Var}(K) = \lambda^2 + \lambda - \lambda^2 = \lambda$.

</Prove>
