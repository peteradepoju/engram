<Step n={1} title="Densities" minutes={25} />

A continuous random variable $X$ has a **probability density function** (PDF) $f(x) \ge 0$ with

$$
P(a \le X \le b) = \int_a^b f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x, \qquad \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x = 1.
$$

The density is not a probability: it can exceed 1, and it has units of one over the units of $X$ (per millisecond, per millivolt). The probability of any single exact value is zero, since an interval of zero width has zero area. The CDF is $F(x) = \int_{-\infty}^x f(u)\,\mathrm{d}u$, so the density is its derivative, and expectations are integrals: $\mathbb{E}[X] = \int x f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x$.

Two densities matter today. The **exponential**, $f(t) = re^{-rt}$ for $t \ge 0$, describes Poisson intervals (yesterday). The **uniform** on $[0, 1]$, $f(x) = 1$, is what a computer's random number generator produces, and on Day 5 you will turn it into anything else.

<Step n={2} title="The Gaussian" minutes={25} />

The **Gaussian** (normal) density with mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma$ is

$$
f(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{(x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right).
$$

Any Gaussian becomes the **standard normal** (mean 0, sd 1) after **standardising**, $z = (x - \mu)/\sigma$. About 68% of the probability lies within one standard deviation of the mean, 95% within two (1.96 to be exact) and 99.7% within three. Its CDF has no formula in elementary functions, but is written with the error function, $\Phi(z) = \tfrac{1}{2}\left(1 + \operatorname{erf}(z/\sqrt{2})\right)$, available as `math.erf`.

A membrane potential sitting two standard deviations below threshold crosses it on about 2% of moments. This is the fluctuation-driven regime of Shadlen and Newsome: the mean input keeps the neuron below threshold, and its spikes are caused by the noise, which is why they are irregular.

<Step n={3} title="The central limit theorem" minutes={40} />

Let $X_1, \dots, X_n$ be independent draws from any distribution with mean $\mu$ and finite variance $\sigma^2$. The **central limit theorem** (CLT) says that the standardised sum

$$
Z_n = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n X_i - n\mu}{\sigma\sqrt{n}}
$$

has a distribution that approaches the standard normal as $n \to \infty$. The shape of the individual distribution washes out; only its mean and variance survive. Asymmetry fades slowly, with the skewness of the sum falling like $1/\sqrt{n}$.

A single synapse's contribution is wildly skewed (almost always zero, occasionally large), yet the sum of 2,000 is nearly symmetric, and a Gaussian with the predicted mean and variance gets even its tail right. That answers the prediction. Note how much the inhibition matters: it halves the mean (from 40 mV of summed excitation to 20 mV) while roughly *doubling* the variance, which is exactly the balanced, fluctuation-driven regime.

<Step n={4} title="When it fails" minutes={15} />

The CLT needs independence (or weak dependence) and finite variance. Strongly correlated inputs do not average out, so their sum stays as variable as the shared part. And some distributions have tails so heavy that the variance is infinite. The **Cauchy** distribution, for example, the ratio of two independent standard normals, has no mean at all: the average of a thousand Cauchy draws is exactly as spread out as a single draw.

Heavy tails are not exotic: the distribution of synaptic weights in cortex is close to log-normal, with a long tail of very strong connections (Song and colleagues, 2005), and a few strong inputs can dominate a sum in a way the CLT does not describe.

<Prove title="Mean and variance of the exponential" task="For $f(t) = re^{-rt}$ on $t \ge 0$, show that $\mathbb{E}[T] = 1/r$ and $\operatorname{Var}(T) = 1/r^2$, so the coefficient of variation is 1.">

Integrate by parts with $u = t$, $\mathrm{d}v = re^{-rt}\mathrm{d}t$: $\mathbb{E}[T] = \left[-te^{-rt}\right]_0^\infty + \int_0^\infty e^{-rt}\mathrm{d}t = 0 + 1/r$.

Similarly $\mathbb{E}[T^2] = \left[-t^2e^{-rt}\right]_0^\infty + \int_0^\infty 2te^{-rt}\mathrm{d}t = \tfrac{2}{r}\mathbb{E}[T] = 2/r^2$.

So $\operatorname{Var}(T) = 2/r^2 - 1/r^2 = 1/r^2$, the standard deviation is $1/r$, and the coefficient of variation is $(1/r)/(1/r) = 1$.

</Prove>
