<Step n={1} title="The inverse transform" minutes={30} />

If $U$ is uniform on $[0, 1]$ and $F$ is a CDF, then $X = F^{-1}(U)$ has CDF $F$. (Last week's Forge proved the reverse: $F(X)$ is uniform.) For the exponential, $F(t) = 1 - e^{-rt}$, so $F^{-1}(u) = -\ln(1 - u)/r$, and since $1 - U$ is also uniform, $-\ln(U)/r$ works too.

For a discrete distribution, $F^{-1}$ means: find the first value whose cumulative probability exceeds $U$. This is exactly how a language model picks its next token. It turns its scores (logits) $z_k$ into a PMF with a softmax at **temperature** $\tau$,

$$
p_k = \frac{e^{z_k/\tau}}{\sum_j e^{z_j/\tau}},
$$

and draws one token by the inverse transform. Low temperature concentrates the probability on the top token; high temperature spreads it out.

<Step n={2} title="Thinning" minutes={25} />

To simulate a Poisson process whose rate $\lambda(t)$ changes in time, you could cut time into tiny bins (Week 2). A neater, exact method is **thinning** (Lewis and Shedler, 1979): generate candidate events from a constant-rate process at $\lambda_{\max} \ge \lambda(t)$, using exponential intervals, and keep each candidate at time $t$ with probability $\lambda(t)/\lambda_{\max}$. Thinning a Poisson process independently gives a Poisson process, and the kept events have exactly rate $\lambda(t)$.

<Step n={3} title="Monte Carlo with error bars" minutes={25} />

Many probabilities have no convenient formula, but they can be estimated by simulating the process many times and counting: this is the **Monte Carlo** method, named by Ulam and von Neumann after the casino. An estimate $\hat{p}$ from $n$ independent simulations is a mean of Bernoulli variables, so its standard error is $\sqrt{\hat{p}(1 - \hat{p})/n}$ (Day 2), and $\hat{p} \pm 1.96$ standard errors is an approximate 95% interval (Day 4).

Take the question from Day 2's journal: a synapse with 8 release sites whose release probabilities differ. What is the probability that at least 3 sites release? There is an exact answer, but Monte Carlo gets it in two lines.

Each extra decimal place of accuracy costs a hundred times more simulations, the $1/\sqrt{n}$ rule again. Monte Carlo is slow but general, and it is the basis of the sampling methods of Week 19.

<Step n={4} title="Dropout" minutes={25} />

**Dropout** (Srivastava and colleagues, 2014, today's paper) multiplies each input to a layer, during training, by an independent Bernoulli mask $m_i$ that is 1 with probability $p$. In the usual **inverted** form, kept inputs are scaled by $1/p$, so the unit receives $u = \sum_i w_i x_i m_i/p$.

<Derivation title="What dropout does to a unit's input">

<div>

By linearity, with $\mathbb{E}[m_i] = p$:

$$
\mathbb{E}[u] = \sum_i w_i x_i\,\frac{\mathbb{E}[m_i]}{p} = \sum_i w_i x_i.
$$

The expected input is unchanged, which is why no rescaling is needed at test time.

</div>

<div>

The masks are independent, so variances add. Each term $w_i x_i m_i/p$ has variance $(w_i x_i/p)^2\,p(1 - p)$:

$$
\operatorname{Var}(u) = \frac{1 - p}{p}\sum_i w_i^2 x_i^2.
$$

</div>

<div>

Dropout adds noise whose size depends on the weights: large weights on active inputs are penalised most, so the network is pushed to spread its reliance across many inputs. For linear models this is close to an adaptive L2 penalty (Wager, Wang and Liang, 2013).

</div>

</Derivation>

<Prove title="Why the inverse transform works" task="Let $F$ be a continuous, strictly increasing CDF and $U$ uniform on $[0, 1]$. Show that $X = F^{-1}(U)$ has CDF $F$.">

For any $x$, $P(X \le x) = P(F^{-1}(U) \le x)$.

Because $F$ is strictly increasing, $F^{-1}(U) \le x$ exactly when $U \le F(x)$.

For a uniform variable, $P(U \le c) = c$ for $c$ in $[0, 1]$, so $P(X \le x) = F(x)$. For discrete distributions the same argument works with the generalised inverse, the smallest $x$ with $F(x) \ge u$.

</Prove>
