With a CV of about 0.55 this neuron is far more regular than Poisson, and the gamma model is overwhelmingly more likely. Short intervals are the giveaway: the exponential density is highest at zero, while real neurons, with refractoriness, almost never fire twice within a few milliseconds. Likelihoods and how to compare models fairly are the subject of Weeks 17 and 18.

Even with no quantal variability and plenty of trials, both estimates are biased low. Sites with high probability saturate early, so the variance falls sooner than a uniform-probability synapse would predict. The method is still useful, but its numbers are effective values, and published analyses use more general models (Silver, 2003, reviews them).

  from math import erf, sqrt
  def Phi(z): return 0.5 * (1 + erf(z / sqrt(2)))
  pool_sizes = [1, 10, 100, 1000]

  acc_shared = [accuracy(N, True) for N in pool_sizes]

With independent noise, a hundred neurons decide almost perfectly. With a modest shared gain, a thousand neurons do barely better than ten: the variance of the pooled count is $N\mu + \sigma_g^2N^2\mu^2$, and the second term grows as fast as the squared signal, so the signal-to-noise ratio saturates. This is the quantitative core of Shadlen and Newsome's argument, and of later work on how correlations limit population codes (Week 119).

<Prove title="1. The pooling limit" task="Let the pooled count be Poisson with mean $gN\mu$, where $g$ has mean 1 and variance $\sigma_g^2$. Show that $\operatorname{Var} = N\mu + \sigma_g^2N^2\mu^2$, and that the ratio of the squared mean difference $(N\Delta\mu)^2$ to this variance tends to $\Delta\mu^2/(\sigma_g^2\mu^2)$ as $N \to \infty$.">

By the law of total variance, $\operatorname{Var} = \mathbb{E}[\operatorname{Var}(K \mid g)] + \operatorname{Var}(\mathbb{E}[K \mid g]) = \mathbb{E}[gN\mu] + \operatorname{Var}(gN\mu) = N\mu + \sigma_g^2N^2\mu^2$.

Then $\dfrac{N^2\Delta\mu^2}{N\mu + \sigma_g^2N^2\mu^2} = \dfrac{\Delta\mu^2}{\mu/N + \sigma_g^2\mu^2} \to \dfrac{\Delta\mu^2}{\sigma_g^2\mu^2}$.

The square root, $d' = \Delta\mu/(\sigma_g\mu)$, does not grow with $N$: with $\Delta\mu = 2$, $\mu = 11$ and $\sigma_g^2 = 0.05$ it is about 0.81, the ceiling in Part C.

</Prove>

<Prove title="2. The method of failures" task="If the number of quanta released is Poisson with mean $m$, show that $m = -\ln P(\text{failure})$. If it is binomial($n$, $p$), what does $-\ln P(\text{failure})$ estimate instead, and is it larger or smaller than $np$?">

For Poisson, $P(K = 0) = e^{-m}$, so $m = -\ln P(K = 0)$.

For the binomial, $P(K = 0) = (1 - p)^n$, so $-\ln P(K = 0) = -n\ln(1 - p) = n\left(p + p^2/2 + p^3/3 + \cdots\right)$.

This exceeds $np$ for every $p > 0$, by a factor close to $1 + p/2$ when $p$ is small. The method of failures is accurate only when release probability is low, which is why del Castillo and Katz lowered calcium.

</Prove>
