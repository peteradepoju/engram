<Step n={1} title="Bayes' rule" minutes={25} />

Write the product rule two ways, $P(H \text{ and } D) = P(D \mid H)P(H) = P(H \mid D)P(D)$, and divide by $P(D)$:

$$
\underbrace{P(H \mid D)}_{\text{posterior}} = \frac{\overbrace{P(D \mid H)}^{\text{likelihood}}\;\overbrace{P(H)}^{\text{prior}}}{\underbrace{P(D)}_{\text{evidence}}}, \qquad P(D) = \sum_{H'} P(D \mid H')P(H').
$$

Here $H$ is a hypothesis (the stimulus, a parameter, a cause) and $D$ the data. The **prior** is what you believed before the data; the **likelihood**, read as a function of $H$ with the data fixed, says how well each hypothesis predicts what you saw; the **posterior** is what you should believe afterwards. The evidence is just the normalising constant that makes the posterior sum to 1, computed by the law of total probability. Often you only need proportionality: posterior $\propto$ likelihood $\times$ prior.

<Step n={2} title="Odds and sequential updating" minutes={25} />

For two hypotheses, dividing Bayes' rule for each removes the evidence term and gives the **odds form**:

$$
\frac{P(H_1 \mid D)}{P(H_0 \mid D)} = \frac{P(D \mid H_1)}{P(D \mid H_0)} \times \frac{P(H_1)}{P(H_0)}.
$$

Posterior odds are the **likelihood ratio** times the prior odds. Yesterday's neuron has a likelihood ratio of $0.9/0.05 = 18$ for a burst, but the prior odds are $0.02/0.98$, so the posterior odds are only about 0.37. If observations are independent given the hypothesis, the likelihood ratios multiply, and today's posterior becomes tomorrow's prior. In logarithms the evidence simply adds, which is the idea behind the drift-diffusion model of decisions (Week 24).

<Step n={3} title="Decoding a population" minutes={40} />

Now the hypothesis is a continuous quantity, a grating's orientation $\theta$, and the data are the spike counts $k_1, \dots, k_N$ of $N$ neurons with tuning curves $f_i(\theta)$. If the counts are independent Poisson given $\theta$, the likelihood is a product, and its logarithm a sum:

$$
\log P(\mathbf{k} \mid \theta) = \sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[k_i\log f_i(\theta) - f_i(\theta)\right] + \text{const}.
$$

Evaluate it on a grid of orientations, add the log prior, subtract the maximum (to avoid overflow), exponentiate and normalise: that is the posterior. Its peak is the **maximum a posteriori** (MAP) estimate; the peak of the likelihood alone is the **maximum likelihood** (ML) estimate.

<Step n={4} title="When does the prior matter?" minutes={15} />

With four neurons the likelihood is broad, sometimes with several bumps, and the prior rescues many trials: the mean squared error falls by about 40% (from about 130 to 77 squared degrees). With 32 neurons the likelihood is sharp and the prior barely moves it (10.1 against 9.7). That answers the prediction: a correct prior helps on average, and helps most when the evidence is weak.

Two cautions. The prior helps only if it is right: decode gratings near 120° with a prior centred on 45° and MAP is *worse* than ML, biased towards the expected value. And this bias is not a flaw of the method but a prediction about perception. Körding and Wolpert (2004, today's paper) showed that people's reaching corrections are biased towards a learned prior exactly as much as Bayes' rule predicts, more so when their visual feedback was blurred.

<Prove title="The odds form" task="Starting from Bayes' rule for two hypotheses $H_0$ and $H_1$, derive the odds form. Then show that for $n$ observations that are independent given the hypothesis, the log posterior odds equal the log prior odds plus the sum of the $n$ log likelihood ratios.">

Bayes' rule gives $P(H_j \mid D) = P(D \mid H_j)P(H_j)/P(D)$ for each $j$. Dividing the $j = 1$ equation by the $j = 0$ one, $P(D)$ cancels:

$$
\frac{P(H_1 \mid D)}{P(H_0 \mid D)} = \frac{P(D \mid H_1)}{P(D \mid H_0)}\cdot\frac{P(H_1)}{P(H_0)}.
$$

If $D = (d_1, \dots, d_n)$ with the $d_i$ independent given each hypothesis, $P(D \mid H_j) = \prod_i P(d_i \mid H_j)$, so the likelihood ratio is a product of the individual ratios. Taking logarithms turns the product into a sum: $\log \text{posterior odds} = \log\text{prior odds} + \sum_i \log\frac{P(d_i \mid H_1)}{P(d_i \mid H_0)}$.
