<Step n={1} title="Multiplying Gaussians" minutes={25} />

Suppose vision reports $x_V$ and touch $x_H$, each the true size $s$ plus independent Gaussian noise with standard deviations $\sigma_V$ and $\sigma_H$. With a flat prior, the posterior over $s$ is proportional to the product of the two likelihoods, each a Gaussian in $s$:

$$
p(s \mid x_V, x_H) \propto \exp\!\left(-\frac{(s - x_V)^2}{2\sigma_V^2}\right)\exp\!\left(-\frac{(s - x_H)^2}{2\sigma_H^2}\right).
$$

<Derivation title="The product of two Gaussians">

<div>

The exponent is a quadratic in $s$. Collecting terms, the coefficient of $s^2$ is $-\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\tfrac{1}{\sigma_V^2} + \tfrac{1}{\sigma_H^2}\right)$, so the product is a Gaussian whose **precision** (one over variance) is the sum of the two precisions:

$$
\frac{1}{\sigma^2} = \frac{1}{\sigma_V^2} + \frac{1}{\sigma_H^2}.
$$

</div>

<div>

The coefficient of $s$ is $\tfrac{x_V}{\sigma_V^2} + \tfrac{x_H}{\sigma_H^2}$, and completing the square puts the mean at that coefficient divided by the total precision:

$$
\hat{s} = w_V x_V + w_H x_H, \qquad w_V = \frac{1/\sigma_V^2}{1/\sigma_V^2 + 1/\sigma_H^2}, \quad w_H = 1 - w_V.
$$

</div>

<div>

Each cue is weighted by its share of the total reliability, and the combined variance is smaller than either. With two equally reliable cues, $\sigma = \sigma_V/\sqrt{2}$, which answers the prediction: about 1.4 mm.

</div>

</Derivation>

<Step n={2} title="Ernst and Banks" minutes={40} />

Ernst and Banks (2002, this week's deep read) tested two predictions. First, the weights: when vision and touch are put slightly in conflict, judgements should fall between the two, nearer the more reliable cue, and shift towards touch as vision is degraded. Second, the variance: combined judgements should be more precise than either sense alone. They measured each sense's noise separately, predicted both quantities, and found that people matched them closely.

The variance prediction is the stronger test, because it rules out a tempting alternative: an observer who simply relies on whichever cue is better. That observer's weights would jump from 0 to 1 as visual noise crosses touch noise, and its variance could never beat the better cue.

<Step n={3} title="The prior as a cue" minutes={25} />

A prior is a Gaussian too, and combines in exactly the same way. If hidden shifts $s$ come from a prior $\mathcal{N}(\mu_p, \sigma_p^2)$ and feedback reports $x = s + $ noise of sd $\sigma_\ell$, the posterior mean is

$$
\hat{s} = \frac{\sigma_p^2}{\sigma_p^2 + \sigma_\ell^2}\,x + \frac{\sigma_\ell^2}{\sigma_p^2 + \sigma_\ell^2}\,\mu_p.
$$

Plotted against the true shift, the average estimate is a straight line whose slope, $\sigma_p^2/(\sigma_p^2 + \sigma_\ell^2)$, falls as the feedback gets noisier. This is what Körding and Wolpert (yesterday's paper) measured in reaching: blurrier feedback, flatter slope, more reliance on the learned prior.

<Step n={4} title="Is perception optimal?" minutes={15} />

Results like these are often summarised as "perception is Bayes-optimal", and that claim deserves care. Optimal relative to what? The predictions depend on the noise model, the prior and the cost of errors, all assumed by the experimenter. Many deviations have been found: people sometimes overweight one cue, combine cues that come from different objects, or use priors that differ from the experiment's statistics. A more defensible reading is that the Bayesian observer is a powerful *model* of behaviour: it makes precise, testable predictions, and its failures are informative. <Prove title="Combination never hurts" task="Show that the optimally combined variance $\sigma^2 = (1/\sigma_V^2 + 1/\sigma_H^2)^{-1}$ is smaller than both $\sigma_V^2$ and $\sigma_H^2$, and find its value when $\sigma_V = \sigma_H$.">

Since $1/\sigma_H^2 > 0$, the total precision $1/\sigma_V^2 + 1/\sigma_H^2$ exceeds $1/\sigma_V^2$, so its reciprocal $\sigma^2$ is less than $\sigma_V^2$. The same argument with the roles swapped gives $\sigma^2 \lt \sigma_H^2$.

When $\sigma_V = \sigma_H = \sigma_0$, the total precision is $2/\sigma_0^2$, so $\sigma^2 = \sigma_0^2/2$ and $\sigma = \sigma_0/\sqrt{2}$: two equally good cues cut the standard deviation by about 29%.

</Prove>
