<Step n={1} title="The density" minutes={25} />

A random vector $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is **multivariate Gaussian** with mean $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ and covariance matrix $\Sigma$ if its density is

$$
p(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2}\det(\Sigma)^{1/2}}\exp\!\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})^\top\Sigma^{-1}(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})\right).
$$

The covariance matrix holds every variance on its diagonal and every covariance $\Sigma_{ij} = \operatorname{Cov}(x_i, x_j)$ off it. It is symmetric and positive semidefinite (Week 6). The exponent's quadratic form,

$$
d_M^2(\mathbf{x}) = (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})^\top\Sigma^{-1}(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}),
$$

is the squared **Mahalanobis distance**: distance from the mean measured in units of the noise in each direction. Its level sets, the contours of equal density, are ellipsoids whose axes are the eigenvectors of $\Sigma$ and whose half-lengths are proportional to the square roots of its eigenvalues, the PCA picture of Week 7.

<Step n={2} title="Sampling and distance" minutes={25} />

To sample from $\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \Sigma)$, factor the covariance as $\Sigma = LL^\top$ with $L$ lower triangular (the **Cholesky factorisation**), draw independent standard normals $\mathbf{z}$, and set $\mathbf{x} = \boldsymbol{\mu} + L\mathbf{z}$. Its covariance is $L\,I\,L^\top = \Sigma$, as you will prove in step 3. This is also the **reparameterisation trick** of today's AI paper, used to train variational autoencoders (tomorrow).

<Step n={3} title="Linear combinations" minutes={20} />

If $\mathbf{x} \sim \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \Sigma)$, then for any matrix $A$ and vector $\mathbf{b}$,

$$
A\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b} \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(A\boldsymbol{\mu} + \mathbf{b},\; A\Sigma A^\top\right).
$$

The mean part follows from linearity of expectation; the covariance part from $\operatorname{Cov}(A\mathbf{x}) = \mathbb{E}[A(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})^\top A^\top] = A\Sigma A^\top$. For a single readout $\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}$, the variance is $\mathbf{w}^\top\Sigma\mathbf{w}$, the quantity you minimised in Week 13. For the plain population sum, $\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{1}$, it is the sum of every entry of $\Sigma$, which is where correlations bite.

<Step n={4} title="Conditioning" minutes={35} />

Split the vector into two blocks, $\mathbf{x} = (\mathbf{x}_1, \mathbf{x}_2)$, with the mean and covariance split to match:

$$
\boldsymbol{\mu} = \begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mu}_1 \\ \boldsymbol{\mu}_2\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \Sigma = \begin{pmatrix}\Sigma_{11} & \Sigma_{12} \\ \Sigma_{21} & \Sigma_{22}\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The **marginal** of $\mathbf{x}_1$ is simply $\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_1, \Sigma_{11})$: delete the other rows and columns. The **conditional** of $\mathbf{x}_1$ given $\mathbf{x}_2$ is also Gaussian:

$$
\mathbf{x}_1 \mid \mathbf{x}_2 \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}_1 + \Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}(\mathbf{x}_2 - \boldsymbol{\mu}_2),\;\; \Sigma_{11} - \Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}\Sigma_{21}\right).
$$

Three things to notice. The conditional mean is linear in the observed values, with coefficients $\Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}$: exactly the population regression coefficients, so for Gaussians the best predictor is linear regression. The conditional covariance does not depend on which value was observed. And it is never larger than the marginal covariance: observing correlated variables can only reduce uncertainty. In two dimensions the formulas reduce to the figure's: mean $\rho(\sigma_2/\sigma_1)x_1$ and standard deviation $\sigma_2\sqrt{1 - \rho^2}$.

<Prove title="Conditioning in two dimensions" task="For $(x_1, x_2)$ Gaussian with zero means, standard deviations $\sigma_1, \sigma_2$ and correlation $\rho$, use the block formula to show that $x_2 \mid x_1$ has mean $\rho(\sigma_2/\sigma_1)x_1$ and variance $\sigma_2^2(1 - \rho^2)$.">

Here the observed block is $x_1$ and the predicted block is $x_2$, so the roles in the formula are: $\Sigma_{22} \to \sigma_1^2$ (observed), $\Sigma_{11} \to \sigma_2^2$ (predicted), and $\Sigma_{12} \to \rho\sigma_1\sigma_2$.

The conditional mean is $\rho\sigma_1\sigma_2 \cdot \sigma_1^{-2}\cdot x_1 = \rho(\sigma_2/\sigma_1)x_1$.

The conditional variance is $\sigma_2^2 - (\rho\sigma_1\sigma_2)^2/\sigma_1^2 = \sigma_2^2(1 - \rho^2)$. With $\rho = 0$ nothing is learned; as $|\rho| \to 1$, $x_1$ determines $x_2$ completely.

</Prove>

<Key title="Before you go">
<ul>
<li>A multivariate Gaussian is fixed by its mean vector and covariance matrix; its contours are ellipsoids along the eigenvectors.</li>
<li>Sample with μ + Lz using the Cholesky factor.</li>
<li>Linear readouts are Gaussian with variance wᵀΣw; small pairwise correlations dominate large sums.</li>
<li>Marginals delete rows and columns; conditionals are Gaussian with a regression mean and reduced covariance.</li>
</ul>
</Key>
