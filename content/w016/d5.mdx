<Step n={1} title="Loss functions" minutes={25} />

**Bayesian decision theory** chooses the action $a$ that minimises the **expected loss** under the posterior:

$$
a^* = \arg\min_a \;\mathbb{E}\left[L(a, \theta) \mid \text{data}\right] = \arg\min_a \int L(a, \theta)\,p(\theta \mid \text{data})\,\mathrm{d}\theta.
$$

For estimating a number, three losses give three familiar answers. Squared error, $L = (a - \theta)^2$, is minimised by the **posterior mean**. Absolute error, $|a - \theta|$, by the **posterior median**. And the all-or-nothing loss, zero if exactly right and one otherwise, by the **posterior mode** (MAP). For a symmetric posterior they coincide; for a skewed one they do not, and the choice matters.

<Step n={2} title="Linear discriminant analysis" minutes={40} />

Now the decision is between two classes, $c \in \{0, 1\}$, from a response vector $\mathbf{x}$. Suppose each class's responses are Gaussian with its own mean but a **shared covariance** $\Sigma$. Bayes' rule gives the posterior log odds:

$$
\log\frac{P(c = 1 \mid \mathbf{x})}{P(c = 0 \mid \mathbf{x})} = \underbrace{(\boldsymbol{\mu}_1 - \boldsymbol{\mu}_0)^\top\Sigma^{-1}}_{\mathbf{w}^\top}\mathbf{x} + b,
$$

because the quadratic terms $\mathbf{x}^\top\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{x}$ cancel between the two classes. The posterior is therefore a sigmoid of a linear function of $\mathbf{x}$: exactly the form of logistic regression (Week 12). This is **linear discriminant analysis** (LDA, Fisher 1936). Its weights are $\Sigma^{-1}$ times the difference of means, so the noise correlations tilt the boundary away from the line joining the means. If the classes have different covariances, the quadratic terms survive and the boundary curves (quadratic discriminant analysis).

LDA is a **generative** classifier: it models how the data were produced in each class and inverts with Bayes' rule. Logistic regression is **discriminative**: it fits the posterior directly. When the Gaussian model is right, LDA needs less data; when it is wrong, logistic regression is more robust.

Here the signal pushes the two neurons partly in opposite directions, which is where positively correlated noise is smallest: the neurons' fluctuations go up and down together, so their difference is quiet. A decoder that knows the correlation weights the neurons to read the signal along that quiet direction; one that ignores it reads mostly along the noisy one. On test trials the full decoder is right 74% of the time, against 66% for the one that ignores correlations. That answers the prediction: noticeably worse. When the signal lies along the noisy direction instead, correlations hurt everyone, and ignoring them costs little. This is the geometry at the heart of today's reading by Averbeck and colleagues.

<Step n={3} title="Priors and thresholds" minutes={15} />

With unequal priors the log odds gain a constant, $\log\frac{P(c = 1)}{P(c = 0)}$, which slides the boundary towards the rarer class: you need stronger evidence to choose something unlikely. Different costs for the two kinds of error shift it the same way. In psychophysics this is the observer's **criterion** in signal detection theory, and separating a change in sensitivity ($d'$) from a change in criterion is how experimenters tell whether attention improved perception or merely made people more willing to say "yes".

<Step n={4} title="The reparameterisation trick" minutes={25} />

Many models must optimise an average over Gaussian noise, $\mathbb{E}_{z \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)}[f(z)]$, with respect to $\mu$ and $\sigma$. You cannot differentiate through "draw a sample". But you can write the sample as $z = \mu + \sigma\varepsilon$ with $\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ fixed, and then

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial\mu}\mathbb{E}[f(z)] = \mathbb{E}_\varepsilon\left[f'(\mu + \sigma\varepsilon)\right],
$$

which can be estimated by averaging over a few samples of $\varepsilon$. This is Kingma and Welling's **reparameterisation trick** (2014, today's paper), and with $z = \boldsymbol{\mu} + L\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$ it works for full covariances too. The older alternative, the score-function estimator $\mathbb{E}[f(z)(z - \mu)/\sigma^2]$, needs no derivative of $f$ but is far noisier.

<Prove title="The posterior mean minimises squared error" task="Show that $a^* = \mathbb{E}[\theta \mid \text{data}]$ minimises $\mathbb{E}[(a - \theta)^2 \mid \text{data}]$, and that the minimum value is the posterior variance.">

Write $m = \mathbb{E}[\theta \mid \text{data}]$. Then $(a - \theta)^2 = (a - m)^2 + 2(a - m)(m - \theta) + (m - \theta)^2$.

Taking the posterior expectation, the middle term vanishes because $\mathbb{E}[m - \theta \mid \text{data}] = 0$, leaving $(a - m)^2 + \operatorname{Var}(\theta \mid \text{data})$.

This is smallest when $a = m$, and the minimum is the posterior variance: the irreducible uncertainty that no single answer can remove.
