**Part A: does the posterior mean what it says?** Sixteen neurons tile orientation evenly. On each trial a grating appears at a random orientation, and a gain (like contrast) that varies from 0.5 to 2 scales every neuron's rate. A decoder that does not know the gain computes the posterior over orientation from a flat prior. If the posterior is **calibrated**, its 68% credible set should contain the true orientation on about 68% of trials.

The posterior is close to calibrated even though the decoder never knew the gain. Because the tuning curves tile orientation evenly, the summed rate does not depend on orientation, so the unknown gain cancels from the posterior's shape, and the spikes themselves set its width: more spikes, narrower posterior. Ma, Beck, Latham and Pouget (2006) built a theory of **probabilistic population codes** on exactly this property: with Poisson-like variability, a population's activity carries a whole likelihood function, and its total activity encodes certainty.

The observer is not optimal: it puts 0.92 of its weight on sharp vision where 0.76 would be optimal, and 0.49 on blurred vision where 0.34 would be. Yet its combined precision still beats either sense alone, so the variance test alone would not have caught the bias. Only measuring both quantities, as Ernst and Banks did, and comparing each with its prediction separates "combines cues" from "combines cues optimally". Real data add a further complication: weights and variances are estimated with error, so "consistent with optimal" needs a statistical test (Week 18).

With 200 training trials the prediction explains 68% of neuron 0's variance, close to the 71% limit set by the true covariance. With 30 trials for 20 neurons the estimated covariance is noisy and the prediction explains only 24%: a sample covariance needs many more trials than dimensions. Estimating 190 correlations from a few dozen trials is a real problem in neuroscience, and shrinkage estimators and factor models (Week 17 and Part 5) exist to solve it.

<Prove title="1. The posterior of a mean" task="You observe $x_1, \dots, x_n$, independent $\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ with $\sigma$ known, and put a prior $\mathcal{N}(\mu_0, \sigma_0^2)$ on $\mu$. Show that the posterior of $\mu$ is Gaussian with precision $n/\sigma^2 + 1/\sigma_0^2$, and write its mean.">

The likelihood is $\prod_i \exp(-(x_i - \mu)^2/2\sigma^2)$. As a function of $\mu$ its exponent is $-\tfrac{n}{2\sigma^2}\mu^2 + \tfrac{n\bar{x}}{\sigma^2}\mu + \text{const}$, the same as a single Gaussian cue at $\bar{x}$ with variance $\sigma^2/n$.

Combining this cue with the prior cue by Day 3's rule, precisions add: $1/\sigma_{\text{post}}^2 = n/\sigma^2 + 1/\sigma_0^2$, and the mean is precision-weighted:

$$
\mu_{\text{post}} = \frac{(n/\sigma^2)\,\bar{x} + (1/\sigma_0^2)\,\mu_0}{n/\sigma^2 + 1/\sigma_0^2}.
$$

As $n$ grows the data's precision dominates and the posterior concentrates on $\bar{x}$; this is the conjugate Gaussian model of Week 19.

</Prove>

<Prove title="2. Why LDA is linear" task="For two classes with Gaussian class-conditionals $\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_c, \Sigma)$ sharing $\Sigma$, and priors $\pi_0, \pi_1$, show that the log posterior odds are $\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x} + b$ with $\mathbf{w} = \Sigma^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_1 - \boldsymbol{\mu}_0)$.">

The log odds are $\log\pi_1 - \log\pi_0 - \tfrac{1}{2}(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_1)^\top\Sigma^{-1}(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_1) + \tfrac{1}{2}(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_0)^\top\Sigma^{-1}(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_0)$; the normalising constants cancel because $\Sigma$ is shared.

Expanding, the $\mathbf{x}^\top\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{x}$ terms cancel. The terms linear in $\mathbf{x}$ give $(\boldsymbol{\mu}_1 - \boldsymbol{\mu}_0)^\top\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{x}$, so $\mathbf{w} = \Sigma^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_1 - \boldsymbol{\mu}_0)$.

The rest is constant: $b = -\tfrac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\mu}_1^\top\Sigma^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}_1 + \tfrac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\mu}_0^\top\Sigma^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}_0 + \log(\pi_1/\pi_0)$. With separate covariances the quadratic terms would not cancel and the boundary would curve.

</Prove>
