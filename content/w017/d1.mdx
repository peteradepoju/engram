<Step n={1} title="Estimators" minutes={20} />

A statistical **model** is a family of distributions indexed by unknown **parameters** $\theta$: Poisson spike counts with an unknown rate, Gaussian noise with unknown mean and variance. An **estimator** $\hat{\theta}$ is any rule that turns data $x_1, \dots, x_n$ into a guess for $\theta$. The sample mean is an estimator of a rate; so is the median, and so, absurdly, is "always say 5". Because the data are random, $\hat{\theta}$ is a random variable, and the question "how good is this estimator?" is a question about its distribution.

Fisher (1922, today's paper) set out the criteria still used. An estimator is **unbiased** if $\mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}] = \theta$; **consistent** if it converges to $\theta$ as $n$ grows; and **efficient** if no other unbiased estimator has smaller variance.

<Step n={2} title="The sampling distribution" minutes={25} />

Imagine repeating your experiment many times and computing $\hat{\theta}$ each time. The distribution of those values is the **sampling distribution**. Its standard deviation is the **standard error** (SE). For the sample mean of $n$ independent draws, the SE is $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$ (Week 15), estimated in practice by $s/\sqrt{n}$ with $s$ the sample standard deviation. You can never see the sampling distribution of a real experiment, because you run it once; simulation lets you see it for a model, and the bootstrap (next week) approximates it from data.

<Step n={3} title="Bias, variance and mean squared error" minutes={35} />

The **bias** is $\mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}] - \theta$; the **variance** is $\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\theta})$. The overall accuracy is the **mean squared error**, and it splits exactly into the two:

$$
\operatorname{MSE}(\hat{\theta}) = \mathbb{E}\left[(\hat{\theta} - \theta)^2\right] = \operatorname{bias}^2 + \operatorname{Var}(\hat{\theta}).
$$

The proof is the same move as the law of total variance: add and subtract $\mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}]$, and the cross term vanishes. This decomposition is the seed of the bias-variance trade-off of Day 5.

The classic example is the variance. Dividing the sum of squared deviations from the *sample* mean by $n$ underestimates $\sigma^2$, because the sample mean is, by construction, closer to the data than the true mean is.

<Derivation title="Why n − 1">

<div>

Write $\sum_i (x_i - \bar{x})^2 = \sum_i (x_i - \mu)^2 - n(\bar{x} - \mu)^2$ (expand and use $\sum_i (x_i - \mu) = n(\bar{x} - \mu)$).

</div>

<div>

Take expectations: the first term has expectation $n\sigma^2$, and $\mathbb{E}[(\bar{x} - \mu)^2] = \operatorname{Var}(\bar{x}) = \sigma^2/n$, so

$$
\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_i (x_i - \bar{x})^2\right] = n\sigma^2 - \sigma^2 = (n - 1)\sigma^2.
$$

</div>

<div>

Dividing by $n - 1$ therefore gives an unbiased estimator; dividing by $n$ gives one with bias $-\sigma^2/n$. The $n - 1$ counts the **degrees of freedom** left after estimating the mean.

</div>

</Derivation>

The estimator that divides by $n$ is biased, yet its mean squared error is smaller, because shrinking every estimate towards zero reduces their variance by more than it adds in bias squared. Accepting a little bias for a large cut in variance is the idea behind every regulariser you will meet this week.

<Step n={4} title="The winner's curse" minutes={25} />

Some biases come not from the formula but from how estimates are *selected*. If you pick the largest of several noisy means and report it, you have picked the one whose noise happened to be most positive, so it is biased upwards. This is the **winner's curse**, and it is everywhere in neuroscience: the peak rate of a tuning curve, the most responsive voxel, the strongest effect in a screen. Its cure is to select on one half of the data and measure on the other, a theme of next week.

The naive peak averages 10.29 spikes against a true 10, too high, which answers the prediction; the inflation grows with more noise, fewer trials and more conditions near the peak. The price is noisier estimates, since each half has only five trials.

<Prove title="The bias-variance decomposition" task="Show that $\mathbb{E}[(\hat{\theta} - \theta)^2] = (\mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}] - \theta)^2 + \operatorname{Var}(\hat{\theta})$.">

Let $m = \mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}]$. Then $\hat{\theta} - \theta = (\hat{\theta} - m) + (m - \theta)$, and squaring gives $(\hat{\theta} - m)^2 + 2(\hat{\theta} - m)(m - \theta) + (m - \theta)^2$.

Take expectations. The middle term is $2(m - \theta)\,\mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta} - m] = 0$, because $m - \theta$ is a constant and $\mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}] = m$.

What remains is $\mathbb{E}[(\hat{\theta} - m)^2] + (m - \theta)^2 = \operatorname{Var}(\hat{\theta}) + \operatorname{bias}^2$.

</Prove>

<Key title="Before you go">
<ul>
<li>An estimate is random; its sampling distribution describes its accuracy.</li>
<li>MSE = bias² + variance, and a little bias can buy a large cut in variance.</li>
<li>The sample variance needs n − 1 to be unbiased.</li>
<li>Selecting the best of noisy estimates biases it; use independent data to select and to measure.</li>
</ul>
</Key>
