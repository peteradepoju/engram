<Step n={1} title="The likelihood" minutes={25} />

For independent observations $x_1, \dots, x_n$ from a model $p(x \mid \theta)$, the **likelihood** is the probability of the whole dataset, read as a function of the parameters:

$$
L(\theta) = \prod_{i=1}^n p(x_i \mid \theta), \qquad \ell(\theta) = \log L(\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^n \log p(x_i \mid \theta).
$$

The **maximum likelihood estimate** (MLE) is the $\theta$ that maximises it. Working with the log is almost always easier: products become sums, the tiny numbers that underflow a computer become manageable, and the maximum is in the same place. The likelihood is not a probability distribution over $\theta$ (it need not integrate to 1); turning it into one needs a prior, which is Day 4.

<Step n={2} title="Closed-form estimates" minutes={20} />

For Poisson counts with rate $\lambda$, $\ell(\lambda) = \sum_i (k_i\log\lambda - \lambda) + \text{const}$. Setting $\ell'(\lambda) = \sum_i k_i/\lambda - n = 0$ gives $\hat{\lambda} = \bar{k}$, the mean count. For Gaussian data, the MLEs are the sample mean and $\frac{1}{n}\sum_i (x_i - \bar{x})^2$: the variance divided by $n$, biased, exactly as yesterday. Maximum likelihood estimates are not always unbiased, but under mild conditions they are **consistent** and, for large $n$, **efficient**: their variance reaches the Cramér-Rao bound of tomorrow.

Two more properties make the MLE convenient. It is **invariant**: the MLE of any function of $\theta$ is that function of the MLE (the MLE of a standard deviation is the square root of the MLE of the variance). And minimising squared error is maximum likelihood under Gaussian noise (Week 12), so least squares is a special case.

<Step n={3} title="The Poisson GLM" minutes={25} />

Most models have no closed form. The workhorse of neural data analysis is the **Poisson generalised linear model** (GLM): spike counts $k_i$ are Poisson with rate

$$
\lambda_i = \exp(\mathbf{x}_i^\top\mathbf{w}),
$$

where $\mathbf{x}_i$ holds the covariates on trial or time bin $i$ (stimulus features, the recent stimulus, the neuron's own recent spikes, other neurons' spikes) and $\mathbf{w}$ the weights to fit. The exponential keeps rates positive and turns weights into multiplicative effects. Its log-likelihood and derivatives are

$$
\ell(\mathbf{w}) = \sum_i \left[k_i\,\mathbf{x}_i^\top\mathbf{w} - e^{\mathbf{x}_i^\top\mathbf{w}}\right], \qquad \nabla\ell = X^\top(\mathbf{k} - \boldsymbol{\lambda}), \qquad \nabla^2\ell = -X^\top\operatorname{diag}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})X.
$$

The Hessian is negative semidefinite for every $\mathbf{w}$, so $\ell$ is **concave**: it has no false peaks, and any method that climbs will find the global maximum (Week 12). This is why GLMs with hundreds of parameters, such as those of Pillow and colleagues (2008, today's paper), can be fitted reliably.

**Newton's method** (Week 9) uses the curvature to take much better steps than gradient ascent: $\mathbf{w} \leftarrow \mathbf{w} - (\nabla^2\ell)^{-1}\nabla\ell$. Near the peak it converges quadratically, doubling the number of correct digits each step.

<Step n={4} title="Newton's method on a tuning curve" minutes={35} />

An orientation tuning curve of the form $\exp(a + b\cos 2\theta + c\sin 2\theta)$ is a Poisson GLM in disguise: the covariates are $(1, \cos 2\theta, \sin 2\theta)$. From the fitted weights, the preferred orientation is $\tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{atan2}(c, b)$ and the tuning strength is $\sqrt{b^2 + c^2}$.

Maximum likelihood uses the right noise model: Poisson counts are noisier where the rate is high, and the likelihood automatically weights each trial accordingly, while least squares on transformed counts treats every trial as equally reliable and distorts low counts. Here the gain is modest, a mean squared error of 14.7 against 16.9 square degrees, but it is free, and it grows at lower firing rates, where the log(count + 1) transformation is crudest. That answers the prediction.

<Prove title="The Poisson GLM is concave" task="For $\ell(\mathbf{w}) = \sum_i [k_i\mathbf{x}_i^\top\mathbf{w} - \exp(\mathbf{x}_i^\top\mathbf{w})]$, derive the gradient and Hessian and show that the Hessian is negative semidefinite.">

Differentiate term by term. The derivative of $k_i\mathbf{x}_i^\top\mathbf{w}$ is $k_i\mathbf{x}_i$, and of $\exp(\mathbf{x}_i^\top\mathbf{w})$ is $\lambda_i\mathbf{x}_i$, so $\nabla\ell = \sum_i (k_i - \lambda_i)\mathbf{x}_i = X^\top(\mathbf{k} - \boldsymbol{\lambda})$.

Differentiating again, only $\lambda_i$ depends on $\mathbf{w}$, with $\partial\lambda_i/\partial\mathbf{w} = \lambda_i\mathbf{x}_i$, so $\nabla^2\ell = -\sum_i \lambda_i\mathbf{x}_i\mathbf{x}_i^\top$.

For any vector $\mathbf{v}$, $\mathbf{v}^\top\nabla^2\ell\,\mathbf{v} = -\sum_i \lambda_i(\mathbf{x}_i^\top\mathbf{v})^2 \le 0$, since every $\lambda_i > 0$. So the Hessian is negative semidefinite and $\ell$ is concave.

</Prove>
