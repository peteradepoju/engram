<Step n={1} title="Curvature" minutes={25} />

The **score** is the derivative of the log-likelihood, $s(\theta) = \partial\ell/\partial\theta$. At the true parameter its expectation is zero: on average, the data do not push $\theta$ either way. Its variance is the **Fisher information**,

$$
I(\theta) = \operatorname{Var}\left[s(\theta)\right] = -\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\partial^2\ell}{\partial\theta^2}\right],
$$

the expected curvature of the log-likelihood. For $n$ independent observations it is $n$ times the information in one. For Poisson counts, $\ell'' = -\sum_i k_i/\lambda^2$, so $I(\lambda) = n\lambda/\lambda^2 = n/\lambda$.

<Step n={2} title="Error bars from the curvature" minutes={25} />

Two classic results connect information to precision. The **Cramér-Rao bound**: any unbiased estimator has $\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\theta}) \ge 1/I(\theta)$. And **asymptotic normality**: for large $n$ the MLE is approximately $\mathcal{N}(\theta, 1/I(\theta))$, so it attains the bound. Together they give the **Wald interval**, $\hat{\theta} \pm 1.96/\sqrt{I(\hat{\theta})}$, the most common error bar in science. It is only an approximation: with little data, or near a boundary such as a rate near zero, it can be badly wrong.

<Step n={3} title="Fisher information of a population" minutes={35} />

Now flip the roles: the parameter is a **stimulus** $\theta$, and the data are the spike counts of $N$ neurons with tuning curves $f_i(\theta)$. For independent Poisson neurons the Fisher information about $\theta$ is

$$
I(\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^N \frac{f_i'(\theta)^2}{f_i(\theta)}.
$$

Each neuron contributes most where its tuning curve is *steep*, not where it fires most. For independent neurons, information adds, so doubling $N$ doubles $I$ and improves the best possible precision $\sqrt{I}$ by $\sqrt{2}$, which answers the first part of the prediction. Through the Cramér-Rao bound, $1/\sqrt{I}$ is a floor on the discrimination threshold of any decoder, which is why Fisher information is the standard way to ask how well a population could support perception (Week 36).

<Step n={4} title="Information-limiting correlations" minutes={20} />

With correlated noise of covariance $\Sigma$, the information available to a linear decoder is the **linear Fisher information** $I = \mathbf{f}'^\top\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{f}'$, the reciprocal of the minimum-variance unbiased readout's variance from Week 13. Moreno-Bote and colleagues (2014, today's paper) showed that only one kind of correlation stops it growing with $N$: **differential correlations**, $\Sigma = \Sigma_0 + \varepsilon\,\mathbf{f}'\mathbf{f}'^\top$, which look exactly like a small random shift of the stimulus itself. Then

$$
I = \frac{I_0}{1 + \varepsilon I_0},
$$

which can never exceed $1/\varepsilon$, however many neurons you add. That answers the second part of the prediction.

With independent noise the information grows a hundredfold from 10 to 1,000 neurons; with differential correlations it creeps towards the ceiling of 5 and stops. A correlation of this kind adds only $\varepsilon f_i'f_j'$ to each covariance, far too small to notice pair by pair, yet it caps the whole population. That is why such correlations are hard to measure and why they matter so much.

<Prove title="The score has mean zero" task="Show that $\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\log p(x \mid \theta)\right] = 0$ at the true $\theta$, assuming you may swap differentiation and integration.">

$\mathbb{E}[s] = \int \frac{\partial_\theta p(x \mid \theta)}{p(x \mid \theta)}\,p(x \mid \theta)\,\mathrm{d}x = \int \partial_\theta p(x \mid \theta)\,\mathrm{d}x$.

Swapping the derivative and the integral gives $\partial_\theta \int p(x \mid \theta)\,\mathrm{d}x = \partial_\theta 1 = 0$.

So the score averages to zero at the truth, and the Fisher information, its variance, equals its mean square $\mathbb{E}[s^2]$. Differentiating the identity once more gives the curvature form $I = -\mathbb{E}[\partial_\theta^2\log p]$.

</Prove>
