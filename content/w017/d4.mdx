<Step n={1} title="The MAP estimate" minutes={20} />

Bayes' rule gives the posterior $p(\theta \mid \text{data}) \propto p(\text{data} \mid \theta)\,p(\theta)$. Its peak is the **maximum a posteriori** estimate:

$$
\hat{\theta}_{\text{MAP}} = \arg\max_\theta \left[\ell(\theta) + \log p(\theta)\right].
$$

The log-prior is simply added to the log-likelihood. Written as a minimisation, the negative log-prior is a **penalty** on parameter values the prior considers unlikely. Every regulariser has a prior hiding inside it, and every prior gives a regulariser. As data accumulate, the log-likelihood (a sum over $n$ observations) grows and the fixed prior matters less, so MAP and MLE agree for large $n$.

<Step n={2} title="Ridge is a Gaussian prior" minutes={30} />

Take a linear model with Gaussian noise, $y = X\mathbf{w} + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$ with noise variance $\sigma^2$, and a Gaussian prior $\mathbf{w} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \tau^2 I)$. The negative log-posterior is, up to constants,

$$
\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\lVert\mathbf{y} - X\mathbf{w}\rVert^2 + \frac{1}{2\tau^2}\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert^2.
$$

Multiplying by $2\sigma^2$, this is ridge regression with $\lambda = \sigma^2/\tau^2$, whose solution is $\hat{\mathbf{w}} = (X^\top X + \lambda I)^{-1}X^\top\mathbf{y}$. A tight prior (small $\tau$) or noisy data (large $\sigma$) means strong shrinkage. Here the posterior is Gaussian, so its mode and mean coincide and the MAP estimate is also the posterior mean.

<Step n={3} title="Receptive fields from little data" minutes={40} />

A simple cell's linear receptive field $\mathbf{w}$ is estimated by showing white-noise stimuli $\mathbf{x}_i$ and recording responses $y_i = \mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}_i + $ noise. Least squares needs many more trials than pixels. But a prior can say more than "weights are small": real receptive fields are **smooth**, so neighbouring pixels have similar weights. A Gaussian prior with precision matrix $D^\top D$, where $D$ takes differences between neighbours, penalises roughness:

$$
\hat{\mathbf{w}} = (X^\top X + \lambda D^\top D)^{-1}X^\top\mathbf{y}.
$$

Priors like this, and the automatic smoothness determination of Sahani and Linden (2003), are standard in receptive-field estimation.

Least squares misses by 36% of the receptive field's size (in squared error), ridge by 18% and the smoothness prior by 12%. A prior that encodes something true about receptive fields does better than one that only says "small", and both beat no prior at all, though how much varies from dataset to dataset: try other seeds. That answers the prediction: the bias the prior introduces is tiny compared with the variance it removes. The strength $\lambda$ was simply chosen here; tomorrow you choose it from the data.

<Step n={4} title="Laplace priors and sparsity" minutes={15} />

A **Laplace** prior, $p(w_j) \propto e^{-|w_j|/b}$, gives the penalty $\lambda\sum_j|w_j|$: the lasso (Tibshirani, 1996, today's paper). Its sharp peak at zero is why the MAP estimate is exactly sparse, the geometry you saw in Week 13. Note a subtlety: the Laplace prior does not believe weights are exactly zero (it assigns zero probability to any exact value); the zeros come from taking the posterior *mode*. The posterior *mean* under the same prior is never exactly sparse.

<Prove title="Lasso as MAP" task="Show that with Gaussian noise of variance σ² and independent Laplace priors $p(w_j) = \frac{1}{2b}e^{-|w_j|/b}$, the MAP estimate minimises $\lVert\mathbf{y} - X\mathbf{w}\rVert^2 + \lambda\sum_j|w_j|$, and find λ.">

The negative log-likelihood is $\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\lVert\mathbf{y} - X\mathbf{w}\rVert^2$ plus a constant, and the negative log-prior is $\frac{1}{b}\sum_j|w_j|$ plus a constant.

Maximising the posterior means minimising their sum. Multiplying by $2\sigma^2$, which does not move the minimum, gives $\lVert\mathbf{y} - X\mathbf{w}\rVert^2 + \frac{2\sigma^2}{b}\sum_j|w_j|$.

So $\lambda = 2\sigma^2/b$: noisier data or a narrower prior (small $b$) mean a stronger penalty and more zeros.

</Prove>
