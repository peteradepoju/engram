<Step n={1} title="The trade-off" minutes={25} />

Suppose data are $y = f(x) + \varepsilon$ with noise variance $\sigma^2$, and you fit a model $\hat{f}$ to a random training set. At a new point $x$, the expected squared prediction error splits into three parts:

$$
\mathbb{E}\left[(y - \hat{f}(x))^2\right] = \underbrace{\left(\mathbb{E}[\hat{f}(x)] - f(x)\right)^2}_{\text{bias}^2} + \underbrace{\operatorname{Var}(\hat{f}(x))}_{\text{variance}} + \underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{noise}}.
$$

This is Day 1's decomposition applied to predictions. Simple models have high bias and low variance; flexible ones the reverse. Noise is irreducible. Training error, measured on the data used to fit, always falls as complexity grows, so it cannot be used to choose: it rewards fitting the noise.

<Step n={2} title="Cross-validation" minutes={35} />

In a real experiment you have one dataset, so you cannot average over many. **k-fold cross-validation** imitates it: split the data into $k$ folds, fit on $k - 1$ of them, measure the error on the held-out fold, rotate, and average. The result estimates the test error of a model fitted to slightly less data. Choose the complexity, or the regularisation strength, with the lowest cross-validated error. Typical choices are $k = 5$ or 10; $k = n$ is **leave-one-out**.

<Step n={3} title="Information criteria" minutes={20} />

Cross-validation refits the model $k$ times. **Information criteria** estimate the same out-of-sample performance from a single fit, by penalising the maximised log-likelihood $\hat{\ell}$ for the number of parameters $p$:

$$
\text{AIC} = 2p - 2\hat{\ell}, \qquad \text{BIC} = p\log n - 2\hat{\ell}.
$$

Lower is better. AIC (Akaike, 1974) approximates leave-one-out cross-validation and tends to choose models that predict well; BIC penalises complexity more heavily for $n > 7$ and tends to choose simpler models, consistently finding the true one if it is among the candidates. For Gaussian errors with the variance estimated, $-2\hat{\ell} = n\log(\text{RSS}/n) + \text{const}$.

<Step n={4} title="Honest reporting and double descent" minutes={25} />

Once you have chosen a model by cross-validation, its cross-validated error is optimistic: it is the best of several noisy scores, the winner's curse of Day 1. To report performance honestly, either keep a final **test set** untouched until the end, or use **nested cross-validation**, which repeats the whole selection inside each outer fold. In neuroscience, where datasets are small, this matters: a decoder "selected" on the same trials used to report its accuracy will look better than it is.

The U-shaped curve is not the whole story. Belkin and colleagues (2019, this week's deep read) showed that, for models that can interpolate the data, test error can fall again as complexity grows far beyond the number of data points, the **double descent** you glimpsed in Week 13. The bias-variance decomposition still holds; what changes is that among the many models that fit the training data exactly, the fitting procedure picks a smooth one, and its variance can fall.

<Prove title="Expected prediction error" task="With $y = f(x) + \varepsilon$, $\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon] = 0$, $\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon) = \sigma^2$, and $\varepsilon$ independent of the training data, show that $\mathbb{E}[(y - \hat{f}(x))^2] = \text{bias}^2 + \operatorname{Var}(\hat{f}(x)) + \sigma^2$.">

Write $y - \hat{f} = \varepsilon + (f - \hat{f})$. Squaring and taking expectations, the cross term $2\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon(f - \hat{f})] = 2\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon]\mathbb{E}[f - \hat{f}] = 0$, because the new noise is independent of the fitted model.

So $\mathbb{E}[(y - \hat{f})^2] = \sigma^2 + \mathbb{E}[(f - \hat{f})^2]$.

The second term is the mean squared error of $\hat{f}(x)$ as an estimator of $f(x)$, which Day 1 split into $\text{bias}^2 + \operatorname{Var}(\hat{f}(x))$.

</Prove>

<ul>
<li>Test error = bias² + variance + noise; training error cannot choose complexity.</li>
<li>Cross-validation estimates out-of-sample error from a single dataset.</li>
<li>AIC and BIC penalise the likelihood for parameters; BIC prefers simpler models.</li>
<li>Report performance on data not used for selection, and be aware that very large models can break the U-shape.</li>
