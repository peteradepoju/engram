<Step n={1} title="The logic of a test" minutes={25} />

A test has four parts.

1. A **null hypothesis** $H_0$, a precise statement of "nothing interesting": the mean rate change is zero, the two conditions have the same distribution.
2. A **test statistic** $T$ computed from the data, large when the data disagree with $H_0$: for example $z = (\bar{x} - \mu_0)/(\sigma/\sqrt{n})$.
3. The **null distribution** of $T$: its distribution if $H_0$ were true.
4. The **p-value**: the probability, under $H_0$, of a statistic at least as extreme as the one observed.

If $p$ is below a **significance level** $\alpha$ chosen in advance (often 0.05), you **reject** $H_0$. Rejecting a true null is a **type I error**, with probability $\alpha$; failing to reject a false null is a **type II error**, with probability $\beta$. The **power** is $1 - \beta$, the chance of detecting a real effect (Day 4).

What a p-value is not: it is not the probability that $H_0$ is true, not the probability that the result is due to chance, and not a measure of the size or importance of an effect. These are three of the six principles in today's paper.

<Step n={2} title="p-values under the null" minutes={25} />

If $H_0$ is true and the test is correct, the p-value is **uniformly distributed** between 0 and 1 (it is the null CDF of the statistic evaluated at itself, Week 13's histogram equalisation in reverse). So exactly a fraction $\alpha$ of null experiments give $p \lt \alpha$: that is the sense in which a test "controls" its type I error rate. Under a real effect, p-values pile up near zero, more steeply the higher the power.

<Step n={3} title="Does this neuron respond?" minutes={25} />

A common real test: on each of $n$ trials you count spikes in a baseline window and in a response window. The **paired** design compares each trial with itself, removing slow drifts between trials. Test whether the mean of the differences is zero with $t = \bar{d}/(s_d/\sqrt{n})$, where $s_d$ is the sample standard deviation of the differences. With unknown variance this follows Student's **t distribution** with $n - 1$ degrees of freedom, which has heavier tails than the Gaussian for small $n$; for $n$ above about 30 the Gaussian approximation is close.

  base = rng_p.poisson(drift)

  p_paired = 2 * (1 - Phi(abs(t_stat)))
  z_unp = (resp.mean() - base.mean()) / np.sqrt(base.var(ddof=1) / n + resp.var(ddof=1) / n)

The paired test finds the response (p about 0.02); the unpaired test, swamped by the drift, does not (p about 0.23). The drift adds variance to each condition, but it cancels in the paired differences. Designing the comparison to remove known sources of variance is often worth more than collecting more trials.

<Step n={4} title="What a significant result means" minutes={30} />

$\alpha$ is the probability of a significant result *given* a true null. What a reader wants is the reverse: the probability the effect is real *given* a significant result, the **positive predictive value** (PPV). Bayes' rule (Week 16) connects them through the prior fraction $\pi$ of tested hypotheses that are true and the power $1 - \beta$:

$$
\text{PPV} = \frac{(1 - \beta)\,\pi}{(1 - \beta)\,\pi + \alpha\,(1 - \pi)}.
$$

Barely half of the significant results are real. That answers the prediction: "p below 0.05" is far from "95% sure". It gets worse with lower power or rarer true effects, the argument of this week's deep read by Button and colleagues.

<Prove title="p-values are uniform under the null" task="Let T have a continuous null CDF $F$, and define the one-sided p-value $p = 1 - F(T)$. Show that, if $H_0$ is true, $P(p \le \alpha) = \alpha$ for every $\alpha$ in $[0, 1]$.">

Under $H_0$, $T$ has CDF $F$, so $F(T)$ is uniform on $[0, 1]$ (the probability integral transform, Week 15's Forge).

Then $1 - F(T)$ is also uniform, so $P(p \le \alpha) = \alpha$.

This is exactly what it means for the test to have type I error rate $\alpha$: if you reject when $p \le \alpha$, you reject a true null with probability $\alpha$. Two-sided p-values work the same way with $|T|$.

</Prove>
