<Step n={1} title="Exchangeability" minutes={20} />

Suppose you have responses under a control and a drug condition. The null hypothesis says the drug does nothing, so each response would have been the same whichever label it had been given. The labels are then **exchangeable**: every relabelling of the data is equally likely under the null. Compute the statistic (say, the difference in means) for many random relabellings, and the collection is the null distribution. The **permutation p-value** is

$$
p = \frac{1 + \#\{\text{shuffles with } |T^*| \ge |T_{\text{obs}}|\}}{1 + N_{\text{shuffles}}},
$$

where the 1s count the observed labelling as one of the possibilities, so $p$ is never zero. No formula, no Gaussian assumption, and any statistic you like: a difference in medians, a decoding accuracy, a correlation.

When the data really are Gaussian, the permutation test agrees with the classical test. Its advantage shows when they are not: skewed firing rates, outliers, small samples, or statistics such as decoding accuracy for which no formula exists.

<Step n={3} title="Paired data: sign flips" minutes={20} />

For paired data (baseline and response on the same trial), the null says each trial's difference was as likely to be positive as negative. The exchangeable units are the signs: flip each difference's sign at random and recompute the mean. This **sign-flip test** is the permutation version of the paired t-test.

<Step n={4} title="Fifty neurons: the maximum statistic" minutes={40} />

Testing 50 neurons at $\alpha = 0.05$ each, the chance of at least one false positive when none responds is $1 - 0.95^{50} \approx 0.92$, which answers the prediction. The **family-wise error rate** (FWER), the probability of any false positive, must be controlled across the family of tests.

Permutation tests have an elegant solution (Nichols and Holmes, 2002, today's paper). On each shuffle, apply the *same* relabelling to all neurons and record only the **maximum** statistic across them. A neuron is significant if its observed statistic beats the 95th percentile of that maximum distribution. Because the same shuffle is used for every neuron, any correlations between neurons are automatically respected, which simple corrections like Bonferroni ignore.

<Callout kind="warning">

Permutation tests still assume exchangeability, and it fails for **autocorrelated** data. Shuffling individual time bins of a slowly varying signal destroys its autocorrelation, so the shuffled statistics are too tame and the test finds "significant" relationships between unrelated signals. For time series, shuffle whole trials, or shift one signal circularly in time, as you did for spike-phase locking in Week 11.

</Callout>

<Prove title="Family-wise error with independent tests" task="Show that for m independent tests of true nulls, each at level α, the probability of at least one false positive is $1 - (1 - \alpha)^m$, and that it is at most $m\alpha$ for any tests, independent or not.">

Under independence, the probability that no test rejects is the product of the individual probabilities, $(1 - \alpha)^m$, so at least one rejects with probability $1 - (1 - \alpha)^m$. For $m = 50$, $\alpha = 0.05$ this is 0.92.

Without independence, use the union bound: $P(\text{any rejects}) \le \sum_i P(\text{test } i \text{ rejects}) = m\alpha$.

Setting the per-test level to $\alpha/m$ therefore keeps the FWER at most $\alpha$: the Bonferroni correction. The maximum statistic does the same job but uses the actual dependence between tests, so it is less conservative when tests are correlated.

</Prove>
