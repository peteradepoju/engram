<Step n={1} title="Confidence intervals" minutes={20} />

A **95% confidence interval** is a procedure that, applied to repeated experiments, produces intervals containing the true parameter 95% of the time. The promise is about the procedure, not about one interval: after you compute $[2.1, 3.4]$, the truth either is or is not inside it. For a mean with known standard error, the interval $\bar{x} \pm 1.96\,\text{SE}$ is exact for Gaussian data and approximately right for large samples (Week 17's Wald interval).

Confidence intervals are more informative than p-values. An interval that excludes zero is significant at 0.05, but it also says how large the effect might be: $[0.01, 0.02]$ and $[1, 20]$ are both "significant" and mean very different things.

<Step n={2} title="The bootstrap" minutes={30} />

The sampling distribution of a statistic needs repeated samples from the population, which you do not have. Efron's (1979, today's paper) substitution: use the **empirical distribution** of your sample as a stand-in for the population. Drawing $n$ points *with replacement* from your data mimics drawing a new sample. Repeat $B$ times (1,000 to 10,000), compute the statistic each time, and:

- the standard deviation of the $B$ values is the **bootstrap standard error**;
- the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles give a **percentile interval**.

Resampling with replacement is essential: without replacement every resample would be the original data reshuffled.

<Step n={3} title="Coverage" minutes={25} />

Is a "95%" bootstrap interval really 95%? Only approximately. The bootstrap relies on the sample resembling the population, which works poorly for small samples and for statistics that depend on extreme values (the maximum, for example). Refinements such as the BCa interval correct some of this. The honest check is a simulation of **coverage**, which you can always do for a model.

<Step n={4} title="Nested data" minutes={30} />

Neuroscience data are usually **nested**: trials within neurons, neurons within animals, animals within labs. Neurons from the same animal share its genetics, its surgery and its brain state, so they are not independent. Resampling neurons as if they were independent treats 200 neurons from 5 mice like 200 mice and produces intervals that are far too narrow. The **hierarchical bootstrap** resamples at every level: first draw animals with replacement, then neurons within each drawn animal (Saravanan, Berman and Sober, 2020).

  def make_data(rng, mice=5, per=40):

The naive interval is about a third as wide as it should be and misses the truth about half the time, which answers the prediction. The hierarchical interval is honest about the fact that you really have five independent units, not 200. Its coverage is still a little below 95%, because five mice are very few for any method; with so few, a model-based approach (Week 19's hierarchical models) can do better.

<Prove title="The chance a point is left out" task="In a bootstrap resample of n points drawn with replacement, show that the probability that a given original point does not appear is $(1 - 1/n)^n$, and that this tends to $e^{-1} \approx 0.368$ as n grows.">

Each of the $n$ draws misses the given point with probability $1 - 1/n$, independently, so the probability that all $n$ miss it is $(1 - 1/n)^n$.

As $n \to \infty$, $(1 - 1/n)^n \to e^{-1}$ (the definition of $e$, as in Week 15's Poisson limit), about 0.368.

So each bootstrap resample contains about 63% of the distinct original points, some of them several times. The left-out 37% can serve as a free test set, which is how random forests estimate their own error ("out-of-bag" error).

</Prove>
