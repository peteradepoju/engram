<Step n={1} title="Effect sizes" minutes={20} />

A p-value mixes the size of an effect with the amount of data. An **effect size** separates them. The most common, **Cohen's d**, is the difference in means divided by the standard deviation:

$$
d = \frac{\mu_1 - \mu_0}{\sigma}.
$$

By convention 0.2 is small, 0.5 medium and 0.8 large, though what counts as important depends on the field. For neural data the natural scale is often physical (spikes per second, percent correct), and reporting the effect with a confidence interval in those units is best of all.

<Step n={2} title="Power and sample size" minutes={30} />

For a two-sided z-test of a mean with effect size $d$ and $n$ observations, the test statistic is centred on $d\sqrt{n}$ when the effect is real. Power is the chance it lands beyond $\pm z_{1-\alpha/2}$, and solving for the $n$ that gives power $1 - \beta$:

$$
n \approx \left(\frac{z_{1-\alpha/2} + z_{1-\beta}}{d}\right)^2.
$$

With $\alpha = 0.05$ and 80% power, $z_{0.975} = 1.96$ and $z_{0.8} = 0.84$, so $n \approx 7.85/d^2$: 32 for a medium effect and 197 for a small one, rounding up. Halving the effect quadruples the sample size.

<Step n={3} title="Power by simulation" minutes={25} />

Real experiments rarely fit a formula: Poisson counts, drifting baselines, permutation tests, decoders. Simulation is the general method. Write down a generative model of the experiment with your best guess of the effect and the noise, simulate it many times with your planned analysis, and count how often it succeeds. Change the number of trials until power reaches your target. This is a habit worth forming before every experiment.

<Step n={4} title="The winner's curse of low power" minutes={30} />

Suppose only significant results are reported. With low power, a study reaches significance only when its noise happened to push the estimate far from zero, so the published estimates are inflated: a **type M** (magnitude) error. Occasionally the noise pushes the estimate far in the *wrong* direction and that is significant too: a **type S** (sign) error (Gelman and Carlin, 2014). Button and colleagues (2013, this week's deep read) argued that median power in neuroscience was around 20%, which makes both errors common.

The published effects average about twice the truth, which answers the prediction (about 0.6). A later, larger replication finds a smaller effect and is wrongly called a "failure to replicate", when the original estimate was simply inflated. The cure is power: plan sample sizes in advance, and treat surprisingly large effects from small studies with suspicion.

<Prove title="The sample-size formula" task="For a two-sided z-test with effect size d, show that power is approximately $\Phi(d\sqrt{n} - z_{1-\alpha/2})$ when $d\sqrt{n}$ is large, and solve for n to get $n = ((z_{1-\alpha/2} + z_{1-\beta})/d)^2$.">

When the effect is real, $z = \bar{x}\sqrt{n}/\sigma$ is Gaussian with mean $d\sqrt{n}$ and sd 1. Power is $P(z > z_{1-\alpha/2}) + P(z \lt -z_{1-\alpha/2})$; for a positive effect the second term is negligible.

So power $\approx P(Z > z_{1-\alpha/2} - d\sqrt{n}) = \Phi(d\sqrt{n} - z_{1-\alpha/2})$, with $Z$ standard normal.

Setting this equal to $1 - \beta = \Phi(z_{1-\beta})$ gives $d\sqrt{n} - z_{1-\alpha/2} = z_{1-\beta}$, so $n = ((z_{1-\alpha/2} + z_{1-\beta})/d)^2$.

</Prove>

<Key title="Before you go">
<ul>
<li>Report effect sizes and intervals, not only p-values.</li>
<li>Sample size grows like 1/d²; plan it with a formula or by simulation.</li>
<li>Simulate the actual experiment and analysis to estimate power.</li>
<li>Low power exaggerates published effects and can flip their sign.</li>
</ul>
