<Step n={1} title="The false discovery rate" minutes={25} />

Yesterday's family-wise error rate (FWER) controls the chance of *any* false positive. With thousands of tests that is very strict, and real effects are missed. Benjamini and Hochberg (1995, today's paper) proposed a different target. If $R$ tests are declared significant and $V$ of them are false, the **false discovery rate** is

$$
\text{FDR} = \mathbb{E}\left[\frac{V}{\max(R, 1)}\right],
$$

the expected fraction of discoveries that are wrong. Controlling the FDR at 5% means that, on average, 95% of what you report is real, a sensible goal when you will follow up the findings.

<Step n={2} title="Benjamini-Hochberg" minutes={30} />

The **Benjamini-Hochberg** (BH) procedure for $m$ tests at level $q$:

1. Sort the p-values: $p_{(1)} \le p_{(2)} \le \cdots \le p_{(m)}$.
2. Find the largest $k$ with $p_{(k)} \le \tfrac{k}{m}q$.
3. Declare the $k$ smallest significant.

Bonferroni compares every p-value with $q/m$; BH lets the threshold rise in proportion to the rank, so when many effects are real it finds many more of them.

<Step n={3} title="Double dipping" minutes={25} />

**Double dipping**, or circular analysis, uses the same data to select something and then to measure it (Kriegeskorte and colleagues, 2009). Choose the "most responsive" neurons, voxels or time windows on all the data, then report their response on the same data, and you report the winner's curse of Week 17 at scale: noise that happened to look like a response was what got them selected. The fix is the one you already know: select on one half of the data and measure on the other, or use cross-validation.

The circular analysis reports a solid "response" from neurons that do nothing, which answers the prediction. The independent half shows the truth. The same error hides in many forms: decoders whose features were selected on all trials, regions of interest defined by the effect being tested, time windows chosen where the difference looked largest.

<Step n={4} title="Forking paths" minutes={25} />

Even without explicit multiple tests, an analysis has many choices: which time window, which trials to exclude, which baseline, which test. Each is defensible, but if they are made after seeing the data, choosing the version that "works" is a multiple comparison in disguise. Gelman and Loken called this the **garden of forking paths**. The defence is to fix choices in advance, ideally in a **pre-registration**, or to report all of them.

A single pre-chosen window gives close to 5% (slightly more, because the Gaussian approximation is a little optimistic with 30 trials). None of this requires bad faith: it is enough to look at the data before deciding how to analyse them.

<Prove title="Bonferroni controls the family-wise error rate" task="Show that rejecting each of m hypotheses when its p-value is below α/m gives a family-wise error rate of at most α, whatever the dependence between the tests.">

Let $I_0$ be the set of true null hypotheses, with $m_0 \le m$ members. A family-wise error means at least one $p_i \lt \alpha/m$ with $i \in I_0$.

By the union bound, $P\left(\bigcup_{i \in I_0}\{p_i \lt \alpha/m\}\right) \le \sum_{i \in I_0} P(p_i \lt \alpha/m) = m_0\cdot\frac{\alpha}{m} \le \alpha$, using that each null p-value is uniform (Day 1).

No independence was needed. The price is power: when the tests are positively correlated, or many effects are real, the bound is far from tight.

</Prove>

<Key title="Before you go">
<ul>
<li>With many tests, control the FWER (Bonferroni, maximum statistic) or the FDR (Benjamini-Hochberg).</li>
<li>BH finds far more real effects when many exist, at the price of a controlled fraction of false ones.</li>
<li>Never select and measure on the same data.</li>
<li>Fix analysis choices in advance, or report every path you tried.</li>
</ul>
</Key>
