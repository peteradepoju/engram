Treating 72 neurons as independent declares a drug effect; respecting the fact that the drug was given to 6 mice, which respond very differently, does not. With only 6 mice the exact sign-flip test has just 64 possible relabellings, so its smallest possible p-value is 2/64, about 0.03: the experiment could never give strong evidence, a power problem built into the design.

**Part B: is the decoder above chance?** Two hundred neurons are recorded on 100 trials of two conditions, but none of them carries any information about the condition. A decoder picks the 5 neurons with the largest difference between conditions and classifies by the nearest class mean, with 5-fold cross-validation. Cross-validation alone does not protect you: if any step that looks at the labels (feature selection, choosing a window, tuning a parameter) happens outside the folds, the test folds have already leaked into the model. Every such step must be inside the loop, and the permutation test must shuffle labels and rerun the whole pipeline.

Uncorrected testing finds 72 neurons, 12 of them false; Benjamini-Hochberg finds 60 with only 2 false. One subtlety: with 500 shuffles the smallest possible p-value is 1/501, about 0.002, and a single discovery would need $p \le (1/300)\times 0.05 \approx 0.00017$. BH works here only because many neurons are tuned at once, so the threshold it needs rises with their rank. If just a handful were tuned, it would find nothing at all: permutation tests combined with multiple-comparison corrections need many shuffles, or the maximum statistic of Day 2.

<Step n={3} title="Derivation set" minutes={10} />

<Prove title="1. The smallest permutation p-value" task="Show that an exact permutation test with N possible relabellings cannot give a p-value below 1/N, and apply this to a sign-flip test with 6 subjects.">

The observed labelling is itself one of the $N$ relabellings, and its statistic is at least as extreme as itself, so at least 1 of the $N$ counts towards the p-value. The smallest possible p-value is therefore $1/N$.

A sign-flip test on 6 subjects has $2^6 = 64$ patterns. For a two-sided test, the observed pattern and its mirror image (all signs flipped) give the same $|\bar{d}|$, so the minimum is $2/64 \approx 0.031$.

No result from 6 subjects can therefore reach $p \lt 0.01$, whatever the effect. The number of independent units limits the strength of the evidence a design can ever provide.

</Prove>

<Prove title="2. BH with independent tests controls FDR (a special case)" task="Suppose all m hypotheses are true nulls with independent uniform p-values. Show that the BH procedure at level q makes at least one discovery with probability at most q, so its FDR is at most q in this case.">

When all nulls are true, every discovery is false, so the false discovery proportion is 1 if anything is found and 0 otherwise. The FDR is then the probability of finding anything.

BH finds something exactly when some $p_{(k)} \le (k/m)q$. Simes (1986) showed that, for independent uniform p-values, the probability of this event is exactly $q$.

So FDR $= q$ when all nulls are true: in this case controlling the FDR is the same as controlling the FWER. With real effects present, BH is far more permissive than FWER control, but its FDR is still at most $q$, as Benjamini and Hochberg proved.

</Prove>
