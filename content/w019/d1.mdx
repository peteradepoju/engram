<Step n={1} title="Conjugacy" minutes={20} />

A prior is **conjugate** to a likelihood if the posterior belongs to the same family as the prior. Then the posterior is described by a few numbers, and each new observation updates those numbers by a simple rule. Three conjugate pairs cover much of neuroscience:

| Data | Likelihood | Conjugate prior | Update |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| successes in trials | binomial | beta(a, b) | a + successes, b + failures |
| counts | Poisson | gamma(a, b) | a + total count, b + number of observations |
| measurements | Gaussian (known variance) | Gaussian | precisions add (Week 16) |

You met the third already: cue combination. Today is about the first two.

<Step n={2} title="The beta-binomial model" minutes={30} />

A synapse releases on $k$ of $n$ trials with unknown probability $p$. The **beta** prior has density $\propto p^{a-1}(1 - p)^{b-1}$ and mean $a/(a + b)$. Multiplying by the binomial likelihood $p^k(1 - p)^{n-k}$ gives

$$
p \mid k \sim \text{Beta}(a + k,\; b + n - k).
$$

The prior acts like $a$ earlier successes and $b$ earlier failures. The posterior mean is

$$
\frac{a + k}{a + b + n} = \underbrace{\frac{a + b}{a + b + n}}_{\text{weight on prior}}\cdot\frac{a}{a + b} + \underbrace{\frac{n}{a + b + n}}_{\text{weight on data}}\cdot\frac{k}{n},
$$

a weighted average of the prior mean and the observed fraction, with the data's weight growing as $n$ grows. A **credible interval** contains, say, 95% of the posterior probability, and unlike a confidence interval it says directly: given the data and the prior, $p$ lies in this interval with probability 0.95.

Sequential updating gives exactly the batch answer, whatever the order: yesterday's posterior is today's prior. Written as an update of the mean after each trial, $\hat{p} \leftarrow \hat{p} + \frac{1}{a + b + t}(x_t - \hat{p})$, it is a learning rule whose learning rate shrinks as $1/t$. Behrens and colleagues (2007, today's paper) showed that people's learning rates rise again when the world becomes volatile, as a Bayesian learner that tracks change would do.

<Step n={3} title="The gamma-Poisson model" minutes={25} />

For spike counts $k_1, \dots, k_n$ with rate $\lambda$ per trial, the conjugate prior is the **gamma** distribution with shape $a$ and rate $b$, density $\propto \lambda^{a-1}e^{-b\lambda}$, mean $a/b$. The posterior is

$$
\lambda \mid k_{1:n} \sim \text{Gamma}\!\left(a + \sum_i k_i,\; b + n\right),
$$

so the prior acts like $b$ earlier trials containing $a$ spikes in total, and the posterior mean $(a + \sum k_i)/(b + n)$ is again a weighted average of the prior mean and the observed mean count.

<Step n={4} title="Shrinkage across neurons" minutes={30} />

Where does a prior come from? Often from other units like the one you are studying. If you record many neurons, the spread of their rates is a natural prior for each one. Pulling each neuron's noisy estimate towards the population average, by an amount set by how noisy it is, is called **shrinkage**, and it is the idea behind hierarchical models and empirical Bayes.

Pulling each estimate towards the population average makes every neuron's estimate slightly biased but much less variable, and the overall error nearly halves (a mean squared error of 1.7 against 3.1), which answers the prediction. The surprise is that this holds even though the neurons are independent: borrowing strength from the others helps each one. In real data the population prior is not known in advance but estimated from the same neurons, which is what hierarchical models do (Part 5).

<Prove title="Gamma-Poisson conjugacy" task="Show that if $\lambda \sim \text{Gamma}(a, b)$ and $k_1, \dots, k_n$ are independent Poisson($\lambda$), then $\lambda \mid k_{1:n} \sim \text{Gamma}(a + \sum_i k_i, b + n)$.">

The prior density is $\propto \lambda^{a-1}e^{-b\lambda}$. Each Poisson likelihood term is $\propto \lambda^{k_i}e^{-\lambda}$, so the likelihood of all the counts is $\propto \lambda^{\sum_i k_i}e^{-n\lambda}$.

Multiplying, the posterior is $\propto \lambda^{a + \sum_i k_i - 1}e^{-(b + n)\lambda}$.

This is the kernel of a Gamma$(a + \sum_i k_i, b + n)$ density. Since the posterior must integrate to 1, it is exactly that distribution.

</Prove>

<Key title="Before you go">
<ul>
<li>Conjugate pairs make the posterior a simple update of the prior's parameters.</li>
<li>Beta for probabilities, gamma for Poisson rates; priors act as pseudo-observations.</li>
<li>Posterior means are weighted averages; their weight on the prior shrinks like a falling learning rate.</li>
<li>A prior drawn from the population shrinks noisy estimates and lowers total error.</li>
</ul>
</Key>
