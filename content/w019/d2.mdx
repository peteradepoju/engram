<Step n={1} title="Summaries" minutes={20} />

From a posterior you can report the **mean**, **median** or **mode** (Week 16 matched each to a loss), a **credible interval**, and, most usefully, the posterior probability of any statement: $P(p > 0.5 \mid \text{data})$, $P(\text{rate increased} \mid \text{data})$. Two kinds of credible interval are common: the **equal-tailed** interval (2.5% of probability in each tail) and the **highest posterior density** interval (the narrowest interval holding 95%), which differ for skewed posteriors. When the posterior is awkward, draw samples from it and compute everything from the samples, the approach of the rest of this week.

<Step n={2} title="The posterior predictive" minutes={25} />

To predict a new observation, average the model's prediction over the posterior:

$$
p(\tilde{x} \mid \text{data}) = \int p(\tilde{x} \mid \theta)\,p(\theta \mid \text{data})\,\mathrm{d}\theta.
$$

This **posterior predictive** distribution includes two kinds of uncertainty: the randomness of the observation given $\theta$, and your uncertainty about $\theta$. Plugging in a single estimate ignores the second and is overconfident. For the gamma-Poisson model the posterior predictive of a new count is a **negative binomial** distribution, whose variance exceeds its mean, the over-dispersion of Week 15 appearing from uncertainty about the rate.

<Step n={3} title="Posterior predictive checks" minutes={30} />

A model can be the best of the ones you tried and still be wrong. A **posterior predictive check** asks: if the fitted model were true, would data like mine be typical? Simulate many replicated datasets from the posterior predictive, compute a statistic that captures a feature you care about (the Fano factor, the longest run of silence, the largest count), and see where the real data's value falls among the replicates. If it is far in the tail, the model misses that feature.

<Step n={4} title="Bayes factors" minutes={30} />

To compare two models $M_0$ and $M_1$, use Bayes' rule at the level of models. Each model's **marginal likelihood** (or **evidence**) averages its likelihood over its own prior:

$$
p(\text{data} \mid M) = \int p(\text{data} \mid \theta, M)\,p(\theta \mid M)\,\mathrm{d}\theta.
$$

The **Bayes factor** $B_{10} = p(\text{data} \mid M_1)/p(\text{data} \mid M_0)$ multiplies the prior odds of the models to give their posterior odds (Kass and Raftery, 1995, today's paper). A flexible model spreads its predictions thinly over many possible datasets, so unless the data need its flexibility it predicts them less well: this built-in penalty is the **Bayesian Occam's razor**.

  from math import erf, sqrt, log, exp
  def log_choose(n, k): return lgamma(n + 1) - lgamma(k + 1) - lgamma(n - k + 1)

  def log_ev_fair(k, n):
      return log_choose(n, k) + n * log(0.5)

  def log_ev_flexible(n):
      return log(1 / (n + 1))

  k_b, n_b = 5100, 10000
  bf01 = exp(log_ev_fair(k_b, n_b) - log_ev_flexible(n_b))
  z = (k_b / n_b - 0.5) / sqrt(0.25 / n_b)
  p_value = 2 * (1 - 0.5 * (1 + erf(abs(z) / sqrt(2))))
  print(f"z = {z:.2f}, p = {p_value:.4f};  Bayes factor for p = 0.5 against unknown p: {bf01:.1f}")

The p-value says "significant"; the Bayes factor favours $p = 0.5$ by about 11 to 1. That answers the prediction. This is **Lindley's paradox**: with huge samples, a tiny deviation from the null is significant, but the flexible model, which spread its predictions over all $p$ from 0 to 1, predicted "very close to half" far less well than the model that said exactly half. The two approaches answer different questions, and the Bayes factor depends strongly on the prior of the flexible model: a prior concentrated near 0.5 would favour it more.

<Prove title="The Bayesian Occam's razor" task="Show that under a uniform prior on p, the evidence for k successes in n trials is $1/(n + 1)$ for every k. Use $\int_0^1 p^k(1-p)^{n-k}\,\mathrm{d}p = \frac{k!\,(n-k)!}{(n+1)!}$.">

The evidence is $\int_0^1 \binom{n}{k}p^k(1 - p)^{n-k}\cdot 1\,\mathrm{d}p = \binom{n}{k}\cdot\frac{k!\,(n-k)!}{(n+1)!}$.

Since $\binom{n}{k} = \frac{n!}{k!\,(n-k)!}$, this simplifies to $\frac{n!}{(n+1)!} = \frac{1}{n + 1}$.

So the flexible model spreads its total probability of 1 equally over the $n + 1$ possible outcomes. The fair model concentrates its probability near $k = n/2$, so it beats the flexible model whenever the data land near the middle, and loses only for clearly lopsided data. Flexibility is paid for by thinner predictions everywhere.

</Prove>

<Key title="Before you go">
<ul>
<li>Report posteriors with intervals and probabilities of the statements you care about.</li>
<li>Predict by averaging over the posterior; plug-in predictions are overconfident.</li>
<li>Check models by simulating replicated data and comparing features.</li>
<li>Bayes factors compare models by their evidence and penalise unneeded flexibility, and they can disagree with p-values.</li>
</ul>
