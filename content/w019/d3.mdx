<Step n={1} title="Monte Carlo" minutes={20} />

If you have samples $\theta^{(1)}, \dots, \theta^{(S)}$ from the posterior, any posterior quantity is an average over them:

$$
\mathbb{E}[g(\theta) \mid \text{data}] \approx \frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^S g(\theta^{(s)}).
$$

The posterior mean, its standard deviation, the probability that $\theta > 0$ (the average of an indicator), a credible interval (percentiles of the samples), and the posterior of any function of $\theta$, such as a preferred orientation computed from tuning weights: all come from the same samples. If the samples are independent the error falls like $1/\sqrt{S}$ (Week 15). The hard part is getting the samples.

<Step n={2} title="The Metropolis algorithm" minutes={30} />

You can usually compute the posterior up to a constant, $\pi(\theta) \propto p(\text{data} \mid \theta)p(\theta)$, but not its normalising evidence. The **Metropolis algorithm** (Metropolis and colleagues, 1953, today's paper) needs only ratios:

1. From the current $\theta$, propose $\theta' = \theta + \varepsilon$ with symmetric noise (for example Gaussian, of width $w$).
2. Accept with probability $\min\left(1, \pi(\theta')/\pi(\theta)\right)$; otherwise stay at $\theta$ (and record it again).

Moves uphill are always accepted; moves downhill sometimes. The resulting Markov chain spends time in each region in proportion to its posterior probability, so after an initial **burn-in** its states are samples from the posterior (correlated ones). The reason is **detailed balance**: the probability flow from $\theta$ to $\theta'$ equals the flow back, $\pi(\theta)T(\theta \to \theta') = \pi(\theta')T(\theta' \to \theta)$, which makes $\pi$ a stationary distribution of the chain. Work in logs to avoid underflow: accept if $\log u \lt \log\pi(\theta') - \log\pi(\theta)$.

<Step n={3} title="Tuning and diagnostics" minutes={30} />

Successive states of a chain are correlated, so $S$ steps carry less information than $S$ independent samples. The **effective sample size** (ESS) estimates how many independent samples they are worth:

$$
\text{ESS} = \frac{S}{1 + 2\sum_{k \ge 1}\rho_k},
$$

where $\rho_k$ is the autocorrelation of the chain at lag $k$ (summed until it becomes negligible). Too small a proposal width gives high acceptance but tiny moves, so successive states are nearly identical; too large a width gives frequent rejections, so the chain sits still. For random-walk Metropolis a rule of thumb targets acceptance rates of roughly 25 to 50%. Further checks: run several chains from different starting points and confirm they agree (the $\hat{R}$ statistic), and look at trace plots for drifts.

The middle width wins by a wide margin, which answers the prediction. Neither extreme of acceptance is good: what matters is how far the chain travels per step, which the ESS measures. Better samplers, such as Hamiltonian Monte Carlo, use the gradient of the log-posterior to make long moves that are still accepted (Neal, 2011).

<Step n={4} title="A Bayesian tuning curve" minutes={25} />

Week 17 fitted a tuning curve by maximum likelihood and gave Wald error bars. Now sample its whole posterior. The model is Poisson counts with log-rate $w_0 + w_1\cos 2\theta + w_2\sin 2\theta$ and a Gaussian prior on the weights. The preferred orientation $\tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{atan2}(w_2, w_1)$ is a non-linear function of the weights, so its posterior has no formula, but its samples are just the function applied to each weight sample.

The same few lines would sample any model whose log-posterior you can write: add spike history, a different tuning shape, or a hierarchy across neurons, and only `log_post` changes. That generality is why MCMC transformed applied statistics.

<Prove title="Metropolis satisfies detailed balance" task="With a symmetric proposal $q(\theta' \mid \theta) = q(\theta \mid \theta')$ and acceptance $A(\theta \to \theta') = \min(1, \pi(\theta')/\pi(\theta))$, show that $\pi(\theta)\,q(\theta' \mid \theta)A(\theta \to \theta') = \pi(\theta')\,q(\theta \mid \theta')A(\theta' \to \theta)$ for $\theta \ne \theta'$.">

Suppose $\pi(\theta') \le \pi(\theta)$. Then $A(\theta \to \theta') = \pi(\theta')/\pi(\theta)$ and $A(\theta' \to \theta) = 1$.

The left side is $\pi(\theta)\,q(\theta' \mid \theta)\cdot\pi(\theta')/\pi(\theta) = \pi(\theta')\,q(\theta' \mid \theta)$. The right side is $\pi(\theta')\,q(\theta \mid \theta')\cdot 1$, which is equal by symmetry of $q$. The case $\pi(\theta') > \pi(\theta)$ is the same with the roles swapped.

Detailed balance implies that if the chain's state is distributed as $\pi$, it remains so after a step: summing the flow into any state over all origins gives back $\pi$. Combined with the chain being able to reach every region, this makes $\pi$ the distribution the chain settles into.

</Prove>

<Key title="Before you go">
<ul>
<li>Samples from a posterior give every summary you need.</li>
<li>Metropolis samples any posterior known up to a constant, thanks to detailed balance.</li>
<li>Tune by effective sample size, not acceptance alone; discard burn-in and check convergence.</li>
<li>Posteriors of derived quantities come free: transform the samples.</li>
