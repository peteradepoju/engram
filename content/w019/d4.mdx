<Step n={1} title="Latent variables" minutes={20} />

A **latent variable** is a quantity the model needs but the data do not show directly. In a **mixture model**, each observation $x_i$ comes from one of $K$ components, and the latent variable $z_i \in \{1, \dots, K\}$ says which:

$$
z_i \sim \text{Categorical}(\boldsymbol{\pi}), \qquad x_i \mid z_i = k \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_k, \sigma^2).
$$

For spike sorting, $x_i$ is a spike's amplitude (or a vector of waveform features), $\mu_k$ is neuron $k$'s typical amplitude and $\pi_k$ the fraction of spikes it fires. The unknowns are the parameters $\mu_k, \pi_k$ *and* every $z_i$. Their joint posterior is complicated, but each piece is simple once the others are fixed.

<Step n={2} title="Gibbs sampling" minutes={30} />

**Gibbs sampling** draws each variable in turn from its **full conditional** distribution, its distribution given the current values of all the others:

$$
\theta_j^{\text{new}} \sim p(\theta_j \mid \theta_{-j}, \text{data}).
$$

Each update is a Metropolis step that is always accepted, so the chain samples the joint posterior (Geman and Geman, 1984). It is ideal when the conditionals are standard distributions, as they are for conjugate models, and it needs no tuning. Its weakness is strong correlation: if two variables are tightly coupled, updating one at a time moves only in small zigzags, like coordinate-wise optimisation in a long valley (Week 13).

<Step n={3} title="Sorting spikes" minutes={40} />

For the two-neuron mixture with known noise $\sigma$, a Gaussian prior on each mean and a uniform prior on $\pi$, every full conditional is standard:

- **Assignments**: $P(z_i = k \mid \cdot) \propto \pi_k\,\mathcal{N}(x_i; \mu_k, \sigma^2)$, a two-way categorical draw for each spike.
- **Means**: given the spikes assigned to unit $k$, $\mu_k$ has a Gaussian posterior with precision $n_k/\sigma^2 + 1/\tau^2$ (Week 16's cue combination).
- **Mixing fraction**: $\pi \mid z \sim \text{Beta}(1 + n_1, 1 + n_0)$ (Day 1).

Spikes far from the boundary are assigned with near certainty; those in the overlap get intermediate probabilities, which answers the prediction. A sorter that forces every spike into one unit throws that uncertainty away, and downstream analyses (such as correlations between the two neurons) inherit hidden errors. Two practical notes: the components can swap labels during sampling (**label switching**), since nothing in the model says which unit is "1", and the **EM algorithm** finds a point estimate of the same model by alternating soft assignments and parameter updates, Gibbs sampling's deterministic cousin. Modern spike sorters use richer models of waveforms, drift and overlapping spikes, but the core is still a mixture.

<Step n={4} title="The sampling brain" minutes={15} />

If a brain must represent uncertainty about latent causes, one way is to let neural activity *be* samples from the posterior, varying from moment to moment. Berkes, Orbán, Lengyel and Fiser (2011, this week's deep read) derived a striking prediction: averaged over natural inputs, the posterior equals the prior, so spontaneous activity (sampling the prior, with no input) should match the average activity evoked by natural scenes, if the brain's model is well adapted. In ferret visual cortex the match improved over development for natural movies, but not for artificial gratings or noise. Read it with today's ideas in mind, and with the question: what else could explain the result?

<Prove title="Gibbs updates are always accepted" task="Show that a Metropolis-Hastings step that proposes a new value of $\theta_j$ from its full conditional $p(\theta_j \mid \theta_{-j})$, leaving $\theta_{-j}$ unchanged, has acceptance probability 1. (The Hastings acceptance for an asymmetric proposal $q$ is $\min\left(1, \frac{\pi(\theta')q(\theta \mid \theta')}{\pi(\theta)q(\theta' \mid \theta)}\right)$.)">

Write $\pi(\theta) = p(\theta_j \mid \theta_{-j})\,p(\theta_{-j})$, and similarly for $\theta'$, which shares $\theta_{-j}$. The proposal is $q(\theta' \mid \theta) = p(\theta_j' \mid \theta_{-j})$, and the reverse is $q(\theta \mid \theta') = p(\theta_j \mid \theta_{-j})$.

The ratio is $\frac{p(\theta_j' \mid \theta_{-j})\,p(\theta_{-j})\cdot p(\theta_j \mid \theta_{-j})}{p(\theta_j \mid \theta_{-j})\,p(\theta_{-j})\cdot p(\theta_j' \mid \theta_{-j})} = 1$.

So the acceptance probability is $\min(1, 1) = 1$. Gibbs sampling is Metropolis-Hastings with a proposal so good it is never rejected.

</Prove>
