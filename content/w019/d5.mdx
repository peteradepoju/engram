<Step n={1} title="The idea" minutes={20} />

Pick a family $\mathcal{Q}$ of tractable distributions $q(z)$ over the latent variables, for example independent Gaussians with free means and variances (the **mean-field** family). Find the member closest to the posterior in **Kullback-Leibler divergence**:

$$
q^* = \arg\min_{q \in \mathcal{Q}} \mathrm{KL}\left(q(z)\,\|\,p(z \mid x)\right), \qquad \mathrm{KL}(q\,\|\,p) = \mathbb{E}_q\left[\log\frac{q(z)}{p(z)}\right] \ge 0.
$$

The KL divergence is zero only when the two distributions are equal, and it is not symmetric; Week 20 studies it properly. The difficulty is that the posterior contains the unknown evidence $p(x)$. The next step removes it.

<Step n={2} title="The evidence lower bound" minutes={30} />

<Derivation title="From KL to the ELBO">

<div>

Write the posterior as $p(z \mid x) = p(x, z)/p(x)$ and expand the KL divergence:

$$
\mathrm{KL}(q\,\|\,p(z \mid x)) = \mathbb{E}_q[\log q(z)] - \mathbb{E}_q[\log p(x, z)] + \log p(x).
$$

</div>

<div>

Rearrange, defining the **evidence lower bound**:

$$
\log p(x) = \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_q[\log p(x, z)] - \mathbb{E}_q[\log q(z)]}_{\text{ELBO}(q)} + \mathrm{KL}(q\,\|\,p(z \mid x)).
$$

</div>

<div>

The left side does not depend on $q$, and the KL term is non-negative, so the ELBO is a lower bound on the log evidence, and *maximising the ELBO minimises the KL divergence*. The ELBO needs only the joint $p(x, z) = p(x \mid z)p(z)$, which you can compute. Its two terms have a natural reading: fit the data on average under $q$, and keep $q$ spread out (its entropy, $-\mathbb{E}_q[\log q]$).

</div>

</Derivation>

Every approximation falls below the log evidence, and the exact posterior reaches it: the gap is precisely the KL divergence. Note that the ELBO also approximates the evidence itself, which is how variational methods compare models.

<Step n={3} title="Variational inference for a tuning curve" minutes={40} />

Return to Day 3's Bayesian tuning curve, and approximate its posterior over the three weights with independent Gaussians, $q(\mathbf{w}) = \prod_j \mathcal{N}(m_j, s_j^2)$. Maximise the ELBO by stochastic gradient ascent, using the **reparameterisation trick** of Week 16: write $\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{m} + \mathbf{s}\odot\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$ with $\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$ standard normal. Then, with $g = \nabla_{\mathbf{w}}\log p(\mathbf{k}, \mathbf{w})$ and optimising $\log\mathbf{s}$ so that $\mathbf{s}$ stays positive,

$$
\nabla_{\mathbf{m}}\text{ELBO} = \mathbb{E}_\varepsilon[g], \qquad \nabla_{\log\mathbf{s}}\text{ELBO} = \mathbb{E}_\varepsilon[g\odot\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}]\odot\mathbf{s} + 1,
$$

where the $+1$ comes from the entropy of each Gaussian factor. Average over a few samples of $\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$ per step and use Adam (Week 13).

<Step n={4} title="What the approximation misses" minutes={15} />

The means agree with MCMC; the standard deviations are equal or smaller. That answers the prediction. Two reasons. First, $\mathrm{KL}(q\,\|\,p)$ heavily penalises $q$ for placing probability where the posterior has little, so $q$ prefers to sit inside the posterior rather than spread over it: it is **mode-seeking**, as the figure shows for two peaks. Second, a mean-field $q$ cannot represent correlations, and an independent Gaussian fitted inside a correlated, elongated posterior must be narrow along the axes. Variational inference is fast, scales to huge models and gives good means, but treat its uncertainty with suspicion; full-covariance or richer families, or a check against MCMC on a small problem, help (Blei and colleagues, 2017, today's paper).

<Prove title="The ELBO is a lower bound by Jensen's inequality" task="Show directly that $\log p(x) \ge \mathbb{E}_q[\log p(x, z) - \log q(z)]$, using Jensen's inequality: for the concave function log, $\log\mathbb{E}[Y] \ge \mathbb{E}[\log Y]$.">

Write the evidence as an expectation under $q$: $p(x) = \int p(x, z)\,\mathrm{d}z = \int q(z)\frac{p(x, z)}{q(z)}\,\mathrm{d}z = \mathbb{E}_q\left[\frac{p(x, z)}{q(z)}\right]$.

Taking logs and applying Jensen's inequality: $\log p(x) = \log\mathbb{E}_q\left[\frac{p(x, z)}{q(z)}\right] \ge \mathbb{E}_q\left[\log\frac{p(x, z)}{q(z)}\right]$, which is the ELBO.

Equality holds in Jensen's inequality when the quantity inside is constant, that is when $p(x, z)/q(z)$ does not depend on $z$, which happens exactly when $q(z) = p(z \mid x)$.

</Prove>
