**Part A: is this neuron tuned?** Compare two models of a neuron's counts at random orientations. $M_0$: a constant rate, $\log\lambda = w_0$. $M_1$: a tuning curve, $\log\lambda = w_0 + w_1\cos 2\theta + w_2\sin 2\theta$. Each weight has a $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ prior. The evidence of a model is the likelihood averaged over its prior, which you can estimate by drawing many parameter vectors from the prior and averaging their likelihoods (computed in logs, with the log-sum-exp trick to avoid underflow).

solution={String.raw`
  rng = np.random.default_rng(2011)
  def log_mean_exp(a):
      mx = a.max()
      return mx + np.log(np.mean(np.exp(a - mx)))

  def log_evidence(k, X, n_draws, rng):
      W = rng.normal(size=(n_draws, X.shape[1]))
      eta = W @ X.T                                          # draws x trials
      ll = (k * eta - np.exp(eta)).sum(axis=1)                                               # Poisson log-likelihood of each draw (drop log k!)
      return log_mean_exp(ll)

  theta = rng.uniform(0, np.pi, 40)
  X1 = np.column_stack([np.ones(40), np.cos(2 * theta), np.sin(2 * theta)])
  X0 = X1[:, :1]
  neurons = {"tuned": rng.poisson(np.exp(1.2 + 0.7 * np.cos(2 * (theta - 1.0)))), "untuned": rng.poisson(np.exp(1.2) * np.ones(40))}
  log_bf = {name: log_evidence(k, X1, 100000, rng) - log_evidence(k, X0, 100000, rng) for name, k in neurons.items()}
  print({name: round(v, 2) for name, v in log_bf.items()}, " (log Bayes factors, tuned against constant)")

For the untuned neuron the evidence favours the simpler model even though the tuned model, with its extra weights, could fit the data at least as well: Occam's razor at work. Averaging likelihoods over the prior works here because there are only three weights; in high dimensions almost every prior draw has negligible likelihood and the estimate becomes useless, which is why better methods (importance sampling, nested sampling, the ELBO) exist.

**Part B: learning in a changing world.** A reward option pays out with a probability that switches between 0.2 and 0.8 every 40 trials. A standard Bayesian learner with a Beta(1, 1) prior accumulates all the evidence and slows down; a learner that **forgets**, multiplying its pseudo-counts by a factor below 1 before each update, keeps its learning rate up. This is a simple stand-in for the volatility tracking of Behrens and colleagues (Day 1).

Neither learner is best everywhere. In the stable world forgetting multiplies the error fivefold (0.0075 against 0.0015), because the learner never stops reacting to noise; in the volatile world it cuts the error by about 40% (0.055 against 0.089), because the full learner is still averaging over blocks that no longer apply. The right amount of forgetting depends on the volatility, which is why Behrens and colleagues' learner infers the volatility itself, and why their participants learned faster in volatile blocks.

With the right prior, Bayesian 95% intervals contain the truth 95% of the time across synapses, as promised. With a confident wrong prior they fail badly, because 10 trials are not enough to overrule 80 pseudo-trials. A Bayesian analysis is only as good as its prior; with little data, check sensitivity to the prior and prefer priors learned from the population (Day 1).

<Prove title="1. Evidence as an average over the prior" task="Show that the Monte Carlo estimate $\frac{1}{S}\sum_s p(x \mid \theta^{(s)})$ with $\theta^{(s)}$ drawn from the prior is an unbiased estimate of the evidence $p(x)$, and explain why its variance explodes in high dimensions.">

For each draw, $\mathbb{E}_{\theta \sim p(\theta)}[p(x \mid \theta)] = \int p(x \mid \theta)p(\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta = p(x)$, so the average of $S$ draws is unbiased.

Its variance is $\frac{1}{S}\operatorname{Var}_{p(\theta)}[p(x \mid \theta)]$. When the posterior is much narrower than the prior, as it is when there is a lot of data or many parameters, almost all prior draws land where the likelihood is negligible, and the average is dominated by rare lucky draws.

The fraction of prior volume inside the posterior shrinks roughly exponentially with the number of parameters, so the number of draws needed grows exponentially. Methods that sample near the posterior instead are needed.

</Prove>

<Prove title="2. A forgetting learner's effective memory" task="A learner multiplies its pseudo-counts by a factor $\gamma \lt 1$ before each update. Show that in a long, stable run the total pseudo-count $a + b - 2$ approaches $1/(1 - \gamma)$, so the learner behaves as if it remembered only the last $1/(1 - \gamma)$ trials.">

Let $c_t = a_t + b_t - 2$ be the total pseudo-count from data. Each step does $c \leftarrow \gamma c$ and then adds one observation, so $c_{t+1} = \gamma c_t + 1$.

The fixed point satisfies $c^* = \gamma c^* + 1$, so $c^* = 1/(1 - \gamma)$, and $c_t$ approaches it geometrically from any start.

With $\gamma = 0.9$ this is 10 trials: the learning rate settles near $1/12$ instead of falling towards zero, which is what lets it follow switches every 40 trials.

</Prove>
