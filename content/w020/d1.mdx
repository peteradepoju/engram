<Step n={1} title="Surprise" minutes={20} />

An outcome with probability $p$ carries **surprise** (or information content) $-\log_2 p$ bits. A certain outcome ($p = 1$) carries none; a fair coin's outcome carries 1 bit; an outcome with probability 1/1024 carries 10 bits. Logarithms make surprise **additive**: for two independent outcomes the probabilities multiply and the surprises add. Base 2 gives **bits**; the natural logarithm gives **nats** (1 nat ≈ 1.44 bits).

<Step n={2} title="Entropy and its properties" minutes={25} />

The **entropy** of a discrete random variable is its average surprise:

$$
H(X) = -\sum_x p(x)\log_2 p(x),
$$

with $0\log 0 = 0$. Shannon (1948, today's paper) showed that this is the only measure (up to the choice of base) that is continuous, grows with the number of equally likely options, and adds up correctly when a choice is broken into stages. Its properties:

- $H \ge 0$, with equality only when one outcome is certain.
- $H \le \log_2 n$ for $n$ possible outcomes, with equality only for the uniform distribution.
- For independent variables, $H(X, Y) = H(X) + H(Y)$; in general $H(X, Y) = H(X) + H(Y \mid X) \le H(X) + H(Y)$, where the **conditional entropy** $H(Y \mid X)$ is the average entropy of $Y$ once $X$ is known.

The last inequality says that dependence between variables reduces their joint uncertainty, the seed of tomorrow's mutual information.

<Step n={3} title="Codes and compression" minutes={30} />

Entropy has an operational meaning: it is the minimum average number of bits per symbol needed to record the outcomes of a source without loss (the **source coding theorem**). The idea is to give frequent outcomes short codewords and rare ones long codewords, with lengths close to $-\log_2 p(x)$. **Huffman's algorithm** builds the best such prefix code: repeatedly merge the two least probable symbols into one, adding a bit to each of their codewords. Its average length is always between $H$ and $H + 1$ bits.

The Huffman code beats the 3-bit fixed-length code and comes within a fraction of a bit of the entropy, which no lossless code can beat. Compression is prediction: the better your model of the source, the shorter your code, an idea that connects entropy to modern language models (Day 4).

<Step n={4} title="The entropy of spike trains" minutes={30} />

To measure a neuron's entropy, discretise its spike train into bins of width $\Delta t$ (a 1 if there is a spike, 0 otherwise) and group them into **words** of $L$ bins. The entropy of the word distribution, estimated from a long recording, measures the variety of patterns the neuron produces: an upper bound on how much it could convey (Strong and colleagues, 1998, Day 3's paper). If bins were independent with spike probability $p$, each would contribute the binary entropy $h(p) = -p\log_2 p - (1 - p)\log_2(1 - p)$, and the words $L\,h(p)$. Any structure, such as refractoriness, makes some words impossible and others more likely, lowering the entropy below that.

At the same firing rate, the refractory neuron's words have about 4.4 bits against 5.1, which answers the prediction: forbidding spikes in quick succession removes possible patterns. Entropy is an upper bound on information, not information itself: a neuron could produce rich patterns that have nothing to do with the stimulus. Separating the two is tomorrow's job.

<Prove title="The uniform distribution has maximum entropy" task="Using Jensen's inequality for the concave function $\log$, show that $H(X) \le \log_2 n$ for any distribution over n outcomes, with equality for the uniform distribution.">

Write $H(X) = \sum_x p(x)\log_2\frac{1}{p(x)} = \mathbb{E}\left[\log_2\frac{1}{p(X)}\right]$, an average over outcomes with nonzero probability.

By Jensen's inequality, $\mathbb{E}[\log_2 Y] \le \log_2\mathbb{E}[Y]$. With $Y = 1/p(X)$, $\mathbb{E}[Y] = \sum_x p(x)\cdot\frac{1}{p(x)} = $ (number of outcomes with nonzero probability) $\le n$.

So $H(X) \le \log_2 n$. Equality in Jensen needs $1/p(x)$ to be constant, that is $p(x) = 1/n$ for all $n$ outcomes. (Week 13 found the same result with a Lagrange multiplier.)

</Prove>
