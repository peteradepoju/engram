<Step n={1} title="The definition" minutes={25} />

The **mutual information** between a stimulus $S$ and a response $R$ is the reduction in uncertainty about one from knowing the other:

$$
I(S; R) = H(R) - H(R \mid S) = H(S) - H(S \mid R) = \sum_{s, r} p(s, r)\log_2\frac{p(s, r)}{p(s)\,p(r)}.
$$

The first form reads: the variety of responses, minus the variety that remains when the stimulus is fixed (the **noise entropy**). The second: what you knew about the stimulus, minus what you still do not know after seeing the response. The third: how far the joint distribution is from the product of the marginals, the KL divergence of Day 4 between $p(s, r)$ and $p(s)p(r)$. Mutual information is symmetric, never negative, zero exactly when $S$ and $R$ are independent, and at most $\min(H(S), H(R))$. Unlike correlation, it detects any kind of dependence, not just linear.

<Step n={2} title="A tuned neuron" minutes={35} />

For a neuron with tuning curve $f(\theta)$ (spikes per second) and a counting window $T$, the count given orientation $\theta$ is Poisson with mean $f(\theta)T$. With 8 equally likely orientations, you can build the joint table of orientation and count exactly from the Poisson probabilities, and compute the information. Dividing by the mean number of spikes gives **bits per spike**, a measure of how efficiently the spikes are used.

Information grows with the window, but less than proportionally, and it can never exceed $\log_2 8 = 3$ bits, the entropy of the stimulus. That answers the prediction. Each extra spike adds less, because the neuron cannot tell apart orientations with identical rates on either side of its peak, however many spikes it fires. A single neuron is a poor reporter; populations do better (Week 36).

<Step n={3} title="Processing cannot add information" minutes={30} />

If a downstream variable $Z$ is computed from $R$ alone, so that $S \to R \to Z$ forms a chain, then

$$
I(S; Z) \le I(S; R).
$$

This is the **data processing inequality**: no decoder, network or brain area can create information about the stimulus that its input did not carry. Processing can only keep it or lose it, though it can make the information easier to read out.

The decoder's single best guess throws away information that the counts contained, such as how confident the guess should be. Information is a ceiling on what any readout could achieve, not what a particular readout does achieve.

<Step n={4} title="What information does and does not tell you" minutes={15} />

Mutual information depends on the **stimulus distribution**: the same neuron conveys different amounts for different stimulus sets, so information values are only comparable under the same stimulus ensemble. It says how much, not **what**: two neurons with the same information may encode entirely different features. It says nothing about whether downstream neurons **use** the information. And it is hard to **estimate** from limited data, tomorrow's subject. Borst and Theunissen (1999, this week's deep read) review how these issues play out across sensory systems.

<Prove title="Mutual information is symmetric" task="Starting from $I(S; R) = H(R) - H(R \mid S)$, show that it equals $H(S) - H(S \mid R)$, using the chain rule $H(S, R) = H(S) + H(R \mid S) = H(R) + H(S \mid R)$.">

From the chain rule written both ways, $H(S) + H(R \mid S) = H(R) + H(S \mid R)$.

Rearranging: $H(R) - H(R \mid S) = H(S) - H(S \mid R)$.

So the reduction in uncertainty about the response from knowing the stimulus equals the reduction in uncertainty about the stimulus from knowing the response. Both equal $H(S) + H(R) - H(S, R)$, the amount by which the joint entropy falls short of the sum of the marginal entropies.

</Prove>
