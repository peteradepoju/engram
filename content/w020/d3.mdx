<Step n={1} title="The plug-in bias" minutes={30} />

The **plug-in** (or naive) estimator replaces probabilities by observed frequencies. Because rare outcomes are often missed and chance fluctuations look like structure, it underestimates entropy (Week 20 figure) and overestimates mutual information. For large samples the bias of the information is approximately

$$
\text{bias} \approx \frac{(S - 1)(R - 1)}{2N\ln 2}\ \text{bits},
$$

for $S$ stimuli, $R$ response bins and $N$ trials in total (Treves and Panzeri, 1995). It grows with the number of response categories and shrinks only like $1/N$, which is why measuring information in rich spike patterns from limited data is hard.

A neuron with no information at all appears to carry about half a bit, which answers the prediction: a substantial value, from nothing but sampling noise. With 8 stimuli the most any neuron could carry is 3 bits, so this bias is large enough to mislead.

<Step n={2} title="Corrections" minutes={25} />

Two widely used corrections.

- **Analytic**: subtract the bias formula (the Miller-Madow correction, applied to each entropy), counting only response bins that were actually observed.
- **Shuffle**: compute the information again after randomly shuffling the stimulus labels many times, which destroys any real relationship but keeps the sampling bias, and subtract the average shuffled value. Because shuffled information still contains the bias, this removes it without a formula. Its distribution also gives a permutation test (Week 18) for whether any information is present.

The naive estimate is too high at every sample size: about 1.1 bits with 10 trials per stimulus against a true 0.61, still 0.63 with 160. The shuffle correction overshoots in the other direction: 0.30 with 10 trials, 0.56 with 160. Shuffled data have no structure, so their responses spread over more response bins and their bias is larger than that of the real data; subtracting it removes too much. Each correction brackets the truth from one side, and with too few trials neither rescues the experiment: the honest answer is then a range, not a number.

<Step n={3} title="The direct method" minutes={35} />

For a time-varying stimulus, Strong and colleagues (1998, today's paper) measured information without choosing a code in advance. Show the same long stimulus many times. Cut the spike trains into words of $L$ bins.

- The **total entropy** $H_{\text{total}}$ is the entropy of all words, pooled over time and repeats: the variety of the neuron's responses.
- The **noise entropy** $H_{\text{noise}}$ is the entropy of the words at one fixed moment of the stimulus, across repeats, averaged over moments: the variability that is not caused by the stimulus.
- The **information** is $H_{\text{total}} - H_{\text{noise}}$, divided by the word duration for a rate in bits per second.

The two neurons fire about the same number of spikes, yet precise timing carries far more information, because the noise entropy across repeats is much lower when each spike lands at the same moment every time. Strong and colleagues found that the fly's motion-sensitive neuron H1 conveyed about half of its total entropy as information, at millisecond resolution: timing matters there.

<Step n={4} title="Choices that matter" minutes={15} />

The direct method still involves choices: the bin width $\Delta t$ (too coarse loses timing information; too fine creates many sparsely sampled words), the word length $L$ (longer words capture correlations between bins but multiply the number of possible words, $2^L$, and with it the bias), and the stimulus ensemble. Strong and colleagues handled the bias by extrapolating estimates computed on fractions of the data to infinite data, and by extrapolating in $1/L$. Modern estimators, such as the NSB method (Nemenman, Shafee and Bialek, 2002), use Bayesian priors over distributions to do better with less data.

<Prove title="Why the plug-in entropy is biased downwards" task="Use Jensen's inequality to show that the expected plug-in entropy is at most the true entropy, $\mathbb{E}[H(\hat{p})] \le H(p)$, where $\hat{p}$ are frequencies from N independent samples.">

Each frequency is unbiased: $\mathbb{E}[\hat{p}] = p$.

Entropy is a concave function of the probability vector (it is a sum of the concave terms $-x\log x$). Jensen's inequality for a concave function $H$ gives $\mathbb{E}[H(\hat{p})] \le H(\mathbb{E}[\hat{p}]) = H(p)$.

The fluctuations of $\hat{p}$ around $p$ therefore lower the average entropy. Mutual information is a difference of entropies, $H(S) + H(R) - H(S, R)$, and the joint entropy has the most cells and so the largest downward bias, which is why the information is biased upwards.

</Prove>
