<Step n={1} title="The Gaussian channel" minutes={25} />

A Gaussian signal $S$ with variance $P$ is sent through a channel that adds independent Gaussian noise with variance $N$: $R = S + \text{noise}$. The mutual information is

$$
I(S; R) = \tfrac{1}{2}\log_2\left(1 + \frac{P}{N}\right) \ \text{bits per sample},
$$

and this is also the channel's **capacity** under a power limit $P$: no input distribution does better (Shannon, 1948). Each doubling of the signal-to-noise ratio adds only about half a bit, so information is cheap at first and then expensive. For jointly Gaussian variables, information and correlation are tied: $I = -\tfrac{1}{2}\log_2(1 - \rho^2)$.

<Step n={2} title="Redundancy" minutes={25} />

When two neurons carry the same signal with independent noise, the best combination of their responses (their average, by Week 16's cue combination) has the noise variance halved, so its SNR doubles. If each alone has SNR 3 (1 bit), together they have SNR 6: $\tfrac{1}{2}\log_2 7 \approx 1.40$ bits, not 2. The difference is **redundancy**: information both neurons share. Redundant codes are robust to noise and loss, but wasteful; how much redundancy sensory populations carry, and whether it is a bug or a feature, is a long-running question.

<Step n={3} title="Efficient coding" minutes={20} />

Barlow (1961) proposed that early sensory processing should **reduce redundancy**: re-encode inputs so that neurons carry as much independent information as possible within their limits. Three predictions follow, each of which you have met.

- A single neuron with a limited range should equalise its output histogram, matching its response curve to the input's cumulative distribution (Laughlin, 1981; Week 13).
- A population receiving correlated inputs should **decorrelate** (whiten) them where the signal is strong, so that neurons do not repeat each other. Atick and Redlich (1990) derived the centre-surround receptive fields of the retina this way, with the twist that at low signal-to-noise ratio the optimal filter averages rather than whitens.
- For natural images, a sparse, independent code gives oriented, localised filters like V1's (Olshausen and Field, 1996; Week 13).

Efficient coding is a normative theory: it says what an optimal system would do under stated constraints, which makes it testable and also easy to over-claim, since the constraints are chosen by the theorist.

<Step n={4} title="The information bottleneck" minutes={35} />

Efficient coding asks for compression; but compression of what, for whom? The **information bottleneck** (Tishby, Pereira and Bialek, 2000, today's paper) adds relevance. Given an input $X$ and a variable $Y$ that matters, find a representation $T$ of $X$ that minimises

$$
I(X; T) - \beta\,I(T; Y):
$$

use as few bits about the input as possible, while keeping as much as possible about what matters. Small $\beta$ favours compression; large $\beta$ favours relevance. By the data processing inequality, $I(T; Y) \le I(X; Y)$: the representation can at best keep all the relevant information.

Compressing 3 bits of input into 1 bit keeps almost all of the relevant information, because the two groups differ only in irrelevant detail. The best summary is not the one that preserves the most about $X$, but the one that keeps what predicts $Y$. That is the bottleneck's idea of a good representation, and it has been proposed both for sensory neurons predicting the future of their input and for the layers of deep networks.

<Prove title="Information of a jointly Gaussian pair" task="For $R = S + Z$ with $S \sim \mathcal{N}(0, P)$ and independent noise $Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, N)$, use the differential entropy of a Gaussian, $h = \tfrac{1}{2}\log_2(2\pi e\sigma^2)$, to show that $I(S; R) = \tfrac{1}{2}\log_2(1 + P/N)$.">

$I(S; R) = h(R) - h(R \mid S)$. Since $R$ is Gaussian with variance $P + N$, $h(R) = \tfrac{1}{2}\log_2(2\pi e(P + N))$.

Given $S$, the only randomness in $R$ is the noise, so $h(R \mid S) = h(Z) = \tfrac{1}{2}\log_2(2\pi eN)$.

Subtracting, $I = \tfrac{1}{2}\log_2\frac{P + N}{N} = \tfrac{1}{2}\log_2(1 + P/N)$. The $2\pi e$ factors cancel, which is why information, unlike differential entropy, does not depend on the units of measurement.

</Prove>

<Key title="Before you go">
<ul>
<li>A Gaussian channel carries ½ log₂(1 + SNR) bits; extra signal buys information slowly.</li>
<li>Neurons that share a signal are redundant: together they carry less than the sum.</li>
<li>Efficient coding predicts equalisation, decorrelation and sparse codes.</li>
<li>The information bottleneck keeps only what is relevant, in as few bits as possible.</li>
