**Part A: count or timing?** A neuron responds to four stimuli. Each evokes about the same number of spikes, but the first spike comes at a different latency for each (10, 20, 30 or 40 ms, with 4 ms of jitter). With 50 trials per stimulus, compare the information in the spike count and in the first-spike latency (in 5 ms bins), correcting both by shuffling.

<PyCell code={String.raw`
  rng = np.random.default_rng(1999)
  def entropy(p):
      p = np.asarray(p, float); p = p[p > 0]
      return -np.sum(p * np.log2(p))
  def plugin_mi(s, r, S, R):
      J = np.zeros((S, R)); np.add.at(J, (s, r), 1); J /= J.sum()
      return entropy(J.sum(1)) + entropy(J.sum(0)) - entropy(J.ravel())

      return plugin_mi(s, r, S, R) - np.mean([plugin_mi(rng.permutation(s), r, S, R) for _ in range(n_shuf)])

  S, n_per = 4, 50

An analysis that only counted spikes would conclude this neuron says nothing. Information measures do not presuppose the code, but they only find information in the variables you give them: choosing the response variables is a scientific decision. Latency codes of this kind have been reported in the retina and somatosensory system.

Each neuron alone is useless; together they carry the stimulus perfectly. This is **synergy**, the opposite of Day 5's redundancy. It means that studying neurons one at a time can miss information entirely, and that a population's information is not the sum of its members'. Separating redundant, unique and synergistic information in populations is an active research area (partial information decomposition).

With output noise the equalising curve still wins: it spreads the common inputs across the output range, where the noise cannot blur them together, instead of crowding them into a small part of it. With very high noise the optimum changes, and the best curve becomes flatter, the same twist Atick and Redlich found for receptive fields.

<Prove title="1. Mutual information is a KL divergence" task="Show that $I(S; R) = \mathrm{KL}\left(p(s, r)\,\|\,p(s)p(r)\right)$, and deduce from Gibbs' inequality that $I(S; R) \ge 0$, with equality only for independence.">

By definition, $\mathrm{KL}(p(s, r)\,\|\,p(s)p(r)) = \sum_{s, r} p(s, r)\log_2\frac{p(s, r)}{p(s)p(r)}$, which is exactly the double-sum form of the mutual information (Day 2).

Gibbs' inequality (Day 4) says a KL divergence is non-negative and zero only when its two arguments are equal. So $I(S; R) \ge 0$, with $I = 0$ only when $p(s, r) = p(s)p(r)$ for all $s, r$: independence.

This also gives the most useful reading of mutual information: how many extra bits you would pay if you coded stimulus-response pairs assuming they were unrelated.

</Prove>

<Prove title="2. Synergy cannot be seen pair by pair" task="In Part B, show without a table that $I(S; R_1) = 0$ but $I(S; R_1, R_2) = 1$ bit.">

$R_1$ is a fair coin chosen independently of $S$, so $I(S; R_1) = 0$. $R_2 = S \oplus R_1$: for either value of $S$, $R_2$ is also a fair coin (it is $R_1$ or its flip), so $I(S; R_2) = 0$ too.

Given both responses, $S = R_1 \oplus R_2$ exactly, so $H(S \mid R_1, R_2) = 0$ and $I(S; R_1, R_2) = H(S) - 0 = 1$ bit.

Any analysis that adds up single-neuron information would report 0 bits for a pair that carries the stimulus perfectly. The information is in the relationship between the neurons.

</Prove>

Give Borst and Theunissen (1999) its third pass and write your critique under the five headings. Focus on one question: **does measuring how many bits a neuron carries tell us how the brain encodes information?** Consider that information depends on the stimulus ensemble and on the response variables chosen (Part A); that it says nothing about whether downstream neurons read the information (the data processing inequality); that single-neuron measures can miss population effects such as synergy (Part B); that estimates are biased with limited data (Day 3); and what has changed since 1999 with recordings of thousands of neurons.
