<Step n={1} title="Rates of change" minutes={20} />

A **differential equation** relates an unknown function to its derivatives. The simplest kind, a first-order equation,

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t} = f(x, t),
$$

says: wherever you are, at value $x$ and time $t$, the solution is changing at rate $f(x, t)$. Knowing the starting value $x(0)$ and the rule, the whole future is fixed. You can picture the rule as a **slope field**, a short line at every point showing the direction of travel, and a solution as a curve that follows the lines (Week 8's derivative, now as an instruction rather than a result).

<Step n={2} title="The membrane as a circuit" minutes={30} />

A patch of neural membrane is a thin insulating lipid layer (a **capacitor**, capacitance $C$) pierced by ion channels that let current leak through (a **resistor**, resistance $R$, or conductance $g_L = 1/R$), with the channels driving the voltage towards a resting potential $E_L$ set by ion concentrations. Inject a current $I$. Kirchhoff's law says the injected current must go somewhere: either into charging the capacitor, $C\,\mathrm{d}V/\mathrm{d}t$, or out through the leak, $(V - E_L)/R$:

$$
C\frac{\mathrm{d}V}{\mathrm{d}t} = -\frac{V - E_L}{R} + I \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \tau\frac{\mathrm{d}V}{\mathrm{d}t} = -(V - E_L) + RI, \qquad \tau = RC.
$$

This is the **membrane equation**. Hodgkin and Huxley (1952, this week's deep read) used exactly this circuit, with the leak split into sodium, potassium and leak conductances whose values change with voltage. With fixed conductances it is linear, and it describes a neuron below threshold remarkably well. Typical values: $C \approx 100$ pF and $R \approx 100$ MΩ give $\tau = 10$ ms.

<Step n={3} title="The exact solution" minutes={30} />

<Derivation title="Solving the membrane equation for a step of current">

<div>

Let the steady state be $V_\infty = E_L + RI$, where the right side vanishes. Write $u = V - V_\infty$; then $\tau\,\mathrm{d}u/\mathrm{d}t = -u$.

</div>

<div>

The function whose derivative is proportional to itself is the exponential: $u(t) = u(0)\,e^{-t/\tau}$, as you can check by differentiating.

</div>

<div>

So

$$
V(t) = V_\infty + (V(0) - V_\infty)\,e^{-t/\tau}.
$$

After one time constant the remaining gap is $e^{-1} \approx 37\%$ of the original; after $\tau\ln 10 \approx 2.3\tau$ it is 10%.

</div>

</Derivation>

That answers the prediction: about 46 ms, a little over two time constants. The time constant sets how quickly a neuron forgets its past input, and so the window over which it integrates: about 10 to 30 ms for many cortical neurons, much shorter when synaptic input opens many channels (Week 107).

<Step n={4} title="Measuring τ" minutes={25} />

Experimenters measure $\tau$ by injecting a small current step and fitting the voltage. Because $\log|V(t) - V_\infty| = \log|V(0) - V_\infty| - t/\tau$ is a straight line in $t$, a linear fit to the logarithm gives $-1/\tau$ as its slope, a trick you will reuse whenever something decays exponentially.

  rng = np.random.default_rng(1952)
  t = np.arange(0, 150, 0.1)
  V = -62 + (-70 + 62) * np.exp(-t / 15) + 0.3 * rng.normal(size=len(t))

The fit uses only the early part, where the gap is large compared with the noise: late in the decay, $V_\infty - V$ is tiny and its logarithm is dominated by noise. Fitting the first 40 ms instead pulls the estimate down to about 13 ms. A nonlinear least-squares fit of the exponential itself (Week 12) avoids that problem.

<Prove title="Linear superposition" task="Show that if $V_1$ solves $\tau\dot{V} = -V + I_1(t)$ and $V_2$ solves $\tau\dot{V} = -V + I_2(t)$, then $V_1 + V_2$ solves $\tau\dot{V} = -V + I_1 + I_2$. Why does this fail once a spiking threshold is added?">

Add the two equations: $\tau(\dot{V}_1 + \dot{V}_2) = -(V_1 + V_2) + I_1 + I_2$, which is the equation for $V_1 + V_2$ with input $I_1 + I_2$. (Here $V$ is measured from rest, so the resting potential drops out.)

So the response to a sum of inputs is the sum of the responses: the equation is **linear**. This is what lets tomorrow's lesson describe the response to any input by convolution with a single impulse response.

A threshold breaks it: whether the neuron spikes, and the reset after a spike, depend on whether the *total* voltage crosses threshold, which is not the sum of what each input alone would do. Two inputs that are each subthreshold can together cause a spike.

</Prove>

<Key title="Before you go">
<ul>
<li>A differential equation is a rule for the slope of its solution.</li>
<li>Kirchhoff's law for an RC circuit gives the membrane equation, τ dV/dt = −(V − E_L) + RI.</li>
<li>Its solution approaches V∞ exponentially, with time constant τ = RC.</li>
<li>τ is measured from the slope of log|V − V∞| against time.</li>
</ul>
