<Step n={1} title="The impulse response" minutes={25} />

Give the membrane equation $\tau\dot{V} = -V + RI(t)$ (with $V$ measured from rest) a very brief pulse of current delivering charge $q$ at time 0. The voltage jumps by $q/C$ and then decays: the **impulse response** is

$$
h(t) = \frac{1}{\tau}e^{-t/\tau} \quad (t \ge 0), \qquad h(t) = 0 \quad (t \lt 0),
$$

for a unit-area input to $RI$. Because the equation is linear and does not change over time, any input can be written as a sum of many tiny pulses, and the response is the sum of their shifted impulse responses:

$$
V(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{t} h(t - s)\,RI(s)\,\mathrm{d}s = (h * RI)(t).
$$

The membrane is a **filter**: a low-pass filter that smooths fast fluctuations and passes slow ones (Week 11's language). This is why linear-nonlinear models of neurons (Week 10) begin with a convolution.

<Step n={3} title="Synaptic potentials" minutes={25} />

A synapse does not deliver an instant pulse: its current rises and decays over a few milliseconds. Rall (1967, today's paper) modelled it with the **alpha function**,

$$
I_{\text{syn}}(t) \propto \frac{t}{\tau_s}e^{1 - t/\tau_s},
$$

which rises from zero, peaks at $t = \tau_s$, and decays. The resulting postsynaptic potential is the alpha current convolved with the membrane's impulse response: a smoothed, delayed version, which peaks later and lasts longer than the current that caused it. Its shape tells you about both the synapse and the membrane, and, through the dendrites, about where on the neuron the synapse sits.

<Step n={4} title="Resonance" minutes={30} />

Some neurons have a slow current (such as the h-current) that opposes changes in voltage. Together with the capacitor, it makes the membrane behave like a mass on a spring with friction, a **second-order** linear equation:

$$
\ddot{x} + 2\zeta\omega_0\dot{x} + \omega_0^2 x = u(t).
$$

Try $x = e^{\lambda t}$ with no input: $\lambda^2 + 2\zeta\omega_0\lambda + \omega_0^2 = 0$, so $\lambda = -\zeta\omega_0 \pm \omega_0\sqrt{\zeta^2 - 1}$. With light damping ($\zeta \lt 1$) the roots are complex, and the solution rings at about $\omega_0$ while decaying. Driven by $u = \cos\omega t$, the steady response has amplitude

$$
A(\omega) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)^2 + (2\zeta\omega_0\omega)^2}},
$$

largest near $\omega_0$: **resonance**. Neurons with subthreshold resonance respond best to inputs in a preferred frequency band, often theta (4 to 12 Hz), which may help them select rhythmic inputs (Hutcheon and Yarom, 2000).

<Prove title="The impulse response of the membrane" task="Show that $V(t) = \int_0^t \frac{1}{\tau}e^{-(t-s)/\tau}u(s)\,\mathrm{d}s$ solves $\tau\dot{V} = -V + u(t)$ with $V(0) = 0$.">

Write $V(t) = \frac{1}{\tau}e^{-t/\tau}\int_0^t e^{s/\tau}u(s)\,\mathrm{d}s$. Differentiate with the product rule and the fundamental theorem of calculus (Week 10):

$$
\dot{V} = -\frac{1}{\tau}V(t) + \frac{1}{\tau}e^{-t/\tau}\,e^{t/\tau}u(t) = -\frac{1}{\tau}V(t) + \frac{1}{\tau}u(t).
$$

Multiplying by $\tau$ gives $\tau\dot{V} = -V + u$, and at $t = 0$ the integral is empty, so $V(0) = 0$. The response is the input convolved with $h(t) = e^{-t/\tau}/\tau$.

</Prove>
