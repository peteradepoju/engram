<Step n={1} title="Euler's method" minutes={20} />

To solve $\dot{x} = f(x, t)$ numerically, chop time into steps of size $\Delta t$ and follow the current slope for one step:

$$
x_{n+1} = x_n + \Delta t\,f(x_n, t_n).
$$

This is **Euler's method** (forward Euler). Each step makes an error of order $\Delta t^2$ (the curvature it ignores, from Taylor's theorem in Week 9), and over a fixed time $T$ there are $T/\Delta t$ steps, so the total error is of order $\Delta t$: halving the step halves the error. It is a **first-order** method. It is also how a residual network updates its state, $x \leftarrow x + f(x)$, the observation behind neural ODEs (Chen and colleagues, 2018, today's paper).

<Step n={2} title="Order of accuracy and Runge-Kutta" minutes={35} />

Better methods sample the slope several times per step and combine the samples to cancel errors. The classic **fourth-order Runge-Kutta** (RK4) method:

$$
\begin{aligned}
k_1 &= f(x_n),\quad k_2 = f(x_n + \tfrac{\Delta t}{2}k_1),\quad k_3 = f(x_n + \tfrac{\Delta t}{2}k_2),\quad k_4 = f(x_n + \Delta t\,k_3),\\
x_{n+1} &= x_n + \tfrac{\Delta t}{6}(k_1 + 2k_2 + 2k_3 + k_4).
\end{aligned}
$$

Its global error is of order $\Delta t^4$: halving the step cuts the error sixteenfold. It costs four evaluations of $f$ per step, but for smooth problems it reaches a given accuracy with far fewer evaluations than Euler. On a log-log plot of error against step size, a method of order $p$ is a straight line of slope $p$.

<Step n={3} title="Stability" minutes={25} />

Accuracy is not the only concern. For the decay $\dot{V} = -V/\tau$, one Euler step multiplies $V$ by $1 - \Delta t/\tau$. If $\Delta t > \tau$ this factor is negative, so the solution oscillates in sign, which the true solution never does; if $\Delta t > 2\tau$ its size exceeds 1, so the "decay" grows without bound. That answers the prediction: with $\Delta t = 25$ ms and $\tau = 10$ ms the factor is $-1.5$, and the simulation explodes. In general, Euler is **stable** for $\dot{x} = \lambda x$ only when $|1 + \lambda\Delta t| \lt 1$, so the fastest process in a model sets the largest usable step.

The **backward** (implicit) Euler method evaluates the slope at the *new* point instead, $x_{n+1} = x_n + \Delta t\,f(x_{n+1})$. For the decay this gives $V_{n+1} = V_n/(1 + \Delta t/\tau)$, which shrinks for every step size: it is unconditionally stable, at the price of solving an equation at each step (easy for linear $f$).

Backward Euler is stable but not magic: with steps much larger than $\tau$ it still gets the timing of the decay wrong. Stability keeps a simulation from exploding; accuracy still needs steps small compared with the processes you care about.

<Step n={4} title="Stiff systems" minutes={25} />

A system is **stiff** when it contains processes on very different timescales: for example a sodium activation gate relaxing in 0.1 ms alongside a membrane with a 20 ms time constant. Forward Euler must use steps below about 0.2 ms to stay stable, even after the fast gate has settled and nothing fast is happening. Implicit methods remove that restriction, and adaptive solvers change the step size automatically, taking small steps during a spike and large ones in between.

The semi-implicit scheme takes steps fifty times larger than the reference and lands in the same place. This idea, treating each gating variable implicitly because its equation is linear in the gate, is the basis of the exponential Euler and Crank-Nicolson schemes used in neuron simulators such as NEURON and Brian.

<Prove title="Euler's stability limit" task="For $\dot{x} = \lambda x$ with real $\lambda \lt 0$, show that forward Euler's iterates satisfy $x_n = (1 + \lambda\Delta t)^n x_0$, and that they decay only if $\Delta t \lt 2/|\lambda|$. Then show that backward Euler's iterates decay for every $\Delta t > 0$.">

Each forward step is $x_{n+1} = x_n + \Delta t\,\lambda x_n = (1 + \lambda\Delta t)x_n$, so $x_n = (1 + \lambda\Delta t)^n x_0$. This decays exactly when $|1 + \lambda\Delta t| \lt 1$, that is $-2 \lt \lambda\Delta t \lt 0$, or $\Delta t \lt 2/|\lambda|$. With $\lambda = -1/\tau$ the limit is $2\tau$.

Backward Euler gives $x_{n+1} = x_n + \Delta t\,\lambda x_{n+1}$, so $x_{n+1} = x_n/(1 - \lambda\Delta t)$. Since $\lambda \lt 0$, the denominator exceeds 1 for every $\Delta t > 0$, and the iterates always decay, without changing sign.

</Prove>

<Key title="Before you go">
<ul>
<li>Euler follows the current slope; its error is first order.</li>
<li>RK4 samples the slope four times per step and is fourth order.</li>
<li>Explicit methods explode if the step is too large for the fastest process.</li>
<li>Implicit methods handle stiff systems; check any simulation by halving the step.</li>
</ul>
