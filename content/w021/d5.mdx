<Step n={1} title="The leaky integrate-and-fire neuron" minutes={20} />

Measure voltage from rest, so $V = 0$ is the resting potential. Below threshold the neuron is the RC membrane of Day 1:

$$
\tau\frac{\mathrm{d}V}{\mathrm{d}t} = -V + RI(t).
$$

When $V$ reaches the threshold $\theta$, a spike is recorded, $V$ is reset to 0 and held there for a refractory period $t_\text{ref}$. That is all. The spike's shape is not modelled; the model claims only that the timing of spikes is what matters, and that a neuron's subthreshold voltage is roughly linear. Lapicque proposed the idea in 1907, long before anyone knew how spikes worked. Throughout today, $\tau = 20$ ms, $\theta = 15$ mV and $t_\text{ref} = 2$ ms, and input is written as $RI$ in millivolts.

<Step n={2} title="The f-I curve, exactly" minutes={30} />

With constant input $RI$, the voltage after a reset climbs towards $RI$ exponentially: $V(t) = RI(1 - e^{-t/\tau})$. If $RI \le \theta$ it never arrives, and the neuron is silent. Otherwise it reaches $\theta$ at the time $T$ where $RI(1 - e^{-T/\tau}) = \theta$:

<Derivation>
<div>

Rearrange for the exponential: $e^{-T/\tau} = 1 - \theta/RI = (RI - \theta)/RI$.

</div>
<div>

Take logs: $T = \tau\ln\dfrac{RI}{RI - \theta}$.

</div>
<div>

Each interspike interval is this charging time plus the refractory period, so the firing rate is

$$
f(I) = \frac{1}{t_\text{ref} + \tau\ln\dfrac{RI}{RI - \theta}}, \qquad RI > \theta,
$$

and 0 otherwise. The smallest current that makes the neuron fire, $RI = \theta$, is called the **rheobase**.

</div>
</Derivation>

The curve rises steeply just above rheobase (the logarithm blows up there, so the rate climbs from zero with infinite slope) and then becomes nearly linear, saturating at $1/t_\text{ref}$ for huge inputs.

  import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
  tau, theta, tref, dt = 20.0, 15.0, 2.0, 0.1          # ms, mV, ms, ms

  def lif_rate(RI, T=1000.0):
      V, refr, n = 0.0, 0.0, 0
      for _ in range(int(T / dt)):
          if refr > 0:
              refr -= dt; continue
          V = V + dt / tau * (-V + RI)                                       # one Euler step of tau dV/dt = -V + RI

      out[ok] = 1000 / (tref + tau * np.log(RI[ok] / (RI[ok] - theta)))                                     # spikes per second (times are in ms)
      return out

<Step n={3} title="Noise smooths the threshold" minutes={30} />

Real neurons receive thousands of synaptic inputs, and their membrane voltage fluctuates. Model the fluctuations as white noise added to each step: $V \leftarrow V + \frac{\Delta t}{\tau}(-V + RI) + \sigma\sqrt{2\Delta t/\tau}\,\xi$, with $\xi$ a standard normal draw, which makes the free voltage fluctuate with standard deviation $\sigma$ around its mean (Week 24 explains why the square root is there). Now a neuron below rheobase can still fire, whenever a fluctuation carries it over threshold. This is the **fluctuation-driven** regime, and cortex in an awake animal is thought to sit largely within it.

<Step n={4} title="Adaptation" minutes={25} />

Inject a step of current into a pyramidal cell in cortex and it fires fast at first, then slows. Calcium-activated potassium currents build up with each spike and pull the voltage down. Model this with an adaptation variable $w$ that jumps by $b$ at each spike and decays with time constant $\tau_w$:

$$
\tau\frac{\mathrm{d}V}{\mathrm{d}t} = -V + RI - w, \qquad \tau_w\frac{\mathrm{d}w}{\mathrm{d}t} = -w, \qquad w \to w + b \text{ at a spike}.
$$

Brette and Gerstner (2005, today's paper) combined an adaptation variable like this with an exponential spike-initiation term to build the adaptive exponential integrate-and-fire model, which with different parameter settings reproduces many of the firing patterns of real cortical neurons and predicts their spike times well.

<Prove title="The f-I curve far above rheobase" task="Show that for $RI \gg \theta$, $\tau\ln\frac{RI}{RI - \theta} \approx \tau\theta/RI$, so that without a refractory period the rate grows linearly, $f \approx RI/(\tau\theta)$, and with one it saturates at $1/t_\text{ref}$.">

Write $\ln\frac{RI}{RI - \theta} = -\ln\left(1 - \frac{\theta}{RI}\right)$. For small $u = \theta/RI$, $-\ln(1 - u) = u + u^2/2 + \cdots \approx u$.

So the charging time is $T \approx \tau\theta/RI$: the voltage rises at its initial slope $RI/\tau$ almost all the way, and the leak barely matters.

With $t_\text{ref} = 0$ the rate is $1/T \approx RI/(\tau\theta)$, linear in the input. With a refractory period, $f = 1/(t_\text{ref} + \tau\theta/RI)$, which tends to $1/t_\text{ref}$ as $RI \to \infty$: the neuron cannot fire faster than once per refractory period.

</Prove>

<Key title="Before you go">
<ul>
<li>The LIF neuron is the RC membrane plus threshold, reset and refractory period.</li>
<li>Its f-I curve for constant input is exact: zero below rheobase, then a logarithmic rise and saturation at 1/t_ref.</li>
<li>Noise smooths the threshold, so neurons fire, slowly, below rheobase.</li>
<li>An adaptation variable makes firing slow down during a sustained input.</li>
</ul>
