<Step n={1} title="Flows on a line" minutes={25} />

A one-variable system $\dot{x} = f(x)$ can be pictured as a flow along a line. At each point $x$, the value $f(x)$ is a velocity: positive means $x$ moves right, negative means it moves left. So plot $f(x)$ against $x$ and you can read off the whole behaviour, for every starting point, without solving anything.

Points where $f(x^*) = 0$ are **fixed points**: start there and you stay there. Whether you return after a small nudge depends on the flow nearby. If $f$ crosses zero going downwards ($f'(x^*) \lt 0$), the flow points towards $x^*$ from both sides and the fixed point is **stable**. If it crosses going upwards ($f'(x^*) > 0$), the flow points away and it is **unstable**. Trajectories move monotonically towards a stable fixed point or off to infinity; on a line they can never overshoot or oscillate, because to turn round they would need to pass through a point where $f = 0$, and that point is a fixed point they can never leave.

<Step n={2} title="A population that excites itself" minutes={30} />

Describe a population of excitatory neurons by its average firing rate $r$, scaled so that 1 is the maximum. The population receives external input $I$ and its own output through recurrent connections of strength $w$, and its rate relaxes towards a sigmoid function of the total input:

$$
\tau\frac{\mathrm{d}r}{\mathrm{d}t} = -r + F(wr + I), \qquad F(u) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-(u - 4)}}.
$$

The sigmoid is a smoothed f-I curve (Week 21). Throughout today, $\tau = 10$ ms.

With weak recurrence ($w = 2$) there is one stable state, a low rate of about 0.02. With $w = 8$ there are three fixed points: a low stable state at 0.021, an unstable one at exactly 0.5 (where $8r - 4 = 0$ puts the sigmoid at its midpoint), and a high stable state at 0.979. Strong recurrent excitation has created a second stable state, in which the population's own output keeps it firing.

<Step n={3} title="Linear stability and timescales" minutes={25} />

Near a fixed point, write $r = r^* + \delta$ with $\delta$ small. A first-order Taylor expansion (Week 8) gives

$$
\tau\dot{\delta} = f(r^* + \delta) \approx f(r^*) + f'(r^*)\delta = f'(r^*)\delta,
$$

which is the membrane equation of Week 21 with solution $\delta(t) = \delta(0)e^{f'(r^*)t/\tau}$. So the slope is not just a sign: a stable fixed point pulls perturbations back with time constant $\tau/|f'(r^*)|$, and a shallow crossing means slow recovery. This is **linearisation**, and it is the tool for the rest of the week.

<Step n={4} title="A switch that remembers" minutes={25} />

Two stable states separated by an unstable one make the population a switch. The unstable fixed point is a threshold: start below it and the rate falls to the low state; start above it and it climbs to the high one. A brief input that pushes the rate past the threshold switches the population on, and it stays on after the input ends, because the high state is self-sustaining. Persistent activity of this kind, in which neurons keep firing during a delay after a stimulus has gone, is seen in prefrontal cortex during working memory tasks, and recurrent excitation is one of its proposed mechanisms.

<Prove title="Stability from the slope" task="For $\dot x = f(x)$ with $f(x^*) = 0$ and $f'(x^*) \ne 0$, show that a small perturbation $\delta$ obeys $\dot\delta \approx f'(x^*)\delta$, and deduce that $x^*$ is stable if $f'(x^*) \lt 0$ and unstable if $f'(x^*) > 0$. Then explain why a one-variable system cannot oscillate.">

Let $x = x^* + \delta$. Then $\dot\delta = \dot x = f(x^* + \delta) = f(x^*) + f'(x^*)\delta + O(\delta^2) = f'(x^*)\delta + O(\delta^2)$.

Dropping the small quadratic term, $\delta(t) = \delta(0)e^{f'(x^*)t}$, which shrinks to zero if $f'(x^*) \lt 0$ and grows if $f'(x^*) > 0$. (If $f'(x^*) = 0$ the linear term vanishes and the quadratic one decides: this is where bifurcations happen, Week 23.)

For oscillation, $x$ would have to go up and later come down, so $\dot x$ would have to change sign. Since $\dot x = f(x)$ depends only on $x$, and $f$ is continuous, the trajectory would have to pass through a point where $f(x) = 0$. But that is a fixed point, and by uniqueness of solutions, a trajectory cannot reach a fixed point in finite time and pass through it. So trajectories on a line are monotonic. Oscillations need at least two dimensions (Day 2).

</Prove>
