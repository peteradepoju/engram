<Step n={1} title="Nullclines" minutes={20} />

For a system $\dot x = f(x, y)$, $\dot y = g(x, y)$, the **$x$-nullcline** is the curve where $f = 0$, where the flow is purely vertical, and the **$y$-nullcline** is where $g = 0$, where the flow is purely horizontal. Fixed points are where the nullclines cross. Drawing them is usually the fastest way to understand a non-linear system: they divide the plane into regions in which the flow points in a fixed general direction (up-right, down-left and so on), and their shape shows how many fixed points there are and how they appear or vanish as parameters change.

<Step n={2} title="Linearisation and the Jacobian" minutes={25} />

Near a fixed point $(x^*, y^*)$, write the state as the fixed point plus a small deviation $\boldsymbol\delta$. A first-order Taylor expansion of both equations gives a linear system,

$$
\dot{\boldsymbol\delta} \approx J\boldsymbol\delta, \qquad J = \begin{pmatrix}\partial f/\partial x & \partial f/\partial y \\ \partial g/\partial x & \partial g/\partial y\end{pmatrix}_{(x^*, y^*)},
$$

where $J$ is the **Jacobian**: the matrix of partial derivatives (Week 9) evaluated at the fixed point. Its trace and determinant classify the fixed point exactly as on Day 2. A theorem of Hartman and Grobman guarantees that this classification is correct for the non-linear system near the fixed point, provided no eigenvalue has zero real part; when one does, the linearisation is inconclusive, and that is where the bifurcations of Week 23 happen.

<Step n={3} title="The Wilson-Cowan model" minutes={30} />

Wilson and Cowan (1972) described a patch of cortex by the mean rates of its excitatory and inhibitory populations:

$$
\tau_E\dot r_E = -r_E + F_E(w_{EE}r_E - w_{EI}r_I + I_E), \qquad \tau_I\dot r_I = -r_I + F_I(w_{IE}r_E - w_{II}r_I + I_I),
$$

with sigmoid activation functions $F_E$ and $F_I$. The parameters below are those of the Neuromatch Academy tutorial: time is in units of $\tau_E$, and the activation functions are shifted so that $F(0) = 0$.

<PyCell code={String.raw`
  import numpy as np
  import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
  tE, aE, thE, tI, aI, thI = 1.0, 1.2, 2.8, 2.0, 1.0, 4.0
  wEE, wEI, wIE, wII = 9.0, 4.0, 13.0, 11.0
  def Fs(x, a, th): return 1 / (1 + np.exp(-a * (x - th))) - 1 / (1 + np.exp(a * th))
  def wc(x, IE=0.0, II=0.0):
      rE, rI = x
      return np.array([(-rE + Fs(wEE * rE - wEI * rI + IE, aE, thE)) / tE,
                       (-rI + Fs(wIE * rE - wII * rI + II, aI, thI)) / tI])
  def classify(A):
      T, D = np.trace(A), np.linalg.det(A)
      if D < 0: return "saddle"
      if T ** 2 >= 4 * D: return "stable node" if T < 0 else "unstable node"
      return "stable spiral" if T < 0 else "unstable spiral"
  print("flow at (0.5, 0.5):", wc(np.array([0.5, 0.5])).round(3))

The model has three fixed points. Rest, at (0, 0), is a weakly damped stable spiral. The middle one, at (0.337, 0.168), is a saddle with eigenvalues 1.057 and −0.873: it is the threshold between the two basins, as the unstable point was on Day 1, but in two dimensions the boundary is a curve, the saddle's stable direction extended. The high state, at (0.938, 0.673), is a stable node. Note $J_{EE}$ at each: −0.65 at rest, +1.52 at the saddle and −0.71 at the high state. At this high state the excitatory population would be stable on its own, because its sigmoid is nearly saturated there; at the saddle, it would not.

<Step n={4} title="The inhibition-stabilised network" minutes={30} />

The top-left entry of the Jacobian, $J_{EE}$, says what the excitatory population would do on its own if inhibition were frozen. If $J_{EE} > 0$, excitation alone is unstable, and the network is held in check only by inhibition: an **inhibition-stabilised network** (ISN). Tsodyks, Skaggs, Sejnowski and McNaughton (1997) pointed out a strange consequence. Give the inhibitory population extra drive. It fires more at first and suppresses the excitatory cells; but because excitation is self-amplifying, the excitatory rate falls so far that the excitatory input to the inhibitory cells drops by more than the extra drive added. The inhibitory rate ends **lower** than before.

You can derive this from the linearised steady state. With extra input $\Delta\mathbf{I}$, the steady-state change is $\Delta\mathbf{r} = -J^{-1}\Delta\mathbf{I}$ (scaled by the time constants), and for extra drive to the inhibitory cells only, the sign of $\Delta r_I$ turns out to be the sign of $-J_{EE}$: it falls exactly when the network is an ISN.

With weak recurrent excitation ($w_{EE} = 0.5$), extra drive of 0.5 to the inhibitory cells raises their rate by 0.033 and lowers the excitatory rate by 0.167, as intuition says. With $w_{EE} = 2.2$ the excitatory rate falls by more, 0.385, and the inhibitory rate falls too, by 0.185, despite its extra drive. The simulation matches the linear prediction to four decimal places, because the threshold-linear network is exactly linear while both rates stay positive. That answers the prediction: in an inhibition-stabilised network the inhibitory rate goes down.

<Prove title="The sign of the paradox" task="For the linear steady state $(I - W)\Delta\mathbf{r} = (0, \Delta I)^\top$ with $W = \begin{pmatrix}w_{EE} & -w_{EI} \\ w_{IE} & -w_{II}\end{pmatrix}$, show that $\Delta r_I = (1 - w_{EE})\Delta I/\det(I - W)$, and deduce that if the network is stable, the inhibitory rate falls exactly when $w_{EE} > 1$.">

Write $M = I - W = \begin{pmatrix}1 - w_{EE} & w_{EI} \\ -w_{IE} & 1 + w_{II}\end{pmatrix}$. By the 2 × 2 inverse formula, $M^{-1} = \frac{1}{\det M}\begin{pmatrix}1 + w_{II} & -w_{EI} \\ w_{IE} & 1 - w_{EE}\end{pmatrix}$.

So $\Delta\mathbf{r} = M^{-1}(0, \Delta I)^\top$ gives $\Delta r_E = -w_{EI}\Delta I/\det M$ and $\Delta r_I = (1 - w_{EE})\Delta I/\det M$.

Stability of the linear network needs $\det M > 0$ (the determinant of the Jacobian, up to positive time-constant factors, must be positive). Then $\Delta r_E \lt 0$ always, as expected, and $\Delta r_I$ has the sign of $1 - w_{EE}$: negative when $w_{EE} > 1$, that is, when the excitatory population on its own would be unstable.

</Prove>
