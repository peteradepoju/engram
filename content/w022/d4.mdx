<Step n={1} title="The model" minutes={20} />

FitzHugh (1961, today's paper) noticed that in the Hodgkin-Huxley equations, the voltage $V$ and the sodium activation $m$ change fast, while sodium inactivation $h$ and potassium activation $n$ change slowly. Lumping the fast pair into one variable $v$ and the slow pair into one recovery variable $w$, he wrote

$$
\dot v = v - \frac{v^3}{3} - w + I, \qquad \dot w = \varepsilon(v + a - bw),
$$

with $a = 0.7$, $b = 0.8$ and $\varepsilon = 0.08$. The cubic gives the fast positive feedback of sodium channels: a small rise in $v$ makes $v$ rise faster. The slow variable $w$ builds up when $v$ is high and pulls it back down, like potassium activation and sodium inactivation. The small $\varepsilon$ makes $w$ about ten times slower than $v$. Nagumo and colleagues built the same equations as an electronic circuit in 1962, which is why the model carries both names.

The $v$-nullcline is the cubic $w = v - v^3/3 + I$ and the $w$-nullcline is the straight line $w = (v + a)/b$. Their crossing is the resting state.

The resting state sits at $v \approx -1.20$, $w \approx -0.62$, on the left branch of the cubic, where its slope $1 - v^2$ is negative. Its Jacobian has trace −0.503 and determinant 0.108, so it is a stable spiral: small disturbances die away with a slight wobble. Linearisation says nothing more. The interesting behaviour comes from disturbances that are not small.

<Step n={3} title="Threshold and the all-or-none spike" minutes={30} />

The middle branch of the cubic, where its slope is positive, is where the fast positive feedback lives. A kick that leaves $v$ to the left of it lets $v$ fall straight back to the left branch. A kick that carries $v$ past it sends $v$ racing right, to the far branch of the cubic, because $w$ is too slow to stop it. On the right branch $w$ slowly grows, sliding the state up until it falls off the top knee of the cubic and races back to the left branch, then creeps down to rest. That journey is the spike.

Kicks of 0.1 to 0.5 give peaks that rise gently from −1.1 to −0.67, the voltage simply relaxing back. Between 0.5 and 0.6 the response jumps to a full spike peaking at 1.71, and larger kicks barely change it (1.83 at a kick of 1.5). The bisection puts the threshold at a kick of 0.556. That answers the prediction: the response jumps. Strictly, FitzHugh showed that there is a very narrow band of intermediate responses around the threshold, which he called a quasi-threshold, but it is far narrower than the noise in any real neuron, so in practice the spike is all or none.

<Step n={4} title="From rest to rhythm" minutes={30} />

Increasing the input $I$ lifts the cubic nullcline, so the crossing slides right along the line $w = (v + a)/b$. While the crossing is on the left branch of the cubic, rest is stable. When it reaches the region near the knee where the cubic's slope $1 - v^2$ exceeds $\varepsilon b$, the trace of the Jacobian, $1 - v^2 - \varepsilon b$, becomes positive, the stable spiral turns into an unstable one, and the trajectory spirals out onto a large closed orbit: repetitive firing. The exact input where the trace crosses zero is where the neuron's behaviour changes qualitatively, a **bifurcation**; this kind, where a complex pair of eigenvalues crosses the imaginary axis, is called a Hopf bifurcation, the subject of Week 23.

The trace crosses zero at $I = 0.3313$. Below it, at $I = 0.25$, a small nudge dies away and $v$ settles to rest (its late swing is about $3 \times 10^{-8}$). Above it, at $I = 0.4$, the same nudge grows into sustained spiking, with $v$ swinging over 3.8 units every cycle. Push $I$ much higher, to about 1.42, and the crossing moves past the right knee onto the right branch, where rest is stable again: the neuron is stuck in a depolarised state, a model of depolarisation block.

<Prove title="Why rest destabilises near the knee" task="Show that the trace of the FitzHugh-Nagumo Jacobian at any point is $1 - v^2 - \varepsilon b$ and its determinant is $\varepsilon(1 - b(1 - v^2))$, and deduce that a resting state on the left branch with $v^2 > 1$ is always stable.">

The Jacobian is $J = \begin{pmatrix}1 - v^2 & -1 \\ \varepsilon & -\varepsilon b\end{pmatrix}$, so $T = 1 - v^2 - \varepsilon b$ and $D = -\varepsilon b(1 - v^2) + \varepsilon = \varepsilon(1 - b(1 - v^2))$.

If $v^2 > 1$ then $1 - v^2 \lt 0$, so $T \lt -\varepsilon b \lt 0$, and $D > \varepsilon > 0$. Both conditions for stability (Day 2) hold.

Instability needs $T > 0$, that is $1 - v^2 > \varepsilon b$: the resting state must lie on the rising middle part of the cubic, where its slope exceeds the small number $\varepsilon b$. Because $\varepsilon$ is small, this happens almost as soon as the crossing passes the knee at $v = -1$.

</Prove>

<Key title="Before you go">
<ul>
<li>FitzHugh-Nagumo keeps a fast voltage and a slow recovery variable; its nullclines are a cubic and a line.</li>
<li>Rest on the left branch is a stable spiral; linearisation cannot see the spike.</li>
<li>The middle branch of the cubic is the threshold, and the response jumps from small to full spike across it.</li>
<li>Raising the input moves rest onto the middle branch, the trace turns positive, and the neuron fires rhythmically.</li>
