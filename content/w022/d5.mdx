<Step n={1} title="Tuned feedback" minutes={25} />

Look to one side and hold your gaze there. The motor neurons driving your eye muscles must keep firing at a rate that encodes the eye's position, long after the brief burst that moved it. The brainstem computes this position by integrating the velocity commands, and holds the result for many seconds, although each neuron involved forgets in tens of milliseconds. How?

The simplest answer is positive feedback. A unit with time constant $\tau$ that feeds its output back with weight $w$ obeys $\tau\dot r = -r + wr + I = -(1 - w)r + I$. Its effective time constant is $\tau/(1 - w)$: as $w$ approaches 1 the eigenvalue $(w - 1)/\tau$ approaches zero and the memory grows without limit. At exactly $w = 1$ the unit is a perfect integrator, $\tau\dot r = I$. This is a fixed point that is neither attracting nor repelling along one direction, an eigenvalue of zero.

<Step n={2} title="A line attractor" minutes={25} />

With many neurons, the same idea gives a **line attractor**. Take two neurons that excite each other equally, with $W = \begin{pmatrix}0.5 & 0.5 \\ 0.5 & 0.5\end{pmatrix}$. Its eigenvalues are 1, with eigenvector $(1, 1)$, and 0, with eigenvector $(1, -1)$. So in $\tau\dot{\mathbf r} = -\mathbf r + W\mathbf r + \mathbf I$, the component along $(1, 1)$ has eigenvalue zero and integrates, while the component along $(1, -1)$ decays with time constant $\tau$. Every point on the line $r_1 = r_2$ is a fixed point, and the network quickly returns to the line after any disturbance and then stays wherever on the line it was put.

<Step n={3} title="Noise on the line" minutes={25} />

A line attractor has a price. Along the line nothing pulls the state back, so every random fluctuation is integrated too, and the stored value performs a random walk: its variance grows in proportion to time. Off the line, fluctuations are pulled back, and their variance settles to a constant. This is one reason working memories for continuous quantities, such as a remembered location, degrade steadily with delay, and it is the first of the stochastic processes of Week 24.

<Step n={4} title="Opening the black box" minutes={30} />

A trained recurrent network is a large non-linear dynamical system, $\dot{\mathbf x} = F(\mathbf x)$, and its weights alone say little. Sussillo and Barak (2013) suggested asking where it rests: search for points where the speed $q(\mathbf x) = \tfrac{1}{2}\lVert F(\mathbf x)\rVert^2$ is zero or nearly so, by gradient descent on $q$ from many starting states, then linearise around each. Maheswaranathan and colleagues (2019, today's paper) did this for networks trained to judge whether movie reviews were positive or negative, and found in every architecture an approximate line attractor: each word pushes the state along the line, and the final position is the verdict.

Here you do the same for a 20-neuron network $\dot{\mathbf x} = -\mathbf x + W\tanh(\mathbf x)$ whose weights hide a line attractor along an unknown direction $\mathbf u$, mixed with random connections.

Of 40 searches, 24 end at slow points, and they all lie on one straight line through the origin: its direction matches the hidden $\mathbf u$ to four decimal places. The points spread from about −0.44 to 0.49 along it. Further out, the tanh saturates, the feedback along $\mathbf u$ falls below 1 and the state drifts back, so the network's line attractor is a segment, approximate like the trained networks' ones. Without knowing $\mathbf u$ in advance, the search found it from the dynamics alone.

<Prove title="Why the component along the line integrates" task="For $\tau\dot{\mathbf r} = -\mathbf r + W\mathbf r + \mathbf I(t)$ with $W$ symmetric, $W\mathbf u = \mathbf u$ and $\lVert\mathbf u\rVert = 1$, show that $a(t) = \mathbf u^\top\mathbf r(t)$ satisfies $\tau\dot a = \mathbf u^\top\mathbf I(t)$, so that $a(t) = a(0) + \frac{1}{\tau}\int_0^t\mathbf u^\top\mathbf I(s)\,\mathrm ds$.">

Multiply the equation on the left by $\mathbf u^\top$: $\tau\dot a = -\mathbf u^\top\mathbf r + \mathbf u^\top W\mathbf r + \mathbf u^\top\mathbf I$.

Because $W$ is symmetric, $\mathbf u^\top W = (W\mathbf u)^\top = \mathbf u^\top$, so the middle term is $\mathbf u^\top\mathbf r = a$, which cancels the leak $-a$.

What remains is $\tau\dot a = \mathbf u^\top\mathbf I(t)$, and integrating both sides gives the result. The leak and the feedback cancel exactly along $\mathbf u$; any mistuning ($W\mathbf u = (1 - \epsilon)\mathbf u$) leaves a leak $-\epsilon a$ and a memory time of $\tau/\epsilon$.

</Prove>
