<Step n={1} title="The saddle-node bifurcation" minutes={25} />

Consider $\dot x = r + x^2$, where $r$ is a parameter. For $r \lt 0$ there are two fixed points, $x^* = \pm\sqrt{-r}$; the slope $2x^*$ makes the left one stable and the right one unstable. As $r$ rises to 0 they approach each other, merge at $x = 0$ and, for $r > 0$, vanish: $\dot x$ is positive everywhere and $x$ runs off. A stable and an unstable fixed point have annihilated each other. This is a **saddle-node bifurcation** (in one dimension the "saddle" is the unstable point).

Its importance is that it is generic. Near the point where any smooth curve $f(x)$ just touches zero, a Taylor expansion looks like $r + x^2$ after rescaling, so every saddle-node, in any system, behaves locally like this one. That is why $\dot x = r + x^2$ is called the **normal form**.

<Step n={2} title="Hysteresis" minutes={30} />

Return to Week 22's population, $\tau\dot r = -r + F(wr + I)$ with $w = 8$. Collect the fixed points for every input $I$ and you get a **bifurcation diagram**: an S-shaped curve whose middle part is unstable. Its two folds are saddle-node bifurcations. Along the fixed-point curve, $I = F^{-1}(r) - wr = 4 + \ln\frac{r}{1 - r} - wr$, and the folds are where $\mathrm{d}I/\mathrm{d}r = 0$, that is $\frac{1}{r(1 - r)} = w$.

The folds are at $I = \pm 1.066$. Sweeping upwards, the population stays low until $I = 1.129$ and then jumps; sweeping downwards it stays high until $I = -1.129$. The jumps come slightly beyond the folds because the sweep, though slow, keeps moving while the state escapes through the bottleneck the folds leave. That answers the prediction: the population jumps back at a much lower input, and between the two folds its state depends on its history. This is **hysteresis**.

<Step n={3} title="Critical slowing down" minutes={25} />

Approaching a saddle-node, the stable fixed point and its unstable partner draw together, and the slope of $f$ at the stable one shrinks towards zero. By Week 22's linearisation, the recovery time from a small disturbance is $1/|f'(x^*)|$, so the system recovers ever more slowly. In the normal form, $f'(x^*) = -2\sqrt{-r}$ and the recovery time is $1/(2\sqrt{-r})$, which diverges as $r \to 0$. This **critical slowing down** has been proposed as an early warning of abrupt transitions, from lake ecosystems to epileptic seizures.

<Step n={4} title="Firing at any rate" minutes={25} />

Hodgkin (1948, today's paper) found that some crab axons, his class 1, could fire repetitively at arbitrarily low rates, with frequency rising smoothly from zero as the current increased. Others, class 2, jumped straight to a high rate, like the Hodgkin-Huxley model in Week 21's Forge. A saddle-node bifurcation explains class 1.

Picture the phase of a neuron as a point on a circle: rest is a stable fixed point on the circle, with an unstable one (threshold) nearby. As the input rises they collide and vanish, and the phase now travels round the circle forever: repetitive spiking. Just past the bifurcation, the point crawls through the narrow gap where the fixed points used to be, the "ghost" of the saddle-node, and that bottleneck sets a long period. This is a **saddle-node on an invariant circle**, and its simplest model is Ermentrout and Kopell's theta neuron,

$$
\dot\theta = 1 - \cos\theta + (1 + \cos\theta)I,
$$

which spikes each time $\theta$ passes $\pi$. For $I \lt 0$ it has two fixed points; for $I > 0$ it fires with period $\pi/\sqrt{I}$, so the rate rises as $\sqrt{I}$, starting at zero.

Hodgkin's class 1 axons behave this way, and so do many cortical pyramidal cells.

<Prove title="The period near a saddle-node" task="For the theta neuron with $I > 0$, show that the time to go once round the circle is $T = \int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{\mathrm d\theta}{1 - \cos\theta + (1 + \cos\theta)I} = \frac{\pi}{\sqrt I}$. (Use $1 - \cos\theta = 2\sin^2(\theta/2)$, $1 + \cos\theta = 2\cos^2(\theta/2)$ and the substitution $u = \tan(\theta/2)$.)">

With the half-angle identities, the denominator is $2\sin^2(\theta/2) + 2I\cos^2(\theta/2) = 2\cos^2(\theta/2)\left(\tan^2(\theta/2) + I\right)$.

Substitute $u = \tan(\theta/2)$, so $\mathrm du = \frac{1}{2\cos^2(\theta/2)}\mathrm d\theta$, and $u$ runs from $-\infty$ to $\infty$ as $\theta$ runs from $-\pi$ to $\pi$:

$$
T = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm du}{u^2 + I} = \frac{1}{\sqrt I}\left[\arctan\frac{u}{\sqrt I}\right]_{-\infty}^{\infty} = \frac{\pi}{\sqrt I}.
$$

Almost all of this time is spent near $\theta = 0$, where the denominator is small: the bottleneck left by the vanished fixed points. The same $1/\sqrt{\text{distance}}$ law holds near every saddle-node, which is why it appears in both today's critical slowing down and the f-I curve.

</Prove>
