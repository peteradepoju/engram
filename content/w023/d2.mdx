<Step n={1} title="Limit cycles" minutes={20} />

A **limit cycle** is an isolated closed trajectory: a loop in the phase plane that neighbouring trajectories approach (a stable cycle) or leave (an unstable one). It is the mathematical form of a self-sustained rhythm, such as a neuron firing repetitively or a heartbeat. It differs from the closed orbits around a centre (Week 22, Day 2), which come in continuous families: perturb a centre's orbit and you land on a neighbouring orbit and stay there, whereas a perturbed limit cycle returns to the same loop, with the same amplitude and period. Linear systems cannot have limit cycles; they need non-linearity.

<Step n={2} title="The Hopf normal form" minutes={30} />

Near any Hopf bifurcation, after a change of coordinates, the dynamics look like this in polar coordinates $(\rho, \phi)$:

$$
\dot\rho = \mu\rho - \rho^3, \qquad \dot\phi = \omega.
$$

The angle turns at a steady rate; the radius obeys a one-dimensional equation that you can analyse with yesterday's tools. For $\mu \lt 0$ the only fixed point of the radius is $\rho = 0$, stable: the origin is a stable spiral. For $\mu > 0$, $\rho = 0$ becomes unstable and a new stable fixed point of the radius appears at $\rho = \sqrt\mu$: a circular limit cycle. In Cartesian form this is $\dot z = (\mu + i\omega)z - |z|^2z$, with eigenvalues $\mu \pm i\omega$ at the origin, crossing the imaginary axis as $\mu$ passes zero. This is a **supercritical Hopf bifurcation**: the oscillation is born with zero amplitude, growing as $\sqrt\mu$, but with a non-zero frequency $\omega$ from the start. That is class 2 excitability's signature, in contrast with yesterday's saddle-node: the rhythm starts at a definite frequency, not at zero.

<Step n={3} title="Subcritical: a sudden start" minutes={25} />

Flip the sign of the cubic term and add a quintic one to stop runaway growth:

$$
\dot\rho = \mu\rho + \rho^3 - \rho^5.
$$

Now the cubic pushes outwards. Setting $\dot\rho = 0$ gives $\rho^2 = \frac{1 \pm \sqrt{1 + 4\mu}}{2}$. For $-\frac{1}{4} \lt \mu \lt 0$ there are two cycles: a small unstable one and a large stable one, with the stable origin inside. Rest and oscillation coexist, and the unstable cycle is the threshold between them. As $\mu$ rises to 0 the unstable cycle shrinks onto the origin and destroys its stability, and the state jumps to the large cycle. This is a **subcritical Hopf bifurcation**. Lower $\mu$ again and the oscillation persists until $\mu = -\frac{1}{4}$, where the two cycles collide and vanish: hysteresis again.

<Step n={4} title="Back to Hodgkin and Huxley" minutes={30} />

Week 21's Forge found that the Hodgkin-Huxley neuron fires repetitively from about 6.3 μA/cm², and Week 22's Forge found that its rest only becomes unstable at 9.78 μA/cm². A subcritical Hopf bifurcation reconciles the two. Between them, rest is stable but a large stable limit cycle, repetitive firing, coexists with it, and an unstable cycle separates the two. Izhikevich (2000, today's paper) showed that this arrangement is common among neurons near a Hopf bifurcation, and gives them a property integrators lack: a brief pulse can switch them from rest to firing, and a well-timed one can switch them back.

<Prove title="Cycles of the subcritical normal form" task="For $\dot\rho = \mu\rho + \rho^3 - \rho^5$, find the non-zero fixed points of $\rho$ and determine their stability from the sign of the derivative, for $-\frac{1}{4} \lt \mu \lt 0$.">

Non-zero fixed points satisfy $\mu + \rho^2 - \rho^4 = 0$. Writing $u = \rho^2$, this is $u^2 - u - \mu = 0$, so $u = \frac{1 \pm \sqrt{1 + 4\mu}}{2}$. For $-\frac{1}{4} \lt \mu \lt 0$ the square root is real and less than 1, so both roots are positive: two cycles, at $\rho_\pm = \sqrt{u_\pm}$.

The derivative of $g(\rho) = \mu\rho + \rho^3 - \rho^5$ is $g'(\rho) = \mu + 3\rho^2 - 5\rho^4$. At a cycle, $\mu = \rho^4 - \rho^2$, so $g'(\rho) = 2\rho^2 - 4\rho^4 = 2\rho^2(1 - 2\rho^2) = 2u(1 - 2u)$.

For the small cycle, $u_- \lt \frac{1}{2}$, so $g' > 0$: unstable. For the large one, $u_+ > \frac{1}{2}$, so $g' \lt 0$: stable. The origin has $g'(0) = \mu \lt 0$: stable. So rest and the large cycle coexist, separated by the small unstable one.

</Prove>
