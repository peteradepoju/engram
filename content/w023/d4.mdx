<Step n={1} title="Maps" minutes={20} />

A **map** updates a state in discrete steps, $x_{n+1} = f(x_n)$, rather than continuously. Maps describe populations with yearly generations, and also arise from continuous systems, by recording the state once per cycle. The logistic map,

$$
x_{n+1} = rx_n(1 - x_n), \qquad 0 \le x \le 1, \quad 0 \lt r \le 4,
$$

models a population (as a fraction of its maximum) that grows by a factor $r$ when sparse and is held back as it approaches the maximum.

Fixed points satisfy $x^* = f(x^*)$: here $x^* = 0$ and $x^* = 1 - 1/r$. Stability works as for flows, with one change. A small deviation $\delta$ becomes $f'(x^*)\delta$ after one step, so it shrinks if the **multiplier** $|f'(x^*)| \lt 1$. For the non-zero fixed point, $f'(x^*) = r(1 - 2x^*) = 2 - r$, so it is stable for $1 \lt r \lt 3$. Beyond $r = 3$ the multiplier passes −1: the deviation flips sign each step and grows, and the orbit settles into alternating between two values instead.

The steady state at $r = 2.8$ becomes a 2-cycle at 3.2, a 4-cycle at 3.5 and an 8-cycle at 3.55. Each doubling happens when the cycle's multiplier passes −1, a **period-doubling bifurcation**. At 3.9 nothing repeats within 64 steps: the orbit is chaotic. But chaos is not the end of the story: at 3.83 the period is 3, inside one of the windows of order that interrupt the chaotic region.

<Step n={3} title="Feigenbaum's constant" minutes={30} />

The doublings come faster and faster. In 1975 Mitchell Feigenbaum noticed that the gaps between successive bifurcation values shrink by the same ratio each time, about 4.669, and then that the same ratio appears for every map with a single smooth hump, not just the logistic one. It was later measured in convection, electronic circuits and lasers. Bifurcation points are awkward to compute because convergence is slow near them; the **superstable** parameters $R_n$, where the point $x = \frac12$ (the top of the hump, with multiplier zero) lies on the $2^n$-cycle, are easier. They interleave the bifurcations and shrink at the same rate.

<Step n={4} title="Measuring chaos" minutes={25} />

What makes an orbit chaotic is not that it looks irregular but that it is **sensitive to initial conditions**: nearby orbits separate exponentially. After $n$ steps, a tiny difference $\delta_0$ has become roughly $\delta_0e^{\lambda n}$, where

$$
\lambda = \lim_{n \to \infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k = 0}^{n - 1}\ln|f'(x_k)|
$$

is the **Lyapunov exponent**, the average logarithmic stretching per step along the orbit. $\lambda \lt 0$ means nearby orbits converge (a stable cycle); $\lambda > 0$ means they diverge (chaos).

At $r = 4$ the exponent is 0.6931, exactly $\ln 2$: on average each step doubles a small difference. It is positive at 3.7 (0.354) and negative in the stable 2-cycle at 3.2 (−0.916) and in the period-3 window at 3.83 (−0.370). Two orbits started $10^{-10}$ apart at $r = 4$ differ by more than 0.1 after 31 steps, close to $\ln(10^{9})/\ln 2 \approx 30$. That answers the prediction: about 30 steps. Measuring the start a thousand times more precisely would buy only 10 more steps.

<Prove title="Stability of the 2-cycle" task="Let $p$ and $q$ form a 2-cycle of a map $f$ ($f(p) = q$, $f(q) = p$). Show that the cycle's multiplier, the derivative of $f(f(x))$ at $p$, is $f'(p)f'(q)$, the same at both points. For the logistic map, the 2-cycle points satisfy $p + q = (r + 1)/r$ and $pq = (r + 1)/r^2$; deduce that the multiplier is $4 + 2r - r^2$ and that the 2-cycle is stable for $3 \lt r \lt 1 + \sqrt6$.">

By the chain rule, $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dx}f(f(x)) = f'(f(x))f'(x)$, which at $x = p$ is $f'(q)f'(p)$. The same product arises at $q$.

For the logistic map $f'(x) = r(1 - 2x)$, so the multiplier is $r^2(1 - 2p)(1 - 2q) = r^2\left(1 - 2(p + q) + 4pq\right) = r^2 - 2r(r + 1) + 4(r + 1) = -r^2 + 2r + 4$.

Stability needs $-1 \lt 4 + 2r - r^2 \lt 1$. At $r = 3$ the multiplier is 1, where the cycle is born. It reaches −1 when $r^2 - 2r - 5 = 0$, at $r = 1 + \sqrt6 \approx 3.449$, where the 2-cycle itself period-doubles into a 4-cycle.
