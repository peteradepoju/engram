<Step n={1} title="The Lorenz system" minutes={20} />

In 1963 Edward Lorenz, a meteorologist at MIT, reduced a model of air convection to three equations:

$$
\dot x = \sigma(y - x), \qquad \dot y = x(\rho - z) - y, \qquad \dot z = xy - \beta z,
$$

with $\sigma = 10$, $\beta = 8/3$ and $\rho = 28$. Restarting a simulation from rounded numbers printed halfway through, he found the new run soon disagreed completely with the old one. The trajectory loops around one of two unstable fixed points, then switches unpredictably to the other, forever, never repeating and never crossing itself. It stays on a bounded set of zero volume and fractal structure, a **strange attractor**. In two dimensions this is impossible: by the Poincaré-Bendixson theorem a bounded trajectory in the plane must approach a fixed point or a closed orbit. Three dimensions give trajectories room to weave past each other.

<Step n={2} title="Measuring the butterfly effect" minutes={25} />

The Lyapunov exponent of a flow is defined as for a map: the average exponential rate at which a small separation grows. To measure it, follow two trajectories, but renormalise their separation back to its starting size after every step, so that it never grows large enough for the non-linearity to fold it back; the average of the logarithm of the growth factors, divided by the time step, estimates the largest exponent. This is the method of Benettin and colleagues (1980).

Over 100 time units the estimate is 0.884, close to the accepted value of 0.906 (longer runs converge on it). A difference of $10^{-8}$ therefore reaches the size of the attractor, about 10, in roughly $\ln(10^9)/0.9 \approx 23$ time units, a couple of dozen loops. Improving the measurement a thousandfold adds less than 8 time units of predictability, which is why weather forecasts lose skill after about two weeks however good the observations.

<Step n={3} title="The map inside the flow" minutes={25} />

Lorenz found order inside the chaos. He recorded each successive maximum of $z$, $z_1, z_2, \dots$, and plotted $z_{n+1}$ against $z_n$. The points fell, not in a cloud, but on a thin curve shaped like a tent: the next maximum is (almost) a function of the last. The flow contains a one-dimensional map. And because the curve's slope is steeper than 1 everywhere, the map stretches every small difference, which, as yesterday, means chaos.

In 100 time units there are 134 maxima of $z$. Knowing one, the nearest previous maximum's successor predicts the next to within 0.05 (median), although the next maximum itself varies with a standard deviation of 3.2: the points lie on a thin curve, a cusp-shaped map. The flow is chaotic, but one step ahead it is almost perfectly predictable. It is long-range prediction that fails.

<Step n={4} title="Chaos in random networks" minutes={35} />

Week 21 showed that a linear network $\dot{\mathbf x} = -\mathbf x + J\mathbf x$, with random $J$ of entry variance $g^2/N$, explodes when $g > 1$, because the eigenvalue disc of $J$ then crosses 1. Real neurons saturate. Sompolinsky, Crisanti and Sommers (1988, today's deep read) studied $\dot{\mathbf x} = -\mathbf x + J\tanh(\mathbf x)$ and showed that for large $N$, the silent state $\mathbf x = 0$ is the only attractor when $g \lt 1$, but for $g > 1$ the network settles into self-sustained, irregular activity: chaos. The linear instability is the same, but the saturation turns runaway growth into endless wandering. Near $g = 1$ the fluctuations are slow, and they speed up as $g$ grows.

That answers the prediction: neither explosion nor silence but irregular activity that never settles, chaos produced by the network with no noise at all.

<Prove title="Volumes shrink in the Lorenz flow" task="For a flow $\dot{\mathbf x} = \mathbf F(\mathbf x)$, a small volume $V$ of initial conditions changes at the rate $\dot V = (\nabla\cdot\mathbf F)V$. Compute the divergence of the Lorenz vector field and show that every volume shrinks exponentially, $V(t) = V(0)e^{-(\sigma + 1 + \beta)t}$. Why does this not contradict a positive Lyapunov exponent?">

The divergence is $\frac{\partial\dot x}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial\dot y}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial\dot z}{\partial z} = -\sigma - 1 - \beta$, a negative constant ($-13.67$ for the standard values).

So $\dot V = -(\sigma + 1 + \beta)V$ and $V(t) = V(0)e^{-(\sigma + 1 + \beta)t}$: any blob of starting points is squeezed to zero volume, which is why the attractor itself has zero volume.

There is no contradiction because volume depends on all three directions together. The sum of the three Lyapunov exponents equals the divergence, $-13.67$. One is positive (about 0.91), one is zero (along the flow) and one is strongly negative (about $-14.57$). A blob is stretched in one direction while being crushed much faster in another, and repeated stretching and folding builds the attractor's fractal layers.

</Prove>
