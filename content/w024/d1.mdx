<Step n={1} title="The random walk" minutes={25} />

Start at 0. At each step, move up by 1 or down by 1 with equal probability. After $n$ steps the position is $X_n = \sum_{k = 1}^{n}\xi_k$, a sum of independent steps with mean 0 and variance 1. Means and variances of independent variables add (Week 15), so

$$
\mathbb{E}[X_n] = 0, \qquad \mathrm{Var}(X_n) = n, \qquad \mathrm{sd}(X_n) = \sqrt n.
$$

The walker does not drift anywhere on average, but its typical distance from the start grows like $\sqrt n$: to go twice as far takes four times as long. By the central limit theorem, $X_n$ is approximately Gaussian for large $n$. This square-root law is the signature of diffusion, and it is why diffusion is fast over the 20 nm of a synaptic cleft and hopelessly slow over the length of an axon.

<Step n={2} title="From walk to diffusion" minutes={20} />

Make the steps small and frequent: in each interval $\Delta t$, move by a normal random amount with mean $\mu\Delta t$ and variance $\sigma^2\Delta t$. Then

$$
X(t + \Delta t) = X(t) + \mu\Delta t + \sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}\,\xi, \qquad \xi \sim \mathcal N(0, 1),
$$

and after time $t$ the position has mean $\mu t$ and variance $\sigma^2t$, whatever $\Delta t$ is. As $\Delta t \to 0$ this defines **Brownian motion with drift**. The $\sqrt{\Delta t}$ is essential: it is what keeps the variance proportional to $t$, and it is why the noise term in Week 21's integrate-and-fire neuron and Week 22's line attractor carried a square root. Brownian paths are continuous but so jagged that they have no derivative anywhere, so "$\mathrm dX/\mathrm dt$" is a convenient fiction; Day 4 makes it precise.

The density of many walkers obeys the **diffusion equation**, $\partial_tp = -\mu\,\partial_xp + \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\partial_x^2p$, whose solution from a point is a Gaussian that drifts and spreads: the same picture seen from the crowd rather than from one walker.

<Step n={3} title="A neuron as a random walk" minutes={35} />

Gerstein and Mandelbrot (1964, today's paper) modelled a neuron's membrane potential as Brownian motion with drift: excitatory inputs push it up, inhibitory ones down, and the average excess of excitation is the drift. When it reaches a threshold $\theta$ the neuron fires and the potential resets. The interval between spikes is then the **first-passage time** of the walk to $\theta$, and its distribution, the inverse Gaussian, has mean $\theta/\mu$ and variance $\theta\sigma^2/\mu^3$. It is skewed, with a long right tail, like the interval histograms of real neurons.

<Step n={4} title="No drift, long waits" minutes={25} />

What if excitation and inhibition balance exactly, so that $\mu = 0$? A one-dimensional random walk is **recurrent**: it eventually reaches any level, so the neuron still fires. But the waiting time has a tail so heavy that its mean is infinite. The probability of not yet having reached $\theta$ by time $t$ falls only like $t^{-1/2}$, so there are always some very long intervals. Gerstein and Mandelbrot used this to explain the occasional enormous gaps in spike trains.

<Prove title="Mean first-passage time with drift" task="Let $T(x)$ be the mean time for Brownian motion with drift $\mu > 0$ and noise $\sigma$ to reach $\theta$ from $x \lt \theta$. Accept that $T$ satisfies $\mu T'(x) + \frac{\sigma^2}{2}T''(x) = -1$, with $T(\theta) = 0$ and $T$ not growing exponentially as $x \to -\infty$. Show that $T(x) = (\theta - x)/\mu$.">

Try $T(x) = (\theta - x)/\mu$. Then $T'(x) = -1/\mu$ and $T''(x) = 0$, so $\mu T' + \frac{\sigma^2}{2}T'' = -1$. The equation holds, and $T(\theta) = 0$.

The general solution adds solutions of the homogeneous equation $\mu T' + \frac{\sigma^2}{2}T'' = 0$, which are $A + Be^{-2\mu x/\sigma^2}$. The constant $A$ must be zero to keep $T(\theta) = 0$ once $B$ is fixed, and $B$ must be zero, because $e^{-2\mu x/\sigma^2}$ grows exponentially as $x \to -\infty$.

So the mean time is distance divided by drift, exactly as without noise. Noise does not change the average, only the spread: the variance $\theta\sigma^2/\mu^3$ grows rapidly as the drift weakens, and at $\mu = 0$ the mean becomes infinite.

</Prove>
