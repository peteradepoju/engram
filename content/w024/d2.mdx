<Step n={1} title="Markov chains" minutes={25} />

A **Markov chain** moves between a set of states, and the probability of its next move depends only on where it is now, not on how it got there. In discrete time it is described by a transition matrix $P$, whose entry $P_{ij}$ is the probability of going from state $i$ to state $j$ in one step; each row sums to 1. If $\boldsymbol\pi_n$ is the row vector of probabilities of being in each state at step $n$, then

$$
\boldsymbol\pi_{n + 1} = \boldsymbol\pi_nP.
$$

Under mild conditions (every state can reach every other, and the chain is not trapped in a cycle), $\boldsymbol\pi_n$ converges to a unique **stationary distribution** $\boldsymbol\pi$ with $\boldsymbol\pi = \boldsymbol\pi P$, whatever the start. That makes $\boldsymbol\pi$ a left eigenvector of $P$ with eigenvalue 1 (Week 6): the column eigenvector of $P^\top$. It also gives the long-run fraction of time a single realisation spends in each state.

<Step n={2} title="Continuous time and dwell times" minutes={25} />

An ion channel does not wait for a clock tick; it can switch at any instant. In a **continuous-time Markov chain**, each possible transition $i \to j$ has a rate $k_{ij}$: in a short interval $\Delta t$ it happens with probability $k_{ij}\Delta t$. For a channel that opens at rate $\alpha$ and closes at rate $\beta$,

$$
\text{closed} \underset{\beta}{\overset{\alpha}{\rightleftharpoons}} \text{open}, \qquad \frac{\mathrm dp}{\mathrm dt} = \alpha(1 - p) - \beta p,
$$

where $p$ is the probability of being open. This is exactly the Hodgkin-Huxley gating equation of Week 21, with $\alpha$ and $\beta$ depending on voltage. It relaxes to $p_\infty = \alpha/(\alpha + \beta)$ with time constant $1/(\alpha + \beta)$.

How long does the channel stay open? In every short interval it closes with the same probability $\beta\Delta t$, whatever has happened before, so the open time has no memory: it is **exponentially distributed** with mean $1/\beta$. Likewise shut times have mean $1/\alpha$. This memorylessness is the defining feature of a Markov state, and it is testable: Colquhoun and Hawkes (1981, today's paper) showed that a shut-time histogram that needs two exponentials reveals two distinct shut states.

<Step n={3} title="Simulating one channel exactly" minutes={30} />

Because dwell times are exponential, a channel can be simulated without any time step: draw how long it stays in its current state, jump to the other state, repeat. This is the **Gillespie algorithm** (1977), exact for any continuous-time Markov chain: with several possible transitions, the total rate sets the exponential waiting time, and the individual rates set the probabilities of which one happens.

<Step n={4} title="From one channel to many" minutes={25} />

A patch of membrane holds many independent channels. At any moment, the number open is a sum of independent yes-or-no variables, each open with probability $p$, so it is binomial: the fraction open has mean $p$ and standard deviation $\sqrt{p(1 - p)/N}$. As $N$ grows the fluctuations shrink and the fraction follows the smooth gating equation, which is why Hodgkin and Huxley's deterministic model works for a large axon. In a small neuron or a thin dendrite, with only hundreds of channels, the noise is large enough to cause spontaneous spikes and jitter in their timing.

<Prove title="Dwell times are exponential" task="A channel leaves the open state at rate $\beta$: in any short interval $\Delta t$ it closes with probability $\beta\Delta t$, independently of the past. Let $S(t)$ be the probability that it is still open at time $t$ after opening. Show that $S(t + \Delta t) = S(t)(1 - \beta\Delta t)$, deduce $S'(t) = -\beta S(t)$, and conclude that the open time has density $\beta e^{-\beta t}$ and mean $1/\beta$.">

To still be open at $t + \Delta t$, the channel must be open at $t$ (probability $S(t)$) and then not close in the next interval (probability $1 - \beta\Delta t$, independent of the past by the Markov property). So $S(t + \Delta t) = S(t)(1 - \beta\Delta t)$.

Rearranging, $\frac{S(t + \Delta t) - S(t)}{\Delta t} = -\beta S(t)$, and letting $\Delta t \to 0$ gives $S'(t) = -\beta S(t)$ with $S(0) = 1$, so $S(t) = e^{-\beta t}$.

The density of the open time is $-S'(t) = \beta e^{-\beta t}$, and its mean is $\int_0^\infty S(t)\,\mathrm dt = 1/\beta$. The same argument for any single state shows that dwell times in it are exponential with mean one over the total rate of leaving it.

</Prove>
