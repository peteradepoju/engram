<Step n={1} title="The sequential probability ratio test" minutes={30} />

Suppose samples $x_1, x_2, \dots$ arrive one at a time, from one of two hypotheses, $H_+$ or $H_-$, and you must decide which, as quickly as possible for a given error rate. After each sample, add its log likelihood ratio to a running total:

$$
L_n = \sum_{k = 1}^{n}\ln\frac{p(x_k \mid H_+)}{p(x_k \mid H_-)}.
$$

Stop and choose $H_+$ when $L_n \ge A$, or $H_-$ when $L_n \le -A$; otherwise take another sample. Wald showed that this **sequential probability ratio test** needs, on average, fewer samples than any other test with the same error rates, and that a bound of $A = \ln\frac{1 - \alpha}{\alpha}$ gives error rates of about $\alpha$ (slightly less, because the total overshoots the bound). For Gaussian samples with means $\pm m$ and standard deviation $s$, each sample contributes $2mx_k/s^2$: the total is a random walk with drift.

The error rate is 0.037, a little below the target of 0.05 because the total jumps past the bound rather than landing on it. Stopping as soon as the evidence is enough more than halves the cost, and on easy trials the saving is far larger.

<Step n={2} title="The drift diffusion model" minutes={30} />

Let the samples become infinitely frequent and small, and the accumulated evidence becomes Brownian motion with drift (Day 1): $\mathrm dx = \mu\,\mathrm dt + \sigma\,\mathrm dW$, starting at 0, stopping at $+a$ (correct) or $-a$ (error). This is the **drift diffusion model** of Ratcliff (1978). The drift $\mu$ measures the strength of the evidence, the bound $a$ the decision maker's caution. Solving first-passage problems like Day 1's gives two classic formulas:

$$
P(\text{correct}) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-2\mu a/\sigma^2}}, \qquad \mathbb E[\text{decision time}] = \frac{a}{\mu}\tanh\frac{\mu a}{\sigma^2}.
$$

Accuracy is a logistic function of evidence strength, the psychometric function measured in countless experiments; decision time falls as evidence strengthens, the chronometric function.

Accuracy matches the formula within 0.006 for all three drifts (0.737, 0.883, 0.987 against 0.731, 0.881, 0.982). Mean decision times are 0.965, 0.788 and 0.487 against 0.924, 0.762 and 0.482: a few per cent long, because checking the bound only every millisecond misses crossings that happen between checks, which acts like a slightly higher bound (by about $0.58\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}$, a known correction). Weak evidence makes decisions both slower and less accurate.

The formula also answers the prediction. With strong evidence, $\tanh(\mu a/\sigma^2) \approx 1$ and the decision time is $a/\mu$, so doubling the bound doubles it. With very weak evidence, $\tanh(u) \approx u$ and the time is $a^2/\sigma^2$, so doubling the bound quadruples it: pure diffusion, the square-root law of Day 1 run backwards.

<Step n={3} title="Speed against accuracy" minutes={25} />

A higher bound buys accuracy with time. Which bound is best depends on what the decision maker wants. If correct answers are rewarded and every trial also costs a fixed time $T_0$ (for moving, waiting for the next trial, and the non-decision parts of the response), a natural goal is to maximise the **reward rate**, correct answers per second:

$$
RR(a) = \frac{P(\text{correct})}{\mathbb E[\text{decision time}] + T_0}.
$$

Bogacz and colleagues (2006) showed that people often come close to the optimal bound in such tasks, though they tend to be more cautious than reward rate alone would dictate.

<Step n={4} title="Neurons that accumulate" minutes={20} />

Gold and Shadlen (2007, today's deep read) review the evidence that the brain implements something like this. In the moving-dots task, neurons in the middle temporal area (MT) respond to motion in their preferred direction, and their difference is the momentary evidence. Neurons in the lateral intraparietal area (LIP), which plan the eye movement that reports the choice, ramp up their firing during the decision, faster for stronger motion, and reach a common level just before the eyes move, whatever the motion strength: a bound. The rise looks like an accumulated drift, the common level like the bound. Week 22's winner-take-all circuit and line attractor are candidate mechanisms for both the accumulation and the commitment. The interpretation is debated: averaged ramps might hide steps that occur at random times in individual trials, and silencing LIP does not always impair the decision. <Prove title="Accuracy of the drift diffusion model" task="Let $P(x)$ be the probability of hitting $+a$ before $-a$ starting from $x$. Accept that $\mu P'(x) + \frac{\sigma^2}{2}P''(x) = 0$, with $P(a) = 1$ and $P(-a) = 0$. Solve it and show that $P(0) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-2\mu a/\sigma^2}}$.">

Let $k = 2\mu/\sigma^2$. The equation is $P'' + kP' = 0$, whose solutions are $P(x) = A + Be^{-kx}$.

The boundary conditions give $A + Be^{-ka} = 1$ and $A + Be^{ka} = 0$. Subtracting, $B(e^{-ka} - e^{ka}) = 1$, so $B = -1/(e^{ka} - e^{-ka})$, and $A = -Be^{ka} = e^{ka}/(e^{ka} - e^{-ka})$.

At $x = 0$: $P(0) = A + B = \frac{e^{ka} - 1}{e^{ka} - e^{-ka}} = \frac{e^{ka} - 1}{e^{-ka}(e^{2ka} - 1)} = \frac{e^{ka}}{e^{ka} + 1} = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-ka}}$.

With $k = 2\mu/\sigma^2$ this is $\frac{1}{1 + e^{-2\mu a/\sigma^2}}$: a logistic function of $\mu a/\sigma^2$, which is the total log likelihood ratio needed to decide. Doubling the bound has the same effect on accuracy as doubling the evidence strength.

</Prove>
