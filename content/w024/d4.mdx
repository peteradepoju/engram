<Step n={1} title="Stochastic differential equations" minutes={25} />

A **stochastic differential equation** (SDE) adds a noise term to an ordinary one:

$$
\mathrm dx = f(x)\,\mathrm dt + g(x)\,\mathrm dW,
$$

where $\mathrm dW$ is the increment of Brownian motion over $\mathrm dt$: Gaussian, mean 0, variance $\mathrm dt$. The drift $f$ is the deterministic push; $g$ sets the size of the random kicks. Because Brownian paths have no derivative, the equation is really shorthand for its simulation rule, the **Euler-Maruyama method**:

$$
x_{k + 1} = x_k + f(x_k)\Delta t + g(x_k)\sqrt{\Delta t}\,\xi_k, \qquad \xi_k \sim \mathcal N(0, 1).
$$

It is Euler's method from Week 21 with a random kick whose size scales as $\sqrt{\Delta t}$, as on Day 1. The **Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process** (Uhlenbeck and Ornstein, 1930, today's paper) is the linear case, a leak plus constant noise:

$$
\mathrm dx = -\frac{x}{\tau}\,\mathrm dt + \sigma\,\mathrm dW.
$$

For a neuron, $x$ is the voltage relative to its mean and $\tau$ the membrane time constant; the noise stands for the summed effect of many random synaptic inputs.

<Step n={2} title="How far it spreads" minutes={25} />

Starting from $x = 0$, the OU process's variance obeys a simple equation. Over one small step, the leak shrinks the existing variance by the factor $(1 - \Delta t/\tau)^2 \approx 1 - 2\Delta t/\tau$, and the noise adds $\sigma^2\Delta t$. So $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\mathrm{Var} = -\frac{2}{\tau}\mathrm{Var} + \sigma^2$, whose solution relaxes to a stationary value:

$$
\mathrm{Var}(x_t) = \frac{\sigma^2\tau}{2}\left(1 - e^{-2t/\tau}\right) \to \frac{\sigma^2\tau}{2}.
$$

The balance between the leak removing variance and the noise adding it sets the spread. A longer time constant means a weaker leak and so larger fluctuations.

<Step n={3} title="How long it remembers" minutes={25} />

In the stationary state, how related is the value now to the value a time $s$ later? Between the two, the leak shrinks the old value by $e^{-s/\tau}$ while fresh noise, independent of the past, is added. So the **autocorrelation** is

$$
\rho(s) = \frac{\mathrm{Cov}(x_t, x_{t + s})}{\mathrm{Var}(x_t)} = e^{-|s|/\tau}.
$$

The membrane time constant is the time over which the voltage remembers its inputs. In the frequency domain (Week 11), this exponential correlation is a Lorentzian power spectrum, flat at low frequencies and falling as $1/f^2$ above $1/(2\pi\tau)$: the membrane is a low-pass filter for noise, as it was for signals.

<Step n={4} title="Sampling with Langevin dynamics" minutes={30} />

The OU process settles into a Gaussian distribution. Change the drift and it settles into something else. For any density $p(x) \propto e^{-U(x)}$, the SDE

$$
\mathrm dx = \nabla\log p(x)\,\mathrm dt + \sqrt2\,\mathrm dW = -U'(x)\,\mathrm dt + \sqrt2\,\mathrm dW
$$

has $p$ as its stationary distribution. This is **Langevin dynamics**: gradient ascent on $\log p$, which pulls samples towards likely regions, plus exactly enough noise to keep them spread out in the right proportions. (The OU process is the case $U = x^2/2$.) It needs only the gradient of the log density, the **score**, and never the normalising constant, which is why it is used to sample from posteriors in Bayesian inference and why tomorrow's diffusion models train a network to estimate the score. Some have proposed that noisy neural dynamics sample from posterior distributions in the same way.

<Prove title="The stationary distribution of Langevin dynamics" task="The density $p(x, t)$ of an SDE $\mathrm dx = f(x)\mathrm dt + \sqrt2\,\mathrm dW$ obeys the Fokker-Planck equation $\partial_tp = -\partial_x(fp) + \partial_x^2p$. Show that with $f = -U'$, the density $p_\infty \propto e^{-U}$ is stationary.">

Write the right-hand side as $-\partial_xJ$ with probability flux $J = fp - \partial_xp$. A density is stationary if $\partial_xJ = 0$; it is enough that $J = 0$ everywhere.

For $p_\infty = Ce^{-U}$, $\partial_xp_\infty = -U'Ce^{-U} = -U'p_\infty$. So $J = -U'p_\infty - (-U'p_\infty) = 0$.

The drift's flow down the gradient of $U$ exactly cancels the diffusive flow from crowded to sparse regions, at every point. For the OU process, $U = x^2/(2v)$ with $v = \sigma^2\tau/2$ after rescaling time and noise, which gives back the Gaussian of Step 2.

</Prove>
