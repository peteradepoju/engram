<Step n={1} title="The forward process" minutes={25} />

Fix a schedule of small noise levels $\beta_1, \dots, \beta_T$. At each step, shrink the data slightly and add a little Gaussian noise:

$$
x_t = \sqrt{1 - \beta_t}\,x_{t - 1} + \sqrt{\beta_t}\,\xi_t.
$$

This is an Euler-Maruyama step of an OU process (Day 4) whose stationary distribution is the standard normal: the shrinking keeps the variance from growing. Because each step is linear and Gaussian, the steps compose. Writing $\alpha_t = 1 - \beta_t$ and $\bar\alpha_t = \alpha_1\alpha_2\cdots\alpha_t$,

$$
x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon, \qquad \varepsilon \sim \mathcal N(0, 1),
$$

so any noise level can be sampled from the data in one go. As $\bar\alpha_t \to 0$ the data are forgotten and $x_T$ is pure noise. Throughout today, $T = 100$ and $\beta_t$ rises linearly from $10^{-4}$ to 0.1, giving $\bar\alpha_T \approx 0.006$. The data are one-dimensional: a mixture of two narrow clusters at $\pm2$.

After 100 steps $\bar\alpha_T = 0.0056$: only $\sqrt{0.0056} = 7\%$ of the original signal remains, buried in noise of standard deviation 0.997. The closed form gives mean 0.001 and variance 1.023 (the theory, $\bar\alpha_T\mathrm{Var}(x_0) + 1 - \bar\alpha_T$, is 1.017), and stepping 100 times gives variance 1.008, the same within sampling error. Two clusters at ±2 have become one featureless Gaussian.

<Step n={2} title="Running it backwards" minutes={25} />

To generate, start from noise and undo one step at a time. Undoing a step means drawing $x_{t - 1}$ given $x_t$, and for small $\beta_t$ that distribution is close to Gaussian, centred on a point that moves $x_t$ a little uphill on the noised data density. What is needed at every step is the **score** of the noised density, $\nabla\log p_t(x)$, exactly as for Langevin dynamics on Day 4.

Ho, Jain and Abbeel (2020, today's paper) found a convenient way to get it. If $x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon$, the best guess of the added noise given $x_t$ is directly related to the score:

$$
\nabla\log p_t(x_t) = -\frac{\mathbb E[\varepsilon \mid x_t]}{\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}}.
$$

So train a network $\hat\varepsilon(x_t, t)$ to predict the noise, by minimising $\lVert\varepsilon - \hat\varepsilon(x_t, t)\rVert^2$ over data and noise levels, and the score comes for free. Sampling then repeats

$$
x_{t - 1} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\left(x_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}}\hat\varepsilon(x_t, t)\right) + \sqrt{\beta_t}\,z, \qquad z \sim \mathcal N(0, 1),
$$

from $t = T$ down to 1, with no noise on the last step.

<Step n={3} title="The perfect score memorises" minutes={25} />

Suppose the training set is just two points, $-2$ and $+2$, equally often. Then the noised density is a mixture of two Gaussians and its exact noise predictor can be written down: given $x_t$, the posterior mean of $x_0$ is $2\tanh\left(\frac{2\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_t}{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\right)$, and $\mathbb E[\varepsilon \mid x_t] = \frac{x_t - \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\mathbb E[x_0 \mid x_t]}{\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}}$. Run the reverse process with it.

Every one of the 4,000 samples lands exactly on −2 or +2, about half on each. That answers the prediction: with the exact score of the training set, the reverse process can only reproduce the training data, because the exact score of a finite set of points pulls everything onto those points. A model that generated only copies would be useless; generation needs a score that is not quite right in a useful way.

<Step n={4} title="Learning the score" minutes={30} />

A real diffusion model never has the exact score. It learns $\hat\varepsilon$ from samples by regression. Here a small model will do: for each noise level $t$, predict the clean value $x_0$ as a weighted sum of a few fixed features of $x_t$ (a constant, $x_t$ itself, and $\tanh(sx_t)$ for several scales $s$), with weights found by least squares on freshly noised data. Since $x_t$ is known, predicting $x_0$ and predicting $\varepsilon$ are equivalent ($\hat\varepsilon = (x_t - \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\hat x_0)/\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}$), but the $x_0$ form lets you clip the prediction to the range the data can take, a safeguard Ho and colleagues also used. Without it, at the smallest noise levels the crude model's errors are divided by a tiny $\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}$ and the occasional sample is flung far away. The data are now the two clusters of width 0.1, so there is genuine spread to learn.

<Prove title="Noise prediction gives the score" task="With $x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha}\,x_0 + \sigma\varepsilon$ and $\sigma = \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha}$, the conditional density is $q(x_t \mid x_0) = \mathcal N(\sqrt{\bar\alpha}\,x_0, \sigma^2)$. Using $p_t(x_t) = \int q(x_t \mid x_0)p(x_0)\,\mathrm dx_0$, show that $\nabla\log p_t(x_t) = -\mathbb E[\varepsilon \mid x_t]/\sigma$.">

Differentiate under the integral: $\nabla p_t(x_t) = \int\nabla_{x_t}q(x_t \mid x_0)\,p(x_0)\,\mathrm dx_0$. For a Gaussian, $\nabla_{x_t}q = -\frac{x_t - \sqrt{\bar\alpha}x_0}{\sigma^2}q$.

Divide by $p_t(x_t)$ and use Bayes' rule, $\frac{q(x_t \mid x_0)p(x_0)}{p_t(x_t)} = p(x_0 \mid x_t)$:

$$
\nabla\log p_t(x_t) = -\int\frac{x_t - \sqrt{\bar\alpha}x_0}{\sigma^2}\,p(x_0 \mid x_t)\,\mathrm dx_0 = -\frac{\mathbb E\left[x_t - \sqrt{\bar\alpha}x_0 \mid x_t\right]}{\sigma^2}.
$$

Since $x_t - \sqrt{\bar\alpha}x_0 = \sigma\varepsilon$, this is $-\mathbb E[\varepsilon \mid x_t]/\sigma$. And the function that minimises the squared error $\mathbb E\lVert\varepsilon - \hat\varepsilon(x_t)\rVert^2$ is exactly $\mathbb E[\varepsilon \mid x_t]$ (the conditional mean is the least-squares estimate, Week 17), so training a noise predictor by least squares is training a score estimator.

</Prove>

<Key title="Before you go">
<ul>
<li>The forward process is a discrete OU process that turns data into Gaussian noise; any step can be sampled directly.</li>
<li>Reversing it needs the score, which a network learns by predicting the added noise.</li>
<li>The exact score of a finite training set only reproduces training points.</li>
<li>A learned, smoothed score generates new samples spread like the data.</li>
