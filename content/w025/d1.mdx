<Step n={1} title="The project and the task" minutes={20} />

A monkey sits facing a screen, hand on a touch point. A target appears, sometimes with barriers that force a curved path: one of 108 different reach conditions, a "maze" task. After a delay of up to a second the monkey is cued to go, and reaches. Meanwhile electrode arrays in motor cortex (M1) and dorsal premotor cortex (PMd) record spikes. For each neuron and each condition, spikes from many repeats of that condition were averaged into a firing rate, smoothed, in 10 ms bins aligned to movement onset. That gives a **tensor** of firing rates: 108 conditions × 61 time points (from 50 ms before to 550 ms after movement onset) × 218 neurons.

The data come from Churchland and colleagues (2012), who released them with their analysis code; Day 4's paper is theirs, and today's paper sets out the idea it tests. Shenoy, Sahani and Churchland (2013) argue that motor cortex is better described as a **dynamical system** than as a set of neurons each representing a movement parameter: preparation sets the population's initial state, and its own dynamics then carry it through the movement. If that is right, population activity should be low-dimensional and its evolution should be predictable from its current state. This week you test that.

<Step n={3} title="Soft normalisation" minutes={25} />

PCA finds directions of large variance. A neuron whose rate swings by 150 spikes/s has over a hundred times the variance of one that swings by 10, so without care the analysis is dominated by a few high-rate cells. Dividing each neuron by its range would make every neuron count equally, including nearly silent ones whose small fluctuations are mostly noise. Churchland and colleagues compromised with **soft normalisation**: divide each neuron's rates by its range plus a constant, 5 spikes/s. Strong neurons end up with a range near 1; weak ones are scaled down rather than amplified.

<Step n={4} title="What is common to every reach" minutes={25} />

Some of each neuron's activity changes in the same way whatever the reach: a burst as the movement starts, say, that is the same for all 108 conditions. This **condition-independent** signal is real and interesting (Kaufman and colleagues later found it is often the largest component), but this project asks how activity differs between reaches and how those differences evolve. So, following the original analysis, subtract the average over conditions at each time point from every condition. Split the variance of the normalised data into the part explained by that cross-condition mean and the rest.

The cross-condition mean accounts for 41% of the variance of the normalised data, and 59% differs between conditions. That answers the prediction: well over a third of each neuron's modulation is the same whatever the reach, a large signal that tells you when the movement happens but not which movement it is. From here on, `X` (conditions × times × neurons, with that common part removed) is the starting point for every analysis.
