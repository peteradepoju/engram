<Step n={1} title="Set up and recap" minutes={20} />

Each lesson page runs its own Python, so start by repeating Day 1's preprocessing. As in the original paper, the main analyses use the window from 50 ms before to 150 ms after movement onset, when the movement is being generated: 21 time points. Stacking conditions and times gives a data matrix with $108 \times 21 = 2{,}268$ rows (population states) and 218 columns (neurons).

<PyCell code={String.raw`
  import numpy as np
  import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
  data = np.load(dataset("churchland2012_reach.npz"))
  rates, times = data["rates"].astype(float), data["times"]
  C, T, N = rates.shape
  Xn = rates / (rates.max(axis=(0, 1)) - rates.min(axis=(0, 1)) + 5)     # soft normalisation
  X = Xn - Xn.mean(axis=0, keepdims=True)                                 # remove the cross-condition mean
  win = (times >= -50) & (times <= 150)
  Xw = X[:, win, :]                                                       # conditions x 21 times x neurons
  D = Xw.reshape(-1, N)                                                   # 2268 population states x 218 neurons
  print("data matrix:", D.shape)
`} />

PCA finds orthogonal directions in neuron space, ordered by how much variance of the data they capture (Week 7). With the data matrix centred, its singular value decomposition $D = USV^\top$ gives everything: the rows of $V^\top$ are the principal directions, the squared singular values are proportional to the variance along each, and $US$ holds the coordinates of every state.

The first component captures 24% of the variance, the second 12%, and the first six together 61%, all from population states of 218 neurons. The SVD and the covariance eigendecomposition agree, as they must; the SVD is preferred because it never forms the covariance matrix, which loses precision.

<Step n={3} title="How many dimensions?" minutes={25} />

There is no single right answer to "how many dimensions". Two common summaries: the number of components needed to reach a fixed share of the variance, such as 80%, and the **participation ratio**,

$$
\mathrm{PR} = \frac{\left(\sum_i\lambda_i\right)^2}{\sum_i\lambda_i^2},
$$

which equals $d$ when the variance is spread equally over $d$ components and falls smoothly as it concentrates. Neither separates signal from noise; tomorrow you will use held-out data to do that.

Eighteen components are needed to reach 80% of the variance, and the participation ratio is 10.7. That answers the prediction: about 10 to 20, far fewer than 218 but more than a handful. The two measures disagree because the variance falls off gradually; neither knows which of the small components are signal. Note too that this is the dimensionality of trial-averaged activity across 108 conditions in a 200 ms window; single trials, other tasks or other windows can give different answers.

Look at different pairs of components. In some, conditions start in different places and fan out; in others, many conditions seem to swing round together. The eye is good at seeing patterns and bad at judging whether they are real. Principal components are chosen to capture variance, not dynamics, so a rotation, if there is one, need not lie in any single pair of them. Day 4 asks the question properly.
