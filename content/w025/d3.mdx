Six components rebuild the median neuron with an R² of 0.47, and a quarter of neurons fall below 0.26. Six dimensions capture the broad shape of the population's activity but leave much of individual neurons' responses unexplained, which is either structure in higher dimensions or noise. Reconstruction alone cannot say which.

<Step n={2} title="Why reconstruction cannot choose" minutes={15} />

Adding a component can only reduce the reconstruction error, on the data used for the fit and on new data alike: projecting onto a bigger subspace never moves a point further from where it was. So the error falls all the way to zero at $k = 218$, and no amount of held-out conditions changes that. To see where structure ends and noise begins, the model must make a *prediction* that extra components can make worse. The trick, **bi-cross-validation** (Owen and Perry, 2009), is to hold out neurons as well as conditions: find $k$ components from one half of the neurons on training conditions, learn by regression how they predict the other half of the neurons, and test that prediction on held-out conditions. Now each extra component adds regression weights that can fit noise in the training conditions, and the test score eventually falls.

  def bicv(k, reps=10):
      r = np.random.default_rng(7); out = []
      for _ in range(reps):
          cp, npm = r.permutation(C), r.permutation(N)
          A, B, n1, n2 = cp[:C // 2], cp[C // 2:], npm[:N // 2], npm[N // 2:]
          DA1 = Xw[A][:, :, n1].reshape(-1, len(n1)); m1 = DA1.mean(0)
          Vk = np.linalg.svd(DA1 - m1, full_matrices=False)[2][:k].T
          LA = (DA1 - m1) @ Vk
          LB = (Xw[B][:, :, n1].reshape(-1, len(n1)) - m1) @ Vk
          YA = Xw[A][:, :, n2].reshape(-1, len(n2)); YB = Xw[B][:, :, n2].reshape(-1, len(n2))
          W = np.linalg.lstsq(np.c_[LA, np.ones(len(LA))], YA, rcond=None)[0]                                   # regression from [LA, 1] to YA
          pred = np.c_[LB, np.ones(len(LB))] @ W
          out.append(1 - np.sum((YB - pred) ** 2) / np.sum((YB - YB.mean(0)) ** 2))                           # R squared of pred on YB
      return np.mean(out)

  scores = [bicv(k) for k in ks]
  print({k: round(float(s), 3) for k, s in zip(ks, scores)})

The held-out score rises steeply at first (0.18 with one component, 0.48 with six), keeps creeping up to a peak of 0.63 around 20 to 30 components, then falls to 0.50 by 80, as the regression starts fitting noise. That answers the prediction: it improves, levels off and then gets worse. By this test the reaching activity has roughly 20 to 30 predictable dimensions, more than the six usually drawn, though most of the predictable variance is in the first ten or so. Report the curve, not just its peak: the plateau is broad.

<Step n={4} title="Do the dimensions change?" minutes={25} />

Kaufman and colleagues (2014, today's paper) showed that preparatory activity in motor cortex lies largely in dimensions that a linear readout to the muscles ignores, the **output-null** space, and that movement activity moves into the **output-potent** space. A related, testable idea: does the population use different dimensions early and late in the movement? Take the top four components of an early window (−50 to 40 ms) and ask how much of the late window's (100 to 190 ms) variance they capture, relative to the late window's own top four components. This **alignment index** is 1 if the subspaces are the same, and for random directions it would be small.

The early window's top four directions capture only 19% as much of the late activity as the late window's own top four, against 4% for random directions. The population does not keep using the same few dimensions through the movement: as the reach unfolds, activity moves into largely different directions, as Elsayed and colleagues (2016) found for preparation and movement. This matters for Day 4: a dynamical system can produce exactly this, by rotating the state out of the dimensions it started in.
