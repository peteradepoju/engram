The hypothesis is that the six-dimensional state $\mathbf z$ obeys $\dot{\mathbf z} = M\mathbf z$ with the **same** $M$ for every reach. Different reaches start from different preparatory states and the shared dynamics carry each one forward. To test it, estimate the derivative at each time by a finite difference, $\dot{\mathbf z}(t) \approx (\mathbf z(t + \Delta t) - \mathbf z(t))/\Delta t$, and ask how well $M\mathbf z(t)$ predicts it. With every state as a row, this is linear regression, the least squares of Week 5, with six outputs.

One matrix, the same for all 108 reaches, predicts 65% of the variance of the population's rate of change, and 63% on reaches it was not fitted to, so the fit is not just memorising conditions. Its eigenvalues are revealing: two complex pairs, oscillations at $8.27/2\pi \approx 1.3$ Hz and $4.34/2\pi \approx 0.7$ Hz, and all with positive real parts. In this window, as the movement gets under way, activity grows as well as turns.

<Step n={3} title="Pure rotation" minutes={30} />

Churchland and colleagues asked a sharper question: how much of the evolution is pure **rotation**? A matrix that only rotates, never stretching or shrinking, is **skew-symmetric**, $M^\top = -M$: its eigenvalues are purely imaginary (Week 22), so every mode circles without growing or decaying. A $6 \times 6$ skew-symmetric matrix has 15 free numbers, the entries above the diagonal, and $\dot{\mathbf z} = M\mathbf z$ is still linear in them. Build one basis matrix $E_{ij}$ (1 at $(i, j)$, −1 at $(j, i)$) per pair, and the fit is ordinary least squares on 15 coefficients.

Pure rotation explains 49% of the variance of the derivative (47% on held-out reaches), three quarters of what the free fit achieves with less than half the parameters. That answers the prediction: about 50%. Much of what the population does from moment to moment is turn, and what the rotational model misses is largely the growth seen in the free fit's eigenvalues.

<Step n={4} title="The jPCA plane" minutes={25} />

The eigenvalues of $M_\text{skew}$ come in pairs $\pm i\omega$, one pair per plane of rotation. The pair with the largest $\omega$ marks the plane in which the population turns fastest; its eigenvector's real and imaginary parts span that plane. Projecting the data onto it gives the **jPCA** plot, and $\omega/2\pi$ is the rotation frequency.

The fastest plane rotates at 1.27 Hz, a period of about 0.8 s, so in the 200 ms window each condition turns through roughly a quarter of a cycle. Ninety-five per cent of the conditions turn the same way, and they are ordered: neighbouring starting states stay neighbours as they rotate. In the figure, tick the control box: when conditions are shuffled independently for each neuron, which keeps every neuron's own responses but destroys how they are coordinated, the rotational fit falls from 0.49 to 0.01. Whatever the rotation is, it is a property of the population, not of single neurons. Tomorrow you ask whether it could still come from something simpler than dynamics.
