<Step n={1} title="Set up and the logic of controls" minutes={20} />

The whole pipeline from Days 2 to 4 fits in one function: take a conditions × times × neurons array (already normalised and with the cross-condition mean removed), run PCA, fit the skew-symmetric dynamics and return its R². Applying the same function to the data and to surrogates guarantees the comparison is fair.

<PyCell code={String.raw`
  import numpy as np
  import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
  data = np.load(dataset("churchland2012_reach.npz"))
  rates, times = data["rates"].astype(float), data["times"]
  C, T, N = rates.shape
  Xn = rates / (rates.max(axis=(0, 1)) - rates.min(axis=(0, 1)) + 5)
  X = Xn - Xn.mean(axis=0, keepdims=True)
  win = (times >= -50) & (times <= 150); tw = times[win]
  Xw = X[:, win, :]
  dt = 0.01
  basis = []
  for i in range(6):
      for j in range(i + 1, 6):
          E = np.zeros((6, 6)); E[i, j], E[j, i] = 1, -1; basis.append(E)

  def rotation_r2(A):
      """PCA to six components, then the R squared of the best skew-symmetric fit of dZ/dt = MZ."""
      D = A.reshape(-1, A.shape[-1]); mu = D.mean(0)
      Vt = np.linalg.svd(D - mu, full_matrices=False)[2]
      Z = (A - mu) @ Vt[:6].T
      Zm, dZ = Z[:, :-1].reshape(-1, 6), (np.diff(Z, axis=1) / dt).reshape(-1, 6)
      G = np.stack([(Zm @ E.T).ravel() for E in basis], axis=1)
      h = np.linalg.lstsq(G, dZ.ravel(), rcond=None)[0]
      return 1 - np.sum((dZ.ravel() - G @ h) ** 2) / np.sum((dZ - dZ.mean(0)) ** 2)

  real = rotation_r2(Xw)
  print(f"real data: rotational R2 = {real:.3f}")
`} />

A control asks: if only feature F of the data were present, how strong would the effect be? If the real effect is no stronger than in data that have F and nothing else, F is enough to explain it. Elsayed and Cunningham (2017, today's paper) made this systematic, generating surrogates that keep the data's correlations across time, neurons and conditions but are otherwise random.

<Step n={2} title="Shuffling conditions" minutes={25} />

Shuffle the 108 condition labels independently for each neuron. Every neuron keeps exactly its own set of responses, but which response goes with which reach no longer matches across neurons, so any coordination is destroyed.

The 20 shuffled datasets give rotational fits between 0.004 and 0.014, against 0.489 for the real data. The p-value, 0.048, is simply the smallest that 20 shuffles can give, $1/21$; with more shuffles it would be smaller still, since the real value is more than thirty times the largest null. Coordination across neurons is essential: individual neurons' responses do not produce the rotation by themselves.

<Step n={3} title="Matching the covariances" minutes={30} />

The shuffle destroys too much: it also removes the correlations between neurons that any population has. A fairer null keeps the data's **temporal covariance** (how each time point covaries with each other, averaged over conditions and neurons) and its **neural covariance** (how neurons covary), and is otherwise random. One simple way, a separable Gaussian model, draws standard normal noise $G$ for each condition and shapes it on both sides: $Y_c = L_t G_c L_n^\top$, where $L_tL_t^\top$ is the temporal covariance and $L_nL_n^\top$ the neural covariance. Elsayed and Cunningham's method also matches the covariance across conditions; this simpler version is a good first test.

Surrogates with the data's temporal and neural covariance give rotational fits between about 0.00 and 0.02. Smooth responses and correlated neurons are not enough: the rotation depends on how the population's activity is organised across conditions, which this separable surrogate does not keep. Elsayed and Cunningham's full method also matches the covariance across conditions; they found that motor cortex rotations exceeded even those surrogates, but that test is stricter than yours.

<Step n={4} title="Is it only timing?" minutes={30} />

Now a sharper alternative. Suppose every reach used the same pattern of activity, and reaches differed only in **when** it happened, a little earlier or later. After the cross-condition mean is removed, what remains is roughly the shift times the pattern's rate of change, and as a sequence unfolds that difference can swing round. Test this in two steps: build such data and see whether the analysis finds rotations; then ask whether the real data look like that.

The time-shifted synthetic data give a rotational fit of 0.85, much stronger than the real data's 0.49. That answers the prediction: stronger. Rotation, even strong and consistent rotation, can come from nothing more than reaches differing in timing, so by itself it is not proof of dynamics. But the real data do not behave like that. The best-fitting shifts are only ±10 ms, and the timing model explains just 0.5% of the real condition-specific variance: the reaches differ in the pattern of activity, not merely its timing. This explanation can be ruled out for these data, which is a stronger conclusion than ignoring it.
