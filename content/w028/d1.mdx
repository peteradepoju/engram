<Step n={1} title="The ladder of scales" minutes={25} />

Neuroscience is studied at every scale, and much of the field's difficulty is connecting them.

| Scale | Size | Example | Typical time |
|---|---|---|---|
| Molecule | 1 to 10 nm | an ion channel, a neurotransmitter receptor | opens in under 1 ms |
| Synapse | about 1 μm | one connection between two neurons | transmits in about 1 ms; changes over minutes to years |
| Neuron | cell body 10 to 30 μm; dendrites up to 1 mm; axons up to 1 m | a pyramidal cell | integrates over 10 to 20 ms |
| Microcircuit | 0.1 to 1 mm | a cortical column, a hippocampal subfield | processes over tens of ms |
| Area | 1 to 10 cm² of cortical sheet | primary visual cortex | responds in 50 to 100 ms |
| System and brain | 10 cm | the visual system; the whole brain | behaviour in 100 ms to seconds |

A human brain weighs about 1.4 kg, about 2% of body mass, and holds about 86 billion neurons, which together form somewhere around $10^{14}$ to $10^{15}$ synapses. Today's paper describes how those neurons were counted.

<Step n={2} title="Counting with uncertainty" minutes={30} />

Many quantities in neuroscience are known only to within a factor of a few. A **Fermi estimate** multiplies rough factors, and a good one says how rough the answer is. If each factor is uncertain over a range, treat it as a random variable spread evenly on a log scale and propagate the uncertainty by simulation (Week 15).

The median is $2.7 \times 10^{14}$ synapses, with a 90% interval from about $10^{14}$ to $8 \times 10^{14}$. Almost all of that spread comes from the synapses per neuron, which is uncertain by a factor of ten, while the neuron count is known to within about 10%. That is the useful output of an uncertainty-aware estimate: it tells you which number to measure better.

<Step n={3} title="The energy budget" minutes={30} />

The brain is expensive: about 20 W, a fifth of the body's resting energy use, almost all of it spent on restoring ion gradients after electrical signalling. Each spike, counting the synaptic transmission it triggers, costs on the order of $10^9$ molecules of ATP, the cell's energy currency (estimates by Attwell and Laughlin, 2001, and Lennie, 2003, vary by a factor of a few). Each ATP releases about $8 \times 10^{-20}$ joules. Those numbers set a ceiling on how much the brain can fire.

The 10 W available for signalling buys about $1.25 \times 10^{20}$ ATP molecules per second, which at $10^9$ ATP per spike supports an average of only 1.5 spikes per second per neuron. With cheaper or costlier spikes the ceiling moves between about 0.5 and 5. That answers the prediction: about one spike per second, not 30 or 300. Neurons that fire at tens of spikes per second must be balanced by many that are almost silent, so the brain must use sparse codes, an idea that returns in Parts 2 and 5.

Across primates, neurons scale with brain mass to the power 0.93, close to proportional; across mouse and rat, to the power 0.69. Extrapolated, a rodent with a human-sized brain would have about 21 billion neurons, a quarter of ours. In primates, neurons stay about the same size as brains grow; in rodents they get bigger, so each gram holds fewer. Two points make a weak fit, of course: Herculano-Houzel's studies used many more species, with the same conclusion.
