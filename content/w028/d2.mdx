<Step n={1} title="The main divisions" minutes={25} />

Running from the spinal cord upwards:

- **Brainstem** (medulla, pons, midbrain): breathing, heart rate, arousal, eye movements, relaying signals to and from the body. The midbrain contains the dopamine neurons of the substantia nigra and ventral tegmental area.
- **Cerebellum**: coordination, timing and motor learning; it holds most of the brain's neurons, in a strikingly regular circuit.
- **Thalamus**: the gateway to the cortex. Almost every sensory signal except smell passes through it, and it forms loops with every cortical area.
- **Hypothalamus**: hunger, thirst, temperature, hormones, and innate behaviours such as aggression and mating.
- **Basal ganglia**: action selection and reinforcement learning, in loops through the thalamus and cortex.
- **Hippocampus and amygdala**: memory and spatial navigation; emotional learning.
- **Cerebral cortex**: a folded sheet, organised into areas for sensing, moving, planning, language and much else; in humans about 16 billion neurons.

The functions listed are simplifications: every area takes part in many functions, and every function involves many areas.

<Step n={2} title="The cortical sheet and its layers" minutes={25} />

The neocortex is a sheet 2 to 4 mm thick and, in humans, about 2,000 cm² in area, folded to fit the skull. Across its thickness it has six layers, which differ in their cell types and connections. Within an area, a simplified **canonical circuit** carries signals from the thalamus into layer 4, up to layers 2/3, then down to layers 5 and 6, which send outputs out of the cortex and back to the thalamus.

The response peaks in layer 4 at 10 ms, as the thalamic pulse ends, then in layers 2/3 at 17.7 ms, layer 5 at 27.6 ms and layer 6 at 37.6 ms, each stage adding about one time constant of delay and smoothing the signal further. Recordings across cortical depth after a sensory stimulus show a similar order, with layer 4 and the deep part of layer 6 responding first, though real circuits have many more paths than this chain.

<Step n={3} title="Areas as a network" minutes={30} />

Felleman and Van Essen (1991, today's paper) used the layers in which each connection between two areas starts and ends to call it feedforward or feedback, and arranged the macaque's visual areas in a hierarchy. Below is a small, simplified subset of the visual pathways: from V1, a ventral stream through V2 and V4 to the inferotemporal areas TEO and TE, concerned with object identity, and a dorsal stream through MT and MST to parietal area 7a, concerned with motion and space. Most connections are reciprocal.

V2 and MT are one step from V1; V4 and MST two; TEO, TE and 7a three. That answers the prediction: about three steps to inferotemporal cortex, even though the hierarchy has several levels, because some connections skip levels (V4 projects to TE directly, V1 to MT). Eight of the ten feedforward connections here have a feedback partner; in the full dataset most connections are reciprocal. A hierarchy with short paths and abundant feedback is very different from a deep feedforward chain.

<Step n={4} title="Wiring and distance" minutes={25} />

Connections cost space and energy, so the brain keeps most wiring short. Tract-tracing studies in the macaque (Markov and colleagues, 2014; Ercsey-Ravasz and colleagues, 2013) found that the density of connections between two areas falls roughly exponentially with the distance between them, an **exponential distance rule**. Fit one to simulated data drawn from such a rule.

The fit recovers a decay rate of 0.196 per mm (true 0.19), so connection strength halves every 3.5 mm, and falls a thousandfold over about 35 mm. Distant areas are still connected, but weakly: most of the brain's wiring budget is spent locally, which shapes everything from the size of cortical areas to how fast signals can spread across the brain.

<ul>
