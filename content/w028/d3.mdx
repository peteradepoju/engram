<Step n={1} title="Anatomy of a neuron" minutes={20} />

A neuron receives inputs on its **dendrites**, branched processes that can carry thousands of synapses; integrates them in its **soma** (cell body); and, when its membrane voltage at the **axon initial segment** crosses threshold, fires an action potential that travels down its **axon** to synapses on other cells. Axons may be local, branching within a millimetre, or project across the brain; many are wrapped in **myelin**, which speeds conduction.

**Glia** are the brain's other cells. **Astrocytes** regulate ions and neurotransmitters around synapses, couple activity to blood flow (the basis of the fMRI signal, Day 5) and may take part in signalling. **Oligodendrocytes** make myelin in the brain and spinal cord. **Microglia** are the brain's immune cells and prune synapses during development. In the human brain, glia and neurons are roughly equal in number overall, not ten to one as once claimed.

<Step n={2} title="Excitatory and inhibitory families" minutes={25} />

In the neocortex about 80% of neurons are **excitatory**: mostly pyramidal cells that release glutamate, with a pyramid-shaped soma, a long apical dendrite and, often, long-range axons. The other 20% are **inhibitory** interneurons that release GABA and mostly connect locally. Three classes, defined by marker proteins, cover most of them:

- **Parvalbumin (PV)** cells, often basket cells, target the soma of pyramidal cells, fire fast with brief spikes, and control the timing of output; they drive gamma rhythms.
- **Somatostatin (SST)** cells, such as Martinotti cells, target dendrites and control how inputs are integrated.
- **VIP** cells mainly inhibit SST cells, so activating them disinhibits pyramidal dendrites.

**Dale's principle** says that a neuron releases the same neurotransmitters at all its synapses, so a cortical neuron is either excitatory or inhibitory onto every target. Most artificial networks ignore this; brains do not.

Two features are enough to recover the narrow-spiking class with 99% accuracy here. Two cautions carry over to real data: k-means can settle in a poor solution from an unlucky start, which is why it is run from several starts and the tightest result kept; and it always returns k clusters, whether or not the data have k types. Choosing k is the same problem as choosing a dimensionality, and has no automatic answer.

<Step n={4} title="Types from genes" minutes={30} />

PCA followed by k-means recovers all three types perfectly, because each type raises its own marker genes, which PCA turns into separate directions. With a single random start, the same clustering can recover only about half of them, by merging two types and splitting a third, which is why restarts matter. Real transcriptomic data are far messier: thousands of genes, technical noise, continuous variation within types, and types that differ by a handful of genes. Tasic and colleagues validated their clusters by checking that they were stable under resampling and matched known anatomy.

With four times as many excitatory cells, inhibitory synapses must be about four times stronger for the books to balance. Balanced excitation and inhibition is a central idea of Part 5 (Week 107): it keeps activity stable and makes neurons fire irregularly.
