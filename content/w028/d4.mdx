<Step n={1} title="The extracellular signal" minutes={20} />

When a neuron fires, current flows into the cell at the active membrane and out elsewhere, and that current flowing through the resistive extracellular space produces a small voltage, tens to a few hundred microvolts at a nearby electrode, lasting about 1 ms. Its size falls steeply with distance (Buzsáki, Anastassiou and Koch, 2012, today's paper), so an electrode sees a handful of neurons within about 50 to 100 μm, each with its own waveform. The slower currents of synaptic inputs, summed over many cells, make up the **local field potential** (LFP), below a few hundred hertz.

Recording technology has scaled up from single wires, through **tetrodes** (four wires close together, so each spike appears on several), to silicon probes. **Neuropixels** probes carry hundreds of sites along a 1 cm shank, recording from hundreds of neurons across several brain regions at once.

<Step n={2} title="Filtering and detection" minutes={30} />

Simulate one channel: two neurons with different waveforms, a slow LFP and noise, sampled at 30 kHz. First **high-pass filter** the trace to remove the LFP, then detect spikes as crossings of a negative threshold. The noise level is estimated robustly from the median absolute value, $\hat\sigma = \mathrm{median}(|x|)/0.6745$ (Quiroga and colleagues, 2004), because the ordinary standard deviation is inflated by the spikes themselves.

Removing everything below 300 Hz takes out the slow wave entirely. With the threshold at −4.6 (in these units, from a noise level of about 1), 99.4% of the true spikes are found within 0.5 ms and only 0.3% of detections are false. Two details matter in practice: the robust noise estimate, which barely notices the spikes, and the 1 ms dead time, without which one broad spike can cross the threshold twice and be counted twice.

<Step n={3} title="Spike sorting" minutes={30} />

Each detected event is a short snippet of waveform, a point in a space with as many dimensions as samples. Spikes from the same neuron cluster together; PCA reduces the snippets to a few features and clustering separates the neurons (Week 7 and Day 3). Modern sorters such as Kilosort instead fit a template for each neuron across many channels and subtract them, which also handles overlapping spikes.

In the first two principal components the snippets form two clouds, and k-means assigns 99.5% of matched spikes to the right neuron. This is an easy case: two neurons with well-separated shapes on a clean channel. With dozens of neurons, similar shapes, overlapping spikes and electrode drift, single-channel sorting fails, which is why modern probes record each spike on many sites and sorters such as Kilosort match multi-channel templates.

<Step n={4} title="Is it one neuron?" minutes={25} />

A real neuron cannot fire twice within its refractory period of about 2 ms. A sorted unit with many interspike intervals shorter than that is contaminated, or is several neurons merged. Count the **refractory violations** of each sorted unit and of a unit made by merging both.

The sorted units have almost no intervals under 2 ms (0% and 0.2%), but the merged unit has 1.5%, because the second neuron's spikes are free to fall within the first's refractory period. That answers the prediction: about 1 to 2%, roughly the rate of the other neuron times the 2 ms window, a little reduced here by the dead time. A small number, but far above a single neuron's near zero, which is why refractory violations are a standard quality metric.
