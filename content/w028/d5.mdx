Each method measures something that is related to neural activity by a transformation, and an analysis must model that transformation. Calcium indicators turn each spike into a slow rise and decay of fluorescence. The **BOLD** signal of fMRI follows changes in blood oxygenation that lag neural activity by seconds. EEG measures the electric field that reaches the scalp, which only many aligned neurons, active together, can produce.

<Step n={2} title="Calcium imaging" minutes={30} />

When a neuron fires, calcium floods in; a genetically encoded indicator such as GCaMP fluoresces more brightly while calcium is high, then decays over hundreds of milliseconds. A simple model, with frames every $\Delta t$:

$$
c_t = \gamma c_{t-1} + s_t, \qquad F_t = c_t + \varepsilon_t, \qquad \gamma = e^{-\Delta t/\tau},
$$

where $s_t$ is the number of spikes in frame $t$. Fluorescence is a leaky integration (Week 10) of spikes plus noise. **Deconvolution** inverts it: $\hat s_t = F_t - \gamma F_{t-1}$, clipped at zero because spike counts cannot be negative.

Raw fluorescence correlates only 0.35 with the true spikes in each frame, because every spike's trace lingers for many frames. Deconvolution raises the correlation to 0.69. The rest is lost to noise: a single spike adds 1 unit of fluorescence against noise of sd 0.3, and spikes close in time blur together. Real pipelines, such as OASIS or the deconvolution in Suite2p, add sparsity priors and fit the indicator's dynamics, but none recovers exact spike times from imaging alone.

<Step n={3} title="fMRI and the general linear model" minutes={35} />

Active neurons use energy, and within a second or two blood flow increases more than oxygen use does, so the local blood becomes more oxygenated, which changes the MRI signal. The **haemodynamic response** to a brief burst of activity peaks about 5 s later and dips below baseline at around 15 s. Logothetis and colleagues (2001, today's deep read) showed that BOLD tracks the local field potential, the input to an area, better than its spiking output.

Analyses treat BOLD as neural activity convolved with a haemodynamic response function (HRF), and fit the result with a **general linear model**, ordinary least squares on predicted regressors (Week 5), testing whether each condition's weight is non-zero.

With the haemodynamic model the fit recovers an effect of 2.02 (true 2.0) with t = 93 and explains 97% of the variance. The raw boxcar, which assumes BOLD follows activity instantly, explains only 34%: in a 20 s block design the 5 s lag puts much of each block's response in the wrong place. This is a toy (a single voxel, white noise, a perfectly known HRF); real fMRI data add slow drifts, autocorrelated noise, head motion and an HRF that varies between regions and people.

<Step n={4} title="EEG and synchrony" minutes={20} />

A scalp electrode sums the fields of millions of neurons, each tiny and attenuated by distance and by the skull. Sources oscillating with random phases largely cancel; sources in phase add. Pyramidal cells, aligned in parallel with their dendrites perpendicular to the cortical surface, add best, which is why they are thought to dominate the EEG.

In phase, 10,000 unit sources sum to 10,000. With random phases the sum's amplitude averages 98 in root-mean-square terms, about $\sqrt N = 100$, though single draws range widely, from 6 to 232. That answers the prediction: in-phase sources produce about 100 times the signal, $\sqrt N$. The scalp EEG is therefore dominated by whatever populations are synchronised, which is why it is so sensitive to rhythms and so blind to the asynchronous activity that carries much of the brain's information.
