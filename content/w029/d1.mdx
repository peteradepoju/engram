<Step n={1} title="The ionic setting" minutes={20} />

A neuron is a bag of salty water in a bath of differently salty water. The membrane between them is a lipid bilayer about 4 nm thick that ions cannot cross on their own; they cross through **ion channels**, proteins that open a pore selective for particular ions, and are moved against their gradients by **pumps**, which spend energy (Day 5). The pumps keep the concentrations roughly as follows in a mammalian neuron (values vary between cells and authors by tens of per cent):

| Ion | Inside (mM) | Outside (mM) | Ratio out/in |
|---|---|---|---|
| K⁺ | 140 | 5 | 0.036 |
| Na⁺ | 12 | 145 | 12 |
| Cl⁻ | 7 | 110 | 16 |
| Ca²⁺ | 0.0001 | 2 | 20,000 |

Hodgkin, Huxley and Katz worked on the squid giant axon, whose concentrations are higher (about 400 mM potassium inside and 440 mM sodium outside, as the squid lives in seawater) but in the same pattern. Potassium is concentrated inside; sodium, chloride and calcium outside.

Each gradient is a store of energy. If the membrane lets potassium through, potassium diffuses out, down its gradient, carrying positive charge. The inside becomes negative, and the negative inside pulls potassium back. The voltage at which the two effects balance, so that there is no net flow, is potassium's **equilibrium** or **reversal potential**, $E_K$.

<Step n={2} title="Deriving the Nernst potential" minutes={30} />

At equilibrium, the Boltzmann distribution from statistical physics gives the probability of finding a particle in a state of energy $U$ as proportional to $e^{-U/k_BT}$. An ion of valence $z$ at electrical potential $V$ has energy $U = zeV$, where $e$ is the elementary charge. That is enough to find the balance point.

<Derivation title="The Nernst equation">
<div>

Let the inside be at potential $V_{in}$ and the outside at $V_{out}$, and define the membrane potential as $V = V_{in} - V_{out}$. At equilibrium the ratio of concentrations equals the ratio of Boltzmann factors:

$$
\frac{c_{in}}{c_{out}} = \frac{e^{-zeV_{in}/k_BT}}{e^{-zeV_{out}/k_BT}} = e^{-zeV/k_BT}.
$$

</div>
<div>

Take logarithms and solve for the voltage at which this holds:

$$
E = \frac{k_BT}{ze}\ln\frac{c_{out}}{c_{in}} = \frac{RT}{zF}\ln\frac{c_{out}}{c_{in}}.
$$

The second form multiplies top and bottom by Avogadro's number: $R$ is the gas constant and $F$ the Faraday constant, the charge of a mole of electrons.

</div>
<div>

The factor $V_T = RT/F$ is the **thermal voltage**, 26.7 mV at body temperature (310 K). In base-10 logarithms, $E = 61.5\,\text{mV}\times\frac{1}{z}\log_{10}(c_{out}/c_{in})$ at 37 °C: each tenfold gradient of a monovalent ion is worth about 60 mV.

</div>
</Derivation>

The same result follows from asking when the flux is zero. The Nernst-Planck equation writes the flux of an ion as diffusion down its gradient plus drift in the electric field, $J = -D\left(\frac{dc}{dx} + \frac{z c}{V_T}\frac{dV}{dx}\right)$. Setting $J = 0$ and integrating across the membrane gives the Nernst equation again: the derivation is the first exercise of the Forge.

Potassium's reversal potential is about -89 mV, sodium's +67 mV, chloride's -74 mV and calcium's +132 mV. A neuron at rest, at about -70 mV, sits much closer to $E_K$ than to $E_{Na}$, which already suggests that the resting membrane is mostly permeable to potassium. Note the sign for chloride: its gradient points the same way as sodium's, but its negative charge flips the potential. For the squid axon at Hodgkin and Huxley's 6.3 °C, $E_K$ is about -72 mV.

<Callout kind="note">

The Nernst potential is a property of one ion's gradient, not of the membrane. It tells you where that ion's current reverses: below $E_K$ potassium flows in, above it potassium flows out. Whether the membrane actually sits near $E_K$ depends on which channels are open, which is tomorrow's subject.

</Callout>

<Step n={3} title="Drift and diffusion in simulation" minutes={30} />

The Boltzmann distribution is not an extra assumption: it is where any collection of particles ends up when they diffuse randomly and drift in a force field. You can see it with the random walks of Week 24. Put 10,000 positive ions in a box of unit width, with a potential that rises linearly across it by $\Delta V = 2V_T$, so each ion feels a constant push towards the left. Each step, an ion drifts by $-\Delta V\,\Delta t$ (in units where $D = 1$ and voltages are measured in $V_T$) and diffuses by a Gaussian kick of variance $2\Delta t$; ions bounce off the walls.

A 20 μm cell body needs about 5.5 million ions to charge to 70 mV, out of 350 billion potassium ions inside: a fraction of $1.6 \times 10^{-5}$, about 0.002%. That answers the prediction. The membrane potential is set by a thin layer of charge at the membrane, while the bulk solutions on either side stay neutral and their concentrations barely move, which is why the Nernst potentials can be treated as constants. The fraction scales as one over the diameter, because area grows as $d^2$ and volume as $d^3$. In a 0.2 μm process it reaches 0.16% per charging, so in thin axons and dendrites that fire repeatedly, concentrations do shift, and the pumps must work harder: a point Day 5 and the Forge return to.

How can a membrane let potassium through but not sodium, an ion that is smaller? Today's paper answered that with the first atomic structure of a potassium channel. Its selectivity filter is a narrow ring of carbonyl oxygens spaced to fit a potassium ion stripped of its water, so that potassium loses its water shell at almost no energy cost. A sodium ion is too small to touch all the oxygens at once, so shedding its water costs more than the filter gives back. The channel passes about $10^8$ potassium ions per second while letting through about one sodium ion for every thousand potassium ions.
