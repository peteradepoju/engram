<Predict question="A membrane is 20 times more permeable to potassium than to sodium, with the concentrations of yesterday's table. Where does its voltage settle: at E_K (about -89 mV), at about -65 mV, or halfway between E_K and E_Na (about -11 mV)?" />

<Step n={1} title="Many ions, one voltage" minutes={20} />

If the membrane were permeable only to potassium it would sit at $E_K$; only to sodium, at $E_{Na}$. With both, potassium flows out at any voltage above $E_K$ and sodium flows in at any voltage below $E_{Na}$, so between the two there is a voltage where the outward potassium current exactly cancels the inward sodium current. The membrane settles there: no net current, so no further charging. Which way it leans depends on the relative permeabilities: the more permeable ion has the stronger pull.

To find the voltage you need a formula for each ion's current as a function of voltage, and that needs a model of how ions cross the membrane. David Goldman (1943) proposed the simplest: the ions move independently, and the electric field across the thin membrane is constant, so the potential falls linearly from one side to the other.

<Step n={2} title="The constant-field equations" minutes={30} />

<Derivation title="The Goldman-Hodgkin-Katz current equation">
<div>

Inside a membrane of thickness $L$, the flux of an ion of valence $z$ obeys the Nernst-Planck equation from yesterday, with a constant field $dV/dx = -V/L$ (the potential falls from $V$ inside, at $x = 0$, to 0 outside, at $x = L$). Write $u = zV/V_T$:

$$
J = -D\left(\frac{dc}{dx} - \frac{u}{L}c\right).
$$

</div>
<div>

With $J$ constant through the membrane (nothing piles up in steady state), this is a first-order linear equation for $c(x)$. Multiply by the integrating factor $e^{-ux/L}$, which turns the left side into a derivative:

$$
\frac{d}{dx}\left(c\,e^{-ux/L}\right) = -\frac{J}{D}e^{-ux/L}.
$$

</div>
<div>

Integrate from $x = 0$, where the concentration at the membrane's edge is $\beta c_{in}$, to $x = L$, where it is $\beta c_{out}$ ($\beta$ is a partition coefficient for how readily the ion enters the membrane):

$$
\beta\left(c_{out}e^{-u} - c_{in}\right) = \frac{JL}{Du}\left(e^{-u} - 1\right).
$$

</div>
<div>

Solve for $J$, collect the constants into the **permeability** $P = D\beta/L$, and multiply by $zF$ to get the current density, counted positive outward:

$$
I = P\,zF\,u\,\frac{c_{in} - c_{out}e^{-u}}{1 - e^{-u}}.
$$

At $u = 0$ this is pure diffusion, $PzF(c_{in} - c_{out})$; it is zero when $c_{in} = c_{out}e^{-u}$, which is the Nernst equation.

</div>
</Derivation>

Now add the currents of potassium, sodium and chloride and set the total to zero. For monovalent ions the factor $u/(1 - e^{-u})$ is common to the cations and, with a sign flip of $u$, to the anion, and it cancels, leaving the **Goldman-Hodgkin-Katz voltage equation**:

$$
V_{rest} = V_T\ln\frac{P_K[K]_{out} + P_{Na}[Na]_{out} + P_{Cl}[Cl]_{in}}{P_K[K]_{in} + P_{Na}[Na]_{in} + P_{Cl}[Cl]_{out}}.
$$

Chloride's inside and outside are swapped because of its negative charge. Only ratios of permeabilities matter. If one permeability dominates, the equation reduces to that ion's Nernst potential.

With potassium 20 times more permeable than sodium, the membrane rests at -65 mV, which answers the prediction: well below halfway, but 24 mV short of $E_K$, because even a small sodium permeability pulls hard against a large sodium gradient. Adding chloride permeability moves rest only to -67 mV, because chloride is close to equilibrium here. And if the permeabilities swap so that sodium is 20 times more permeable, the voltage swings to +54 mV, close to $E_{Na}$. That is the action potential in one line: a spike is a brief switch from a potassium-dominated to a sodium-dominated membrane. This was Hodgkin and Katz's sodium hypothesis, today's paper.

<Step n={3} title="Fitting the Hodgkin-Katz experiment" minutes={30} />

How do you measure a permeability ratio? Hodgkin and Katz changed the bathing solution and watched the voltage. If the resting membrane were permeable only to potassium, the resting potential would follow $E_K$, a straight line of slope 55 mV per tenfold change in $[K]_{out}$ at the squid's 6.3 °C. It does at high external potassium, but at low potassium it flattens, because there the small sodium term in the numerator is no longer negligible. The shape of the bend gives the ratio $P_{Na}/P_K$.

The fit gives $P_{Na}/P_K \approx 0.041$, close to the value of 0.04 that Hodgkin and Katz reported for the resting squid axon (the data here were simulated from that value, so the fit should find it). At high potassium the curve meets the Nernst line with its slope of 55 mV per decade; at 20 mM, close to the potassium of squid blood, the resting potential is 15 mV above $E_K$. Hodgkin and Katz did the same for sodium: replacing external sodium with choline left the resting potential almost unchanged but shrank the spike's peak by about the amount the Nernst equation predicts for sodium, the decisive evidence for the sodium hypothesis.

<Callout kind="history">

The idea that the resting potential is a potassium potential goes back to Julius Bernstein in 1902, who also guessed that during a spike the membrane becomes permeable to everything, so that the voltage should fall to zero. When Hodgkin and Huxley (1939), and Cole and Curtis, first recorded inside a squid axon, the spike overshot zero by tens of millivolts. Bernstein's theory could not explain the overshoot; a selective rise in sodium permeability could.

</Callout>

<Step n={4} title="A steady leak" minutes={25} />

At the resting potential the net current is zero, but that does not mean nothing flows. Potassium leaks out, because rest is above $E_K$, and sodium leaks in, because rest is below $E_{Na}$, in equal and opposite currents. Rest is a **steady state**, like a bath with the tap running and the plug out, not an equilibrium.

At rest the outward potassium current and the inward sodium current are each about 19 units, and they cancel exactly. The sodium that leaks in and the potassium that leaks out would, given enough time, run the gradients down, and the resting potential with them. The Na⁺/K⁺ pump prevents that by moving the ions back, at a cost in ATP that Day 5 estimates and the Forge simulates. Notice also that the GHK equation describes a cell with no pump: a pump that moves three sodium ions out for every two potassium ions in carries its own outward current, which makes rest a few millivolts more negative.

<Key title="Before you go">
<ul>
<li>A membrane permeable to several ions settles where their currents cancel, a permeability-weighted compromise.</li>
<li>Goldman's constant-field assumption gives the GHK current and voltage equations.</li>
<li>The resting membrane is mostly potassium-permeable; a spike is a switch to sodium dominance.</li>
<li>Rest is a steady state with continuous leaks, maintained by pumps.</li>
