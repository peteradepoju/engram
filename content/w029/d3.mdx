<Step n={1} title="Specific properties and cell properties" minutes={25} />

A lipid bilayer is an insulator about 4 nm thick between two conductors, the salty solutions on each side: a capacitor. Its capacitance per unit area, the **specific capacitance** $c_m$, is close to 1 μF/cm² in almost every cell; Gentet, Stuart and Clements (2000), today's paper, measured it directly in patches pulled from neurons and found about 0.9 μF/cm². The membrane's resistance to current, set by its open channels, is given per unit area as a **specific resistance** $r_m$, in Ω·cm², typically 10 to 100 kΩ·cm² in neurons at rest.

A cell with membrane area $A$ then has

$$
C = c_m A, \qquad R_{in} = \frac{r_m}{A}, \qquad \tau = R_{in}C = r_m c_m.
$$

A bigger cell has more capacitance and lower input resistance, because it has more membrane in parallel, but the area cancels in the product: the time constant is a property of the membrane alone. With $r_m$ = 20 kΩ·cm² and $c_m$ = 1 μF/cm², $\tau$ = 20 ms in a cell of any size. That answers the prediction.

A bare 10 μm sphere has a capacitance of 3 pF and an input resistance of 6.4 GΩ: a few picoamperes move it by 10 mV. A 40 μm sphere has 50 pF and 400 MΩ. All three have the same 20 ms time constant. Real neurons have far more membrane in their dendrites than in their cell bodies, so a cortical pyramidal cell typically measures 100 to 200 pF and 50 to 200 MΩ, and small neurons such as cerebellar granule cells, with few dendrites, have input resistances of a gigaohm or more. A high input resistance makes a neuron sensitive: one synapse, or the opening of a single channel, can move its voltage noticeably.

<Step n={2} title="Recording from a cell" minutes={20} />

Until the late 1970s, the voltage inside a neuron was recorded with a **sharp electrode**, a glass micropipette with a tip under 0.1 μm, pushed through the membrane. Erwin Neher and Bert Sakmann's **patch clamp** changed that. A blunter pipette, with a tip about 1 μm across, is pressed against the membrane and gentle suction forms a seal of more than a gigaohm between glass and lipid. In the **whole-cell** configuration a further pulse of suction ruptures the patch under the pipette, so that the pipette's solution becomes continuous with the cytoplasm, and the amplifier can either:

- **Current clamp**: inject a chosen current and record the voltage, as in the step responses above. This is how firing patterns are measured.
- **Voltage clamp**: hold the voltage at a chosen value by feedback and record the current needed to hold it. Because the voltage is fixed, the capacitive current is zero except at steps, and what is recorded is the current through the channels. This is how the currents of Week 30 were separated.

In both, the current flows through the pipette's tip and the ruptured patch, which together have a **series** or **access resistance** $R_s$, typically 5 to 20 MΩ. It sits between the amplifier and the cell and is the main source of error in whole-cell recording.

<Step n={3} title="The membrane test" minutes={35} />

Electrophysiologists check every whole-cell recording with a **membrane test**: a small voltage-clamp step, say 10 mV, applied repeatedly. The current it draws reveals the three numbers that matter. Model the cell as $R_{in}$ and $C$ in parallel, reached through $R_s$. At the instant of the step the capacitor's voltage cannot jump, so the current is $\Delta V/R_s$; it then decays, as the capacitor charges, to the steady value $\Delta V/(R_s + R_{in})$; and the extra charge that flowed during the transient is what the capacitor took up.

The estimates come back as 9.9 MΩ, 200 MΩ and 100 pF, within about 1% of the truth, and the transient decays with a time constant just under 1 ms. The factor $((R_s + R_{in})/R_{in})^2$ corrects for the fact that the cell's voltage reaches only $R_{in}/(R_s + R_{in})$ of the command; it is 1.1 here and matters more as the access worsens. In a real recording the first sample underestimates the peak, because the amplifier's filter rounds it off, so commercial software fits an exponential to the decay and extrapolates back to the step instead.

<Derivation title="Why the transient charge measures capacitance">
<div>

During the step, $I = (\Delta V - V_m)/R_s$ and $C\,dV_m/dt = I - V_m/R_{in}$. The current decays from $I_0 = \Delta V/R_s$ to $I_{ss} = \Delta V/(R_s + R_{in})$ with time constant $\tau_c = C R_s R_{in}/(R_s + R_{in})$, the capacitance times the two resistances in parallel.

</div>
<div>

The charge above the steady current is the area of that exponential:

$$
Q = (I_0 - I_{ss})\tau_c = \Delta V\frac{R_{in}}{R_s(R_s + R_{in})}\cdot\frac{C R_s R_{in}}{R_s + R_{in}} = C\Delta V\left(\frac{R_{in}}{R_s + R_{in}}\right)^2.
$$

</div>
<div>

Rearranging gives the estimate used above. When $R_s \ll R_{in}$ it reduces to $C = Q/\Delta V$: the charge needed to move a capacitor by $\Delta V$.

</div>
</Derivation>

<Step n={4} title="When the clamp is not a clamp" minutes={25} />

In voltage clamp the amplifier sets the voltage at its end of the pipette, not in the cell. When a current $I$ flows, the cell's voltage differs from the command by $IR_s$. For the 10 pA of a single synapse that is a tenth of a millivolt and harmless. For the sodium current of an action potential, several nanoamperes, it is a disaster.

With 10 MΩ of access, a 5 nA current shifts the cell's voltage by 50 mV, and the clamp needs 1 ms to settle, longer than sodium channels take to open. The recorded current would then be that of a cell whose voltage is out of control. Amplifiers compensate for most of $R_s$ electronically (80% compensation cuts these numbers fivefold), and experimenters shrink the currents by working in reduced sodium, at low temperature, or on small patches of membrane. A second problem is the **space clamp**: a pipette at the cell body controls the voltage there, but not in distant dendrites or the axon, whose voltage leaks away along the cable (Week 32).

Both problems explain the choice of the squid giant axon in the 1940s and 1950s. At half a millimetre across it could take a long silver wire threaded along its axis, which clamped a long stretch of membrane to the same voltage at once with negligible series resistance. Week 30 begins with what Hodgkin and Huxley recorded with it.

<Key title="Before you go">
<ul>
<li>Membranes have about 1 μF/cm²; cells differ in area, so their capacitance and input resistance scale with size.</li>
<li>The membrane time constant depends only on the membrane, not on the cell's size.</li>
<li>Whole-cell patch clamp records in current clamp or voltage clamp, through a series resistance.</li>
<li>A voltage step reveals series resistance, input resistance and capacitance.</li>
<li>Series resistance and poor space clamp distort large, fast currents.</li>
