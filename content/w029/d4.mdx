<Step n={1} title="Ohm's law for ions" minutes={20} />

The models from here on describe each kind of channel by a **conductance** $g_i$ and a reversal potential $E_i$, and write its current as

$$
I_i = g_i(V - E_i).
$$

The term $V - E_i$ is the **driving force**: the current is zero at the reversal potential and grows linearly with distance from it, outward above and inward below. This is a linearisation of the GHK current. It is exact when the concentrations on both sides are equal, and otherwise the GHK current **rectifies**: for potassium, with much more inside than outside, outward current flows more easily than inward current. Rectification is modest over the voltages neurons visit, so the linear form is standard, but it is worth knowing how large the error is.

Fifty millivolts above $E_K$ the GHK current is 52 units, against 32 for the linear approximation; fifty below it is 22 units inward, against 32. The same driving force pushes 2.4 times more current out than in. Some channels rectify far more strongly than this, because of how they are built rather than because of the gradients: the inward-rectifier potassium channels are blocked by internal magnesium and polyamines at depolarised voltages, and NMDA receptors are blocked by external magnesium near rest, a property that makes them detectors of coincident input (Weeks 32 and 33).

<Step n={2} title="Conductances in parallel" minutes={25} />

Put a leak conductance $g_L$ with reversal $E_L$ in parallel with an excitatory synaptic conductance $g_e$ (reversal $E_e \approx 0$ mV, for the mixed cation channels of glutamate receptors) and an inhibitory one $g_i$ (reversal $E_i \approx -70$ mV for the chloride channels of GABA-A receptors). The membrane equation becomes

$$
C\frac{dV}{dt} = -g_L(V - E_L) - g_e(V - E_e) - g_i(V - E_i) + I.
$$

Collecting terms shows two things at once. The voltage relaxes towards a conductance-weighted average of the reversal potentials, and it does so with an **effective time constant** that shrinks as conductance is added:

$$
V_\infty = \frac{g_LE_L + g_eE_e + g_iE_i + I}{g_L + g_e + g_i}, \qquad \tau_{eff} = \frac{C}{g_L + g_e + g_i}.
$$

The weighted average is the conductance version of the GHK equation's weighted compromise from Day 2.

On its own the inhibitory conductance moves the voltage by exactly 0 mV, yet it cuts the excitatory response from 6.4 mV to 3.3 mV and halves the time constant, from 18 to 9.5 ms. That answers the prediction. This is **shunting inhibition**: by adding conductance, it short-circuits the current that excitation injects. It is invisible in a recording of the voltage alone and shows up only as a change in input resistance. Because the conductance appears in the denominator, it acts on small inputs roughly by division.

<Step n={3} title="Shunting inhibition and firing" minutes={35} />

If shunting divides voltage responses, does it divide firing rates too? That would make it a way to control a neuron's gain, a multiplication the brain seems to need, for example when attention scales visual responses. Holt and Koch (1997), today's paper, showed that in an integrate-and-fire neuron it does not: shunting inhibition subtracts. You can check their argument numerically. Use the conductance-based leaky integrate-and-fire neuron of Week 21, with the shunt included in the leak.

Each 10 nS of shunt raises the rheobase by 200 pA, the extra current needed to hold the voltage at threshold against it, but the slope at high currents does not fall: it is 110, 122 and 139 Hz per nA. A divisive effect would have cut the slope to a half and a third. The reason is that once the neuron fires, its voltage spends its time between reset and threshold, a fixed range, so the shunt draws a roughly fixed current, $g_i(\bar V - E_i)$, where $\bar V$ is the average voltage between spikes. A fixed current subtracts.

Shunting can divide under other conditions. Chance, Abbott and Reyes (2002) showed in real neurons that when the shunt comes with a matching increase in synaptic noise, as background synaptic input does, the f-I curve's gain falls: noise and conductance together divide. Whether inhibition subtracts or divides depends on the neuron's regime, which is why the question was argued over for years.

<Step n={4} title="The high-conductance state" minutes={25} />

In a slice, a cortical neuron's input resistance is set by its leak. In an awake brain, thousands of synapses are active at any moment, and the total synaptic conductance can be three to five times the leak (Destexhe, Rudolph and Paré, 2003). That **high-conductance state** changes the neuron: by the second formula above, its effective time constant shrinks by the same factor, so it integrates over a few milliseconds rather than twenty, and responds faster and to more precisely timed input.

At rest in a slice, a synaptic potential takes 46 ms to decay to a tenth of its peak; with four times the leak in background conductance it takes 9 ms. The same cell, with the same channels, becomes a different computing device depending on the activity of the network around it: closer to an integrator in quiet conditions, closer to a coincidence detector in active ones.
