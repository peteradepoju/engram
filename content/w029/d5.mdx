<Step n={1} title="Single channels" minutes={20} />

In 1976 Erwin Neher and Bert Sakmann, today's paper, pressed a fire-polished glass pipette against a frog muscle fibre and recorded, for the first time, the current through single ion channels: rectangular pulses of a few picoamperes, opening and closing at random. Each open channel behaves like a fixed conductance $\gamma$, the **single-channel** or **unitary conductance**, typically 5 to 50 pS, so it passes a current $i = \gamma(V - E)$.

A membrane with $N$ channels, each open with probability $p$, then has a mean conductance

$$
G = N p \gamma.
$$

Every smooth conductance in a model is this product. Hodgkin and Huxley's maximal sodium conductance of 120 mS/cm² in squid, divided by the squid sodium channel's unitary conductance of about 4 pS, implies about 300 channels per square micrometre, close to what was later found by counting toxin binding sites.

<Step n={2} title="Counting channels from noise" minutes={35} />

Single channels can only be seen in small patches. In a whole cell, thousands of channels overlap into a smooth current. But the current is not perfectly smooth: because each channel opens at random, the number open fluctuates from trial to trial, and the size of the fluctuations carries information. If $n$ of $N$ channels are open, $n$ is binomial, so the current $I = in$ has

$$
\mathbb E[I] = iNp, \qquad \text{Var}[I] = i^2Np(1 - p) = iI - \frac{I^2}{N}.
$$

The variance is a parabola in the mean current: zero when no channels are open, zero again when all are, largest at half activation. Its initial slope is the unitary current $i$, and its curvature gives $N$. This is **non-stationary fluctuation analysis** (Sigworth, 1980): repeat a stimulus many times, compute the mean and variance across trials at each instant, and fit the parabola.

From nothing but means and variances the fit gives a unitary current of 1.02 pA and 482 channels, against the true 1 pA and 500. That answers the prediction. Here the peak open probability is 0.6, so the parabola is traced past its maximum and the channel number is well constrained; when channels never open with high probability, only the initial rising part is seen and $N$ becomes uncertain, a limitation real experiments face. The same logic applies to synapses: the variance-mean relation of synaptic currents across conditions of different release probability gives the number of release sites and the size of a single vesicle's effect, a method you will meet in Week 33.

<Callout kind="note">

The method assumes the channels are independent and identical, and that $N$ stays constant across sweeps. Channels that cluster and open together, or run-down of channels during a long recording, bias the estimates. Checking the assumptions is part of the analysis, as with any model fit (Week 17).

</Callout>

<Step n={3} title="The pump's bill" minutes={25} />

Day 2 found that sodium leaks in and potassium leaks out at rest. The **Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase** reverses this, using one ATP molecule to move three sodium ions out and two potassium ions in. Because it moves one net positive charge out per cycle, it is **electrogenic**: it carries an outward current of its own. In steady state the pump must remove sodium as fast as it leaks in.

A 20 pA sodium leak brings in $1.2 \times 10^8$ sodium ions a second, which takes $4.2 \times 10^7$ pump cycles and as many ATP molecules. The pump's own current, 6.7 pA, makes rest 1.3 mV more negative. Scaled to 86 billion such neurons, keeping them at rest costs about 0.29 W, a modest share of the brain's 20 W from Week 28: most of the budget goes on spikes and synaptic transmission, which let far more sodium in. The figures are rough, but the logic is that of Attwell and Laughlin's energy budget.

<Step n={4} title="How small can an axon be?" minutes={25} />

Noise from random channel gating matters little in a cell body with millions of channels, but the number of channels scales with membrane area. In a thin axon there may be only tens or hundreds of sodium channels within the length that must reach threshold together, and with a binomial count the relative fluctuation is

$$
\frac{\text{sd}(n)}{\mathbb E[n]} = \sqrt{\frac{1 - p}{Np}}.
$$

In a 1 μm axon the number of open channels fluctuates by 16%; at 0.1 μm, with only 190 channels and about 4 open, by 51%. Simulations of thin axons by Faisal, White and Laughlin (2005) found that below about 0.1 μm in diameter, the opening of a few sodium channels by chance becomes enough to fire spontaneous spikes. The thinnest axons found in the brain sit near that limit. Noise, not only wiring space or energy, constrains how small neural hardware can be.

  neuro="Every conductance is an average over randomly gating molecules. With many channels the average is smooth; with few, the noise is a fact of life, and evolution has had to build neurons that compute reliably with unreliable parts."

The variance of a signal is not a nuisance to average away: it tells you about the parts that make it.
