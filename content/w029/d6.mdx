The 20 μm cell keeps its resting potential within 10 mV for 864 s, about a quarter of an hour; the 1 μm compartment for only 43 s. The leak currents are set by the membrane area, but the stores of ions are set by the volume, so the time scales with volume over area, $d/6$ for a sphere: twenty times smaller, twenty times faster. This is why ouabain takes minutes to affect a cell body but much less to affect fine processes, and why pumps matter most where membranes are thin.

**Part B: sodium loading by spikes.** Each spike must move enough charge to depolarise the membrane by about 100 mV: $Q = c_m \Delta V$ per unit area. Sodium entry is larger than this minimum by a factor $f$, because some sodium current is cancelled by potassium current flowing at the same time (Week 30 measures $f$; take 1.3, a value typical of mammalian axons). In a cylindrical axon of diameter $d$, the volume per unit area of membrane is $d/4$.

In a 0.2 μm axon, typical of the unmyelinated fibres that make up much of the cortex's wiring, each spike adds 0.27 mM of sodium, and four or five spikes raise it by 10%. In a squid giant axon each spike adds about $10^{-4}$ mM, and it would take more than 10,000 spikes. Hodgkin and Huxley could treat their concentrations as constant; a model of a thin cortical axon firing in a burst should not. Activity-dependent changes in concentration, and the pump currents that follow them, are one source of slow adaptation in real neurons.

<Prove title="1. The Nernst equation from zero flux" task="The Nernst-Planck flux is $J = -D\left(\frac{dc}{dx} + \frac{zc}{V_T}\frac{dV}{dx}\right)$. Show that $J = 0$ everywhere implies $V_{in} - V_{out} = \frac{V_T}{z}\ln\frac{c_{out}}{c_{in}}$.">

With $J = 0$, $\frac{1}{c}\frac{dc}{dx} = -\frac{z}{V_T}\frac{dV}{dx}$, that is, $\frac{d}{dx}\ln c = -\frac{z}{V_T}\frac{dV}{dx}$.

Integrate from the inside to the outside: $\ln c_{out} - \ln c_{in} = -\frac{z}{V_T}(V_{out} - V_{in})$.

Rearranging, $V_{in} - V_{out} = \frac{V_T}{z}\ln\frac{c_{out}}{c_{in}}$, the Nernst equation, whatever the shape of the potential in between. Equivalently $c \propto e^{-zV/V_T}$: the Boltzmann distribution.

</Prove>

<Prove title="2. Where channel noise peaks" task="With $N$ independent channels each open with probability $p$ and unitary current $i$, show that the variance of the current is largest at $p = 1/2$, and that the maximum variance is $Ni^2/4$, reached when the mean current is half its maximum.">

The variance is $i^2Np(1 - p)$. Its derivative with respect to $p$ is $i^2N(1 - 2p)$, zero at $p = 1/2$, and the second derivative $-2i^2N$ is negative, so this is a maximum.

At $p = 1/2$ the variance is $i^2N/4$, and the mean current is $iN/2$, half the maximum $iN$.

In terms of the mean, $\text{Var} = iI - I^2/N$, a parabola with its vertex at $I = iN/2$: the same result, which is why recordings that reach half activation give the best estimates of $N$.

</Prove>

<Step n={5} title="Deep read and critique" minutes={25} />

Give Hodgkin and Katz (1949) its third pass and write your critique under the five headings. Focus on one question: **how strong is the evidence that the action potential is carried by sodium?** Consider what changing the external sodium concentration did to the resting potential, the spike's rate of rise and its peak, and how closely the peak followed the Nernst slope; why an internal electrode in a giant axon made the experiment possible; that the GHK equation, derived for steady states, is being applied to the peak of a transient; what alternative explanations the authors had to rule out (for example, effects of the substitute solutions themselves); and what was still missing until the voltage clamp of Week 30. <Callout kind="research">

The GHK equation assumes ions cross the membrane independently. In a real channel, ions move in single file and interact, which predicts measurable departures: the ratio of influx to efflux of an ion should differ from the value independence predicts (the Ussing flux ratio). Look up Hodgkin and Keynes's 1955 work on the potassium flux ratio in squid and cuttlefish axons, explain what an exponent above 1 means about how many ions occupy a channel at once, and connect it to the structure in Doyle and colleagues (1998).

</Callout>
