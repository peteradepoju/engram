<Step n={1} title="The experiment" minutes={20} />

By 1949 Hodgkin and Katz had shown that a spike is a rise in sodium permeability. The question was how that permeability depends on voltage and time. A spike cannot answer it directly, because during a spike the voltage changes the permeabilities and the permeabilities change the voltage, all at once. The **voltage clamp**, developed by Kenneth Cole and George Marmont and refined by Hodgkin, Huxley and Katz, breaks the loop. A feedback amplifier measures the membrane voltage, compares it with a command, and injects whatever current is needed to hold the two equal. With the voltage fixed, the capacitive current is zero after the step, so the injected current equals the current through the ion channels, and the channels' dependence on voltage and time can be studied one voltage at a time.

Two features of the squid made this possible (Week 29, Day 3). The giant axon, up to a millimetre across, could take a long silver wire along its axis, which held a long stretch of membrane at one voltage (no space-clamp problem) through a low resistance (little series-resistance error). And its large size meant large currents, measurable with the electronics of 1949.

<Step n={2} title="Clamping a simulated axon" minutes={30} />

With the voltage held at $V$, each gating variable obeys a linear equation with constant coefficients (Week 21), so it relaxes exponentially from its resting value to its steady value at $V$:

$$
x(t) = x_\infty(V) - \left(x_\infty(V) - x_\infty(V_{rest})\right)e^{-t/\tau_x(V)}, \qquad x_\infty = \frac{\alpha_x}{\alpha_x + \beta_x}, \quad \tau_x = \frac{1}{\alpha_x + \beta_x}.
$$

The clamp currents follow without integrating anything: $I_{Na} = \bar g_{Na}m^3h(V - E_{Na})$ and $I_K = \bar g_Kn^4(V - E_K)$. You will use the rate functions of Week 21 (in the modern convention, with rest at -65 mV).

At -45 mV the currents are small. From -25 mV upwards each step gives the pattern Hodgkin and Huxley recorded: an inward sodium current that peaks within a millisecond, then an outward potassium current that grows and stays. The peak sodium current is largest near -5 mV, about 1.5 mA/cm², and shrinks at higher voltages, even though more channels open, because the driving force $V - E_{Na}$ shrinks; at +55 and +75 mV, above $E_{Na}$, it flows outward. The potassium current only grows. That answers the prediction: sodium carries the early inward current and potassium the late outward one.

<Step n={3} title="Separating the currents" minutes={30} />

The recorded current is the sum of both. Hodgkin and Huxley separated them, in today's paper, by replacing most of the external sodium with choline, which does not pass through sodium channels: that shifts $E_{Na}$ and changes the sodium current while leaving the early potassium current alone. They then assumed that each current depends only on its own ion's concentrations, the **independence principle**, to reconstruct each component. Since the 1960s the same separation has been done more simply with toxins. **Tetrodotoxin** (TTX), from the puffer fish, blocks sodium channels; **tetraethylammonium** (TEA) blocks potassium channels. Record before and after TTX, and the difference is the sodium current.

Subtraction recovers the sodium current, and dividing by the driving force gives a sodium conductance that peaks at about 30 mS/cm² after 0.6 ms and then decays almost to nothing, while the potassium conductance rises after a delay to 24 mS/cm² and stays there. These two shapes, one transient and one delayed and sustained, are what the model had to explain. Note that dividing by the driving force amplifies noise where the driving force is small, so steps close to a reversal potential give poor estimates of conductance.

<Step n={4} title="Conductance, not current" minutes={25} />

Currents are awkward to compare across voltages: the sodium current shrinks as the step approaches $E_{Na}$ and reverses above it, even though the channels open more and more. Dividing by the driving force removes that. The peak sodium conductance rises smoothly and monotonically with voltage, in an S-shaped **activation curve**, the first sign that the channels are opened by a voltage-sensitive mechanism (tomorrow's subject).

The peak sodium conductance is half-maximal at about -12 mV, with a slope factor of 15.5 mV. The curve is shallower than the activation of a single $m$ gate would suggest, because the peak mixes activation ($m^3$) with inactivation, which has already begun by the time the peak is reached; separating the two needs the protocols of Day 3. Curves like this one are the first numbers reported for every newly characterised channel.

<Callout kind="history">

The experiments were done in the summer of 1949 at the Marine Biological Association in Plymouth, where squid were caught. The analysis took longer than the experiments: Huxley computed the propagated action potential by hand on a mechanical calculator, over about three weeks, because the Cambridge computer was out of action. The five papers appeared in 1952, and Hodgkin and Huxley shared the 1963 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with John Eccles.

<Key title="Before you go">
<ul>
<li>The voltage clamp cuts the feedback between voltage and channels, so currents can be measured at fixed voltages.</li>
<li>A depolarising step produces a fast, transient inward sodium current and a slow, sustained outward potassium current.</li>
<li>Ion substitution or toxins separate the two; dividing by the driving force gives conductances.</li>
<li>Peak sodium conductance rises sigmoidally with voltage, the activation curve.</li>
</ul>
