<Step n={1} title="Gates as charged particles" minutes={25} />

A channel that is opened by voltage must contain something that feels the electric field: charged parts of the protein, the **voltage sensor**. Model the simplest case, a particle with charge $ze$ that can sit in a closed position or an open position, with the open position further across the membrane. Last week's Boltzmann distribution gives the probability of each.

<Derivation title="The Boltzmann activation curve">
<div>

Moving the particle from closed to open changes its energy by $\Delta U = \Delta U_0 - zeV$: an intrinsic part $\Delta U_0$ from the protein, and an electrical part that a depolarisation lowers (for a positive charge moving outward).

</div>
<div>

At equilibrium, $\frac{p_{open}}{p_{closed}} = e^{-\Delta U/k_BT}$. With $p_{open} + p_{closed} = 1$:

$$
p_{open}(V) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-(V - V_{1/2})/k}}, \qquad k = \frac{k_BT}{ze} = \frac{V_T}{z},
$$

where $V_{1/2} = \Delta U_0/(ze)$ is the voltage at which the particle is equally likely in either position.

</div>
<div>

The steepness of the curve measures the charge: a slope factor $k$ means an effective gating charge $z = V_T/k$. At the squid's 6.3 °C, $V_T$ = 24.1 mV, so a slope factor of 24 mV means one elementary charge, and one of 6 mV means four.

</div>
</Derivation>

The same physics gives the rates. If the particle must cross an energy barrier to move, each direction's rate depends exponentially on voltage, which is why Hodgkin and Huxley's $\alpha$ and $\beta$ functions are exponentials or close to them. Their exact forms were chosen to fit data, not derived, and some (such as $\alpha_n$, of the form $x/(1 - e^{-x})$) are empirical.

<Step n={2} title="The delayed rise: why a power" minutes={30} />

If a single gate controlled the potassium channel, the conductance after a step would follow the gate: an exponential, rising at its fastest at the start. The measured conductance instead starts slowly. Suppose the channel opens only when $p$ independent gates are all open. Each gate rises as $n(t) = n_\infty - (n_\infty - n_0)e^{-t/\tau}$, and the channel's open probability is $n(t)^p$. Near the start, when $n_0$ is small, $n^p$ grows like $t^p$: a delay that lengthens with $p$. Hodgkin and Huxley found that $p = 4$ fitted their potassium data and $m^3h$ their sodium data.

The data rule out one or two particles decisively but separate three to six much less clearly, because the power is constrained mainly by the first fraction of a millisecond of the rise, a few noisy samples. Hodgkin and Huxley chose 4 as the smallest power that fitted well. Forty years later, potassium channels turned out to be built from four identical subunits, each with its own voltage sensor, which gave the choice a physical meaning.

<Step n={3} title="From time constants to rate functions" minutes={30} />

Fitting each step gives two numbers per voltage, $x_\infty(V)$ and $\tau_x(V)$. Inverting $x_\infty = \alpha/(\alpha + \beta)$ and $\tau_x = 1/(\alpha + \beta)$ gives the rates:

$$
\alpha_x(V) = \frac{x_\infty(V)}{\tau_x(V)}, \qquad \beta_x(V) = \frac{1 - x_\infty(V)}{\tau_x(V)}.
$$

Hodgkin and Huxley plotted these against voltage and chose simple functions that passed through the points. You will do the same for $\beta_n$, whose form is a plain exponential, $\beta_n = A e^{-(V + 65)/B}$, so that a straight-line fit to $\ln\beta_n$ against $V$ recovers $A$ and $B$.

With 5% errors in the fitted steady states and time constants, the line gives $\beta_n = 0.122\,e^{-(V + 65)/71.8}$, close to Hodgkin and Huxley's 0.125 and 80 mV. The rates scatter more than the fits they come from, because each rate combines two noisy numbers, and the scatter grows wherever $x_\infty$ is close to 0 or 1, since one of the two rates is then computed from a small difference. With real data of this quality, several functional forms fit almost equally well, which is one reason later models of the same channels use different formulas.

<Step n={4} title="Gating currents" minutes={20} />

Hodgkin and Huxley's particles were a hypothesis. If they were real charges moving across the membrane, their movement should itself be a tiny current, flowing for a fraction of a millisecond when the voltage steps, before the channels open. In 1973 Clay Armstrong and Francisco Bezanilla, today's paper, found it. They removed the ionic currents (by replacing permeant ions with impermeant ones and blocking the rest) and subtracted the large linear capacitive current using pulses of opposite sign. What remained was an outward current at the start of a depolarising step and an inward one at its end: the **gating current**, the sensors moving out and back.

Its properties matched the theory. The charge it carried saturated at large depolarisations, as a fixed number of charged sensors would; its voltage dependence was Boltzmann-like; and it preceded the opening of sodium channels. Later, cloning revealed the sensors themselves, the S4 segments of the channel protein, each studded with positive amino acids every third residue. Counting the charge they move, about 12 to 13 elementary charges per potassium channel, explains the steep voltage dependence that lets a few millivolts switch a neuron from rest to firing.
