<Step n={1} title="The dual effect" minutes={20} />

Depolarisation does two things to the sodium conductance, as Hodgkin and Huxley put it in the title of today's paper: it switches it on quickly and switches it off slowly. They described this with two kinds of gate in series. The $m$ gates open on depolarisation with a time constant of a fraction of a millisecond; the $h$ gate closes on depolarisation with a time constant of about a millisecond at 0 mV. A channel conducts only when all three $m$ gates and the $h$ gate are open, so $g_{Na} = \bar g_{Na}m^3h$. At rest, $h_\infty$ is about 0.6: four in ten sodium channels are already inactivated.

The two gates also differ in their steady states: $m_\infty$ rises with voltage, $h_\infty$ falls. Their product $m_\infty^3h_\infty$ is nearly zero at every voltage, which is why a steady depolarisation produces only a transient sodium current. Only the overlap of the two curves, around -60 to -40 mV, leaves a small steady **window current**.

<Step n={2} title="Steady-state inactivation" minutes={30} />

To measure $h_\infty$, hold the membrane at a prepulse voltage long enough for $h$ to settle (several of its time constants), then step to a fixed test voltage such as 0 mV. The $m$ gates open in a fraction of a millisecond, much faster than $h$ can change, so the peak sodium current measures how many channels were not inactivated at the prepulse voltage.

Availability is half-maximal at -62 mV, with a slope factor of 7.3 mV: steep, and centred close to rest. At -65 mV 60% of the sodium channels are available; at -55 mV only 28%. That answers the prediction: holding a neuron 10 mV above rest roughly halves the sodium current it can produce. This is why slow depolarisation, for example by raised external potassium, makes neurons less excitable rather than more, and why a strong sustained depolarising current can stop a neuron firing altogether, a state called depolarisation block.

<Step n={3} title="Recovery from inactivation" minutes={25} />

How quickly do inactivated channels come back? Inactivate them with a long step to 0 mV, return to rest for an interval $\Delta t$, then test. The fraction available after the interval traces the recovery of $h$ at the resting voltage: $h(\Delta t) = h_\infty - (h_\infty - h_{inact})e^{-\Delta t/\tau_h}$.

Recovery at -65 mV has a time constant of 8.5 ms in the squid model: after 4 ms only 38% of the channels are back, after 16 ms 85%. <Step n={4} title="Refractoriness and rebound" minutes={30} />

After a spike, $h$ is low (sodium channels inactivated) and $n$ is high (potassium channels still open). Both make a second spike harder: fewer sodium channels can open, and more potassium current opposes them. For a few milliseconds no stimulus can fire the neuron, the **absolute refractory period**; then a stronger-than-usual stimulus can, the **relative refractory period**. In Week 21's Forge you saw this happen; now you can measure it and explain it.

For 4 ms after the first stimulus no pulse up to 200 μA/cm² fires a second spike: the absolute refractory period. At 6 ms it takes 15 times the resting threshold, at 10 ms 3.4 times and at 14 ms 1.4 times: the relative refractory period, as $h$ recovers and $n$ closes. At 20 ms the threshold is slightly below its resting value, 0.9 times. The undershoot after the spike briefly hyperpolarised the membrane, which raised $h$ above its resting value, so for a while the neuron is more excitable than at rest. That same mechanism, pushed further, produces the next effect.

The same gates explain a stranger effect. Hyperpolarise the axon for a while and then release it: the neuron can fire a spike with no depolarising stimulus at all. During the hyperpolarisation $h$ rises above its resting value (more sodium channels become available) and $n$ falls (fewer potassium channels are open). When the voltage returns towards rest, the membrane is briefly more excitable than at rest, and the overshoot of the return carries it past threshold. Hodgkin and Huxley called this **anode-break excitation**; in neurons with dedicated currents for it, rebound firing after inhibition is a common way of generating rhythms.

<Key title="Before you go">
<ul>
<li>Sodium channels have fast activation (m) and slower inactivation (h) gates in series.</li>
<li>Prepulses reveal steady-state availability; paired pulses reveal recovery time.</li>
<li>Small sustained depolarisations inactivate many sodium channels and reduce excitability.</li>
<li>Refractoriness and anode-break excitation both come from the slow gates h and n, not from a fixed threshold.</li>
