<Step n={1} title="Currents during a spike" minutes={25} />

In a spike, as opposed to a voltage-clamp step, the voltage moves and the currents respond. During the rise, sodium current flows in and charges the membrane; during the fall, potassium current flows out and discharges it. If the two currents were perfectly separated in time, the sodium entering would equal the charge needed to depolarise the membrane, $C\,\Delta V$, and no more. But whenever both flow at once, some sodium current is cancelled by potassium current instead of charging the membrane, and both ions must later be pumped back at a cost in ATP (Week 29, Day 5).

The spike peaks at 41 mV, so the minimum charge is 106 nC/cm². The sodium charge that actually enters is 1,411 nC/cm², 13 times the minimum. That answers the prediction. Most of it enters after the peak, when it is no longer charging the membrane but fighting the potassium current, as the plot of the two currents shows.

<Step n={2} title="The sodium bill" minutes={25} />

Why is the squid model so wasteful? Look at the currents in your plot. The sodium current does not stop at the peak. Because $h$ closes slowly, sodium channels stay open through much of the falling phase, while the potassium current, which opens slowly too, is already large. For a millisecond or more the two currents flow against each other, sodium in and potassium out, and that cancelled charge is pure cost.

Two changes would reduce the waste: faster sodium inactivation, so that sodium stops flowing once the peak is reached, and delayed potassium activation, so that potassium starts only then. Today's paper, by Alle, Roth and Geiger (2009), measured the currents during action potentials in mammalian axons, the mossy fibres of the hippocampus at near body temperature, and found exactly that: sodium entry only about 1.3 times the minimum. Their measurements, and later ones by Carter and Bean (2009) on cell bodies, showed that mammalian spikes are far more economical than the squid model, though fast-spiking neurons pay more.

<Step n={3} title="Temperature" minutes={30} />

Hodgkin and Huxley measured at 6.3 °C and specified how their rates scale with temperature: all $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are multiplied by $\phi = Q_{10}^{(T - 6.3)/10}$, with $Q_{10} = 3$, meaning each rise of 10 °C speeds every gate threefold. The membrane capacitance and conductances do not change, so warming makes the gates faster relative to the membrane's own charging. That changes the spike's shape and its cost.

Warming from 6.3 to 26 °C narrows the spike from 1.5 to 0.22 ms and lowers its peak from 41 to 23 mV, while sodium entry falls from 13 to 2.2 times the minimum. At 18.5 °C the ratio is 4.0, close to the figure of about four often quoted for the squid axon. Faster gates mean that inactivation and potassium activation catch up with the rising phase, so the currents overlap less. The price is a smaller spike: in this model, warming beyond about 30 °C shrinks it towards the depolarisation produced by the stimulus alone, a form of heat block that real squid axons also show.

<Callout kind="warning">

A $Q_{10}$ of 3 applied to every rate is a convenience, not a law. Different channels have different temperature sensitivities, conductances themselves rise with temperature (with a $Q_{10}$ nearer 1.5), and pumps and synapses have their own. Simulating a mammalian neuron by warming the squid model gives the right direction of change but not the right numbers, which is why models of mammalian neurons are fitted to mammalian data.

</Callout>

<Step n={4} title="ATP per spike" minutes={25} />

The Na⁺/K⁺ pump spends one ATP for every three sodium ions it removes. So the cost of a spike follows from the sodium charge: the minimum $C\,\Delta V$ per unit area, times the overlap factor, times the neuron's membrane area, converted to ions and divided by three.

An economical mammalian spike in a neuron with 20,000 μm² of membrane costs about $5 \times 10^7$ ATP; the squid model's overlap would make it $5 \times 10^8$. Add the much larger cost of the synaptic currents the spike triggers in its targets, and the total reaches the $10^9$ ATP per spike of Week 28's budget. That answers the prediction from the other side: the squid's thirteenfold waste would have made a human brain unaffordable, and the efficiency Alle and colleagues found is part of what makes it possible.
