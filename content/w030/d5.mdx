<Step n={1} title="The channel zoo" minutes={20} />

The human genome codes for over 140 voltage-gated ion channel subunits, and each neuron expresses its own selection. A few families do most of the work in neuronal firing:

| Current | Opened by | Typical role |
|---|---|---|
| Fast Na⁺ (Nav1.x) | depolarisation | the upstroke of the spike |
| Persistent Na⁺ | depolarisation, no inactivation | boosts subthreshold depolarisation |
| Delayed-rectifier K⁺ (Kv) | depolarisation, slowly | repolarisation |
| A-type K⁺ | depolarisation, transient | delays the first spike, allows slow firing |
| M-type K⁺ (Kv7) | depolarisation, very slowly | spike-frequency adaptation |
| Ca²⁺ (L, N, P/Q, T types) | depolarisation | transmitter release, bursts, dendritic spikes |
| Ca²⁺-activated K⁺ (BK, SK) | internal calcium | afterhyperpolarisation, adaptation |
| HCN (I_h) | hyperpolarisation | rebound, resonance, pacemaking |

Each current shapes behaviour in a characteristic way. The A-type current, for example, converts a neuron that can only fire fast (class 2, like the HH model) into one that can fire arbitrarily slowly (class 1, Week 23), as Connor and Stevens showed in 1971. Calcium currents with calcium-activated potassium currents give bursting. The HCN current makes a neuron resonate at a preferred frequency of input.

<Step n={2} title="Searching parameter space" minutes={35} />

With many conductances, a model has many parameters, and measuring each one in the same cell is rarely possible. So modellers often search: draw parameter sets, simulate each, and keep those that reproduce the target behaviour. Prinz, Bucher and Marder (2004), today's paper, did this for a three-neuron circuit of the crab stomatogastric ganglion, simulating over 20 million versions, and found that very different circuits produced nearly identical rhythms.

You can see the same effect in a single HH neuron. The trick is to simulate hundreds of models at once: put each parameter in an array, one entry per model, and let NumPy update them all in each time step.

<Step n={3} title="Good models lie in a valley" minutes={25} />

The good models are not scattered at random: they line up. What matters for the firing rate here is mainly the balance of sodium to potassium conductance, so a model with more of one can compensate with more of the other. Individually the conductances vary several-fold; in combination they are tightly constrained.

Here the valley is close to a straight line, so the average of the good models is itself a good model, firing at 69 Hz. That is not guaranteed in general.

<Step n={4} title="Degeneracy, fitting and homeostasis" minutes={25} />

**Degeneracy** is the ability of different elements to produce the same output. Its consequences reach well beyond this model.

- **Fitting is not identification.** If many parameter sets fit a recording equally well, the fitted values of individual conductances mean little, unless other measurements break the tie. A good fit tells you the model can reproduce the data, not that its parameters are the cell's.
- **Variability is expected.** Measurements in identified neurons of the crab and lobster, cells that do the same job in every animal, find their channel expression varying two- to sixfold between animals, with correlations between channel types like the valley above (Schulz, Goaillard and Marder, 2006).
- **Averages can mislead.** Averaging the conductances of many good neurons works here because the valley is nearly straight, but when the set of good models is curved, the average lies outside it and the averaged model fails, as Golowasch and colleagues (2002) showed for a model neuron.
- **Homeostasis can exploit it.** If a neuron regulates its channel expression to keep some quantity, such as its average internal calcium, at a target, it will drift to some point in the valley, different in different cells. Models of this kind, by O'Leary, Marder and colleagues, reproduce the correlations seen in real neurons.

<Callout kind="research">
