<Step n={1} title="Separating time scales" minutes={20} />

A model can lose a variable without losing its behaviour if that variable is either much faster than the others, so that it is always at its steady state, or tied to another variable, so that one predicts the other. Both apply to Hodgkin-Huxley. The activation gate $m$ has a time constant of 0.1 to 0.5 ms; $h$ and $n$ have time constants of 1 to 9 ms. On the time scale of $h$ and $n$, $m$ is effectively instantaneous: $m \approx m_\infty(V)$.

<Step n={2} title="h and n in lockstep" minutes={25} />

The second fact is less obvious. Plot $h$ against $n$ during repetitive firing and the points fall close to a straight line: as potassium channels open, sodium channels inactivate, at similar rates. Rinzel (1985) used $h + n \approx 0.8$; you can fit the relation directly.

During firing, $h = 0.871 - 1.075n$, with a correlation of -0.995: knowing $n$ tells you $h$ almost exactly. Rinzel's rounder version, $h \approx 0.8 - n$, lies close to this line over the range of $n$ that firing visits. Why the two gates move together is not deep: both have similar time constants and opposite voltage dependence, so a depolarisation that opens one closes the other at a similar rate.

<Step n={3} title="The reduced model" minutes={30} />

With $m = m_\infty(V)$ and $h = a + bn$, two equations remain:

$$
C\frac{dV}{dt} = I - \bar g_{Na}m_\infty(V)^3(a + bn)(V - E_{Na}) - \bar g_Kn^4(V - E_K) - g_L(V - E_L), \qquad \frac{dn}{dt} = \alpha_n(1 - n) - \beta_n n.
$$

This is a planar system, so everything Week 22 taught applies: nullclines, fixed points and their stability, and limit cycles you can see.

The reduced model fires at every current tested, including 5 μA/cm², where the full model is silent, and always faster: 87 against 68 Hz at 10 μA/cm², 150 against 109 Hz at 40. That answers the prediction: the reduction keeps the spikes but not the numbers. The cause is the instantaneous $m$. In the full model sodium activation lags the voltage by a fraction of a millisecond, which gives the potassium current and inactivation time to resist; removing the lag makes the model more excitable. The phase-plane geometry, which is what you need to understand why the neuron fires, survives; quantitative predictions need the full model, or a reduced one refitted to its behaviour.

<Step n={4} title="The Morris-Lecar model" minutes={30} />

Catherine Morris and Harold Lecar (1981), today's paper, built a two-variable model directly from data, for the voltage oscillations of barnacle giant muscle fibres. It has a fast, non-inactivating calcium current that plays the part of sodium (so its activation is instantaneous, as in the reduction above) and a slower potassium current with a single gate $w$:

$$
C\frac{dV}{dt} = I - \bar g_{Ca}m_\infty(V)(V - E_{Ca}) - \bar g_Kw(V - E_K) - g_L(V - E_L), \qquad \frac{dw}{dt} = \phi\frac{w_\infty(V) - w}{\tau_w(V)},
$$

with $m_\infty$ and $w_\infty$ given by $\frac{1}{2}(1 + \tanh((V - V_1)/V_2))$ and $\tau_w = 1/\cosh((V - V_3)/(2V_4))$. Because it is so simple and so physiological, it became a standard teaching model, and Rinzel and Ermentrout (1989) gave two parameter sets that differ only in the potassium gate and show the two classes of excitability from Week 23.

With the first set, the model is silent up to 88 μA/cm² and jumps to 9.3 Hz at 89: class 2, starting through a Hopf bifurcation. With the second, it fires at 1.1 Hz at 40 μA/cm² and 3.8 Hz at 40.5, its rate rising continuously from zero: class 1, starting through a saddle-node on the limit cycle. The two sets differ only in the voltage dependence and speed of the potassium gate. Whether a neuron can fire arbitrarily slowly depends not on which currents it has but on their kinetics, a point Week 30 made with the A-type current.
