<Step n={1} title="Where the exponential comes from" minutes={25} />

Below threshold, the sodium channels' activation is fast (Day 1) and sits at the foot of its sigmoid curve, where a Boltzmann function is close to an exponential. With $h$ near its resting value, the sodium current is then

$$
I_{Na}(V) \approx -\bar g_{Na}h_0\,m_\infty(V)^3(E_{Na} - V) \propto e^{3(V - V_m)/k_m},
$$

an inward current that grows exponentially, with a slope factor a third of the activation's, since three gates must open. Add it to the leak, and the membrane equation becomes the **exponential integrate-and-fire** (EIF) model:

$$
C\frac{dV}{dt} = -g_L(V - E_L) + g_L\Delta_T\,e^{(V - V_T)/\Delta_T} + I.
$$

$\Delta_T$, the **slope factor**, sets how sharply spikes start; $V_T$ is where the exponential term's slope equals the leak's. When $V$ runs away, the model is reset after it passes a cut-off (say 0 mV). As $\Delta_T \to 0$, the exponential becomes a wall at $V_T$ and the EIF becomes the LIF neuron.

<Step n={2} title="The EIF model and its rheobase" minutes={25} />

Write the right-hand side as $C\,dV/dt = I - G(V)$, with $G(V) = g_L(V - E_L) - g_L\Delta_T e^{(V - V_T)/\Delta_T}$. A resting state is a voltage where $G(V) = I$ and $G$ is increasing. $G$ rises with the leak at low voltages and then turns down as the exponential takes over, so it has a maximum; once the input exceeds that maximum there is no resting state, and the neuron fires. Setting $G'(V) = 0$ gives $e^{(V - V_T)/\Delta_T} = 1$, so the maximum is at $V = V_T$, and the **rheobase**, the smallest steady current that fires, is

$$
I_{rh} = G(V_T) = g_L(V_T - E_L - \Delta_T).
$$

That answers the prediction. There is no voltage above which the neuron always fires: a brief excursion above $V_T$ can fall back if the input stops. And the rheobase depends on $\Delta_T$ as well as $V_T$: the softer the spike onset, the less current is needed.

<Step n={3} title="The dynamic I-V curve" minutes={40} />

How would you measure $\Delta_T$ and $V_T$ in a real neuron? Badel and colleagues (2008) gave a direct method. Inject a fluctuating current $I(t)$, record $V(t)$, and rearrange the membrane equation: the ionic current is $I_{ion} = I - C\,dV/dt$. Averaging $I_{ion}$ over all moments when the voltage was near some value $V$ gives the **dynamic I-V curve**, $\langle I_{ion}\rangle(V)$; for an EIF neuron it is exactly $G(V)$. Two details matter. Times near spikes are excluded, since the reset and refractoriness are not part of $G$. And $C$ must be known; it can be found by choosing the value that makes $I_{ion}$ least variable within a narrow voltage bin, because only the right $C$ removes the input's fluctuations completely.

<Step n={4} title="Sharp or soft spike onset" minutes={15} />

Fourcaud-Trocmé and colleagues, today's paper, showed why $\Delta_T$ matters beyond the shape of the upstroke. A neuron must turn fast fluctuations of its input into spikes; how fast it can follow them depends on how quickly the spike can start once threshold is approached. With a sharp onset (small $\Delta_T$) the neuron can follow inputs varying at hundreds of hertz; with a soft one, its response falls off above a cut-off frequency proportional to $1/\Delta_T$. The LIF neuron, with its infinitely sharp threshold, follows unrealistically fast inputs, and the quadratic integrate-and-fire neuron of Week 9, whose onset is too soft, too slow ones. The EIF, with $\Delta_T$ measured from real neurons, sits in between.

How sharp is a real neuron's onset? Recordings from cortical pyramidal cells show spikes rising more abruptly than the sodium channels' own voltage sensitivity would suggest, which led to a debate between explanations based on cooperative sodium channels and on spikes that begin in the axon and invade the soma. Week 32's cable theory is needed to follow it.
