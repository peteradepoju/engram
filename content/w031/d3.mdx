<Step n={1} title="Adding adaptation" minutes={20} />

The **adaptive exponential integrate-and-fire** model adds one slow current $w$ to yesterday's EIF:

$$
C\frac{dV}{dt} = -g_L(V - E_L) + g_L\Delta_Te^{(V - V_T)/\Delta_T} - w + I, \qquad \tau_w\frac{dw}{dt} = a(V - E_L) - w,
$$

and at each spike, $V \to V_r$ and $w \to w + b$. The adaptation current $w$ has two sources. **Subthreshold adaptation**, $a$, makes $w$ follow the voltage, like a slow potassium conductance (the M-current of Week 30's channel zoo). **Spike-triggered adaptation**, $b$, adds a fixed amount after each spike, like a calcium-activated potassium current. Together with $\tau_w$ and the reset $V_r$, these four parameters control the pattern of firing. Brette and Gerstner (2005, Week 21's paper) showed that with them, the model predicts the spike times of a detailed pyramidal-cell model well.

<Step n={2} title="A zoo of firing patterns" minutes={35} />

Electrophysiologists classify neurons by their responses to current steps: **tonic** (regular firing), **adapting** (intervals lengthen), **bursting** (groups of spikes separated by pauses), **phasic** (a few spikes at the start, then silence), and others. Naud and colleagues (2008), today's paper, mapped which AdEx parameters give which pattern. A useful measure is the adaptation index, the average normalised change between successive intervals,

$$
A = \frac{1}{N - 1}\sum_k\frac{\text{ISI}_{k+1} - \text{ISI}_k}{\text{ISI}_{k+1} + \text{ISI}_k},
$$

near 0 for tonic firing and positive for adaptation.

  import numpy as np
  import math
  import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

  def adex(I, a, b, tw, Vr, T=600.0, dt=0.05, C=200.0, gL=10.0, EL=-70.0, VT=-50.0, DT=2.0, trace=False):
      V, w, spikes, Vs = EL, 0.0, [], []
      for k in range(int(T / dt)):
          t = k * dt
          Iext = I if t >= 50 else 0.0
          dV = (-gL * (V - EL) + gL * DT * math.exp((V - VT) / DT) - w + Iext) / C
          dw = (a * (V - EL) - w) / tw
          V += dt * dV; w += dt * dw
          if V > 0:
              V = Vr; w += b; spikes.append(t)
          if trace:
              Vs.append(min(V, 0.0))
      return (np.array(spikes), np.array(Vs)) if trace else np.array(spikes)

  def adaptation_index(isi):
      return np.mean((isi[1:] - isi[:-1]) / (isi[1:] + isi[:-1]))

  def classify(spikes, T=600.0):
      if len(spikes) < 2 or T - spikes[-1] > 200:
          return "phasic"
      isi = np.diff(spikes)
      if isi.max() / isi.min() > 8:
          return "bursting"
      return "adapting" if adaptation_index(isi) > 0.02 else "tonic"

  sets = {"tonic": (0, 5, 30, -58, 500), "adapting": (0, 60, 200, -58, 500),
          "bursting": (0, 60, 100, -45, 300), "phasic": (30, 10, 200, -58, 350)}   # a (nS), b (pA), tau_w (ms), V_r (mV), I (pA)
  labels = {}
  fig, axes = plt.subplots(4, 1, sharex=True, figsize=(6, 6))
  for ax, (name, (a, b, tw, Vr, I)) in zip(axes, sets.items()):
      s, v = adex(I, a, b, tw, Vr, trace=True)

      print(f"{name:9s}: {len(s):2d} spikes, first intervals {np.round(np.diff(s)[:6], 1)}, classified as {labels[name]}")

The tonic set fires 59 spikes at a steady 9 ms interval; the adapting set's intervals stretch from 10 to over 40 ms; the bursting set fires groups of spikes 2 to 6 ms apart, separated by pauses of about 150 ms; and the phasic set fires twice and stops. The classifier labels all four correctly from the intervals alone. Real classification is messier: the same neuron can change pattern with the size of the step, or under neuromodulators.

<Step n={3} title="Why the reset decides bursting" minutes={30} />

The difference between the adapting and bursting sets is mostly the reset. Picture the $(V, w)$ phase plane. The $V$-nullcline, where $dV/dt = 0$, is $w = -g_L(V - E_L) + g_L\Delta_Te^{(V - V_T)/\Delta_T} + I$: a curve with a minimum near $V_T$. Trajectories to the right of its rising branch run away into a spike. After a spike the state jumps to $(V_r, w + b)$. If $V_r$ is low, well to the left of the minimum, the voltage must climb back slowly: one spike per climb, regular or adapting firing. If $V_r$ is high, near or past the minimum, the state lands where the voltage can run away again at once, unless $w$ has grown large enough to lift the nullcline above it. So the neuron fires spike after spike, each adding $b$, until $w$ is high enough to stop it; then $w$ decays during a pause, and the cycle repeats: bursting.

With the reset between -60 and -48 mV the neuron fires single spikes; at -46 mV it fires bursts of 3, and at -45 and -44 mV bursts of 5 and 7. That answers the prediction: raising the reset towards the region where spikes start turns regular firing into bursting. Real bursting neurons achieve a high effective reset with currents that depolarise the membrane after each spike, such as the calcium currents behind a depolarising afterpotential.

<Step n={4} title="Simple models for many cell types" minutes={20} />

The AdEx neuron is one of a family of simple models designed to be fitted to real neurons: the Izhikevich model of Week 9, with a quadratic rather than exponential term and the same reset-and-adapt logic, reproduces a similar zoo with four parameters. At scale, the Allen Institute has fitted generalised leaky integrate-and-fire models, with adaptation and spike-dependent threshold changes, to thousands of recordings from mouse and human cortex (Teeter and colleagues, 2018). Those fits served two purposes at once: cheap, accurate neuron models for large simulations, and a way to group neurons into types by their fitted parameters, a complement to the transcriptomic cell types of Week 28.

| Model | Variables | Spike onset | Adaptation | Good for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| LIF | 1 | hard threshold | none | analysis, large networks |
| EIF | 1 | exponential, from sodium | none | realistic onset, fitting |
| AdEx | 2 | exponential | subthreshold and spike-triggered | firing patterns, fitting |
| Izhikevich | 2 | quadratic | reset and recovery variable | cheap firing patterns |
| Hodgkin-Huxley | 4 or more | from channels | through channels | biophysics |
