<Predict question="A neuron needs 30 coincident excitatory inputs of 0.5 mV to reach threshold from rest. It receives thousands of independent Poisson inputs and fires at 20 Hz. Will its spikes be about as irregular as a Poisson process (CV near 1), or much more regular (CV well below 0.5)?" />

<Step n={1} title="The puzzle" minutes={20} />

The coefficient of variation of the interspike intervals, CV = sd/mean, is 1 for a Poisson process and 0 for a clock. Recordings from cortical neurons in behaving animals give CVs near 1. Softky and Koch asked whether that is compatible with neurons integrating their inputs. Their argument was simple: if a neuron must sum many small inputs to reach threshold, then by the law of large numbers the sum rises almost deterministically, and the neuron should fire at nearly regular intervals. They simulated integrate-and-fire neurons and a detailed pyramidal-cell model and found CVs far below those observed, and concluded that cortical neurons must instead detect rare coincidences of inputs on fast time scales, perhaps through active dendrites.

The argument was right about the model they tested and wrong about what follows from it, as you will see.

At the same output of about 21 Hz, the CV rises from 0.34 with no inhibition to 0.57, 0.67 and 0.72 as inhibition grows to 10,000 events per second. Nothing about the neuron changed, only its input, which now consists of large excitatory and inhibitory streams that nearly cancel. Real cortical neurons receive thousands of excitatory and inhibitory inputs, and recordings in vivo show large synaptic conductances of both signs (Week 29, Day 4).

<Step n={4} title="Mean and fluctuations" minutes={25} />

Why does balance work? Think of the voltage without a threshold, the **free membrane potential**. Each input is a jump that decays with time constant $\tau$; with Poisson arrivals (Week 24's shot noise), the free voltage has

$$
\mu = \tau\,(w_er_e - w_ir_i), \qquad \sigma^2 = \frac{\tau}{2}\left(w_e^2r_e + w_i^2r_i\right).
$$

The mean depends on the difference of the input rates, the variance on their sum. With excitation alone, $\mu$ sits above threshold and the neuron is **mean-driven**: the voltage climbs steadily after every reset, like a clock. In the balanced regime, $\mu$ sits below threshold and spikes are triggered by fluctuations, at random moments: the neuron is **fluctuation-driven**, and fires irregularly.

With excitation alone the free voltage would average 15.9 mV, above the 15 mV threshold, with an sd of only 2 mV: mean-driven. With the most inhibition the mean is 11.1 mV, below threshold, and the sd 7.3 mV: fluctuation-driven. The simulated free voltage agrees with the formulas (11.2 and 7.2 mV). These two numbers are all that the diffusion approximation of Week 24 needs to predict an integrate-and-fire neuron's rate and irregularity, and they are the basis of the theory of balanced networks.
