<Step n={1} title="From compartments to a cable" minutes={25} />

Cut a cylindrical dendrite of diameter $d$ into short pieces of length $\Delta x$. Each piece is a patch of membrane, a capacitor and a resistor as in Week 29, and neighbouring pieces are joined by the resistance of the cytoplasm between them. Current injected into one piece either crosses its membrane or flows along the core to its neighbours.

<Derivation title="The cable equation">
<div>

Write $r_m$ for the membrane's specific resistance (Ω·cm²), $c_m$ its specific capacitance (F/cm²) and $r_i$ the cytoplasm's resistivity (Ω·cm). A piece of length $\Delta x$ has membrane area $\pi d\Delta x$, and the core between two pieces has resistance $R_a = \frac{4r_i\Delta x}{\pi d^2}$.

</div>
<div>

Current conservation for piece $j$: the current arriving along the core from both neighbours equals the current leaving through the membrane,

$$
\frac{V_{j+1} - V_j}{R_a} + \frac{V_{j-1} - V_j}{R_a} = \pi d\Delta x\left(c_m\frac{dV_j}{dt} + \frac{V_j}{r_m}\right),
$$

with $V$ measured from rest.

</div>
<div>

Divide by $\pi d\Delta x$ and let $\Delta x \to 0$; the left side becomes a second derivative, $\frac{d}{4r_i}\frac{\partial^2V}{\partial x^2}$. Multiplying through by $r_m$:

$$
\lambda^2\frac{\partial^2V}{\partial x^2} = \tau\frac{\partial V}{\partial t} + V, \qquad \lambda = \sqrt{\frac{r_md}{4r_i}}, \quad \tau = r_mc_m.
$$

</div>
<div>

This is the **cable equation**, a diffusion equation with leak, like the equation for heat spreading along a rod that loses heat through its surface. $\lambda$, the **length constant**, sets how far voltage spreads; $\tau$ is the membrane time constant of Week 29.

</div>
</Derivation>

<Step n={2} title="The length constant and steady state" minutes={25} />

For a steady current injected at one end of a very long cable, $\partial V/\partial t = 0$ and the equation becomes $\lambda^2V'' = V$, whose solution decaying away from the injection site is $V(x) = V_0e^{-x/\lambda}$: the voltage falls by a factor $e$ every length constant. A finite cable with a sealed end (no current escapes from its tip) attenuates less, because current that would have flowed further has nowhere to go: $V(x) = V_0\cosh((L - x)/\lambda)/\cosh(L/\lambda)$.

A 2 μm dendrite has a length constant of 816 μm, a 0.5 μm one 408 μm and an 8 μm one 1.6 mm: quadrupling the diameter only doubles λ. The compartmental solution reproduces the sealed-end formula to three decimal places, 0.374, slightly above the infinite cable's $e^{-1}$ = 0.368. Steady voltages spread surprisingly far: many dendrites are shorter than a length constant, so at steady state a voltage at the soma is felt through much of the tree.

<Step n={3} title="Transients: smaller, later, broader" minutes={30} />

Synaptic currents are brief, and for brief signals the steady-state length constant overstates how far they reach. Charge injected for half a millisecond spreads along the cable while also leaking out, so the response further away is not just smaller but delayed and broadened, as the heat from a brief spark spreads and blurs. Rall showed in the 1960s (Week 21's paper) that the shape of a synaptic potential at the soma therefore tells you how far away the synapse was.

At the injection site the response peaks at 33 mV after 0.5 ms and lasts about 0.7 ms. Half a length constant away it peaks at only 4.1 mV, after 2.4 ms, with a half-width of 9.4 ms; one length constant away, 1.6 mV after 6.5 ms, lasting 20 ms. That answers the prediction: a few millivolts, much broader. For brief signals the steady-state rule of 37% per length constant badly underestimates the loss: here less than 5% of the peak survives one length constant. What survives is the charge, spread out in time, which is why distant synapses can still add up at the soma when many are active together.

<Step n={4} title="A dendrite as a filter" minutes={20} />

Why do brief signals attenuate more than steady ones? Try a sinusoidal current of frequency $f$. Substituting $V \propto e^{i\omega t}$ into the cable equation gives $\lambda^2V'' = (1 + i\omega\tau)V$, so the voltage decays with a complex length constant $\lambda/\sqrt{1 + i\omega\tau}$. Its magnitude shrinks as frequency rises: at high frequencies the membrane capacitance shunts the current out before it can travel. Along an infinite cable, the amplitude after a distance $x$ is $|e^{-(x/\lambda)\sqrt{1 + i\omega\tau}}|$.

A steady signal keeps 37% of its amplitude over one length constant; at 10 Hz, 32%; at 100 Hz, 7%; at 1,000 Hz, almost nothing. Dendrites are low-pass filters, with a cut-off set by the membrane time constant. This is why the synaptic potential of Step 3 broadened: its fast components were filtered out on the way, and what arrived was the slow remainder.
