<Step n={1} title="Input resistance of a cable" minutes={25} />

Inject a steady current $I$ into the end of a semi-infinite cable. The voltage there is $V_0 = IR_\infty$, where $R_\infty$, the cable's input resistance, equals the axial resistance of one length constant of core: $R_\infty = \frac{4r_i}{\pi d^2}\lambda$. Substituting $\lambda = \sqrt{r_md/(4r_i)}$,

$$
R_\infty = \frac{2}{\pi}\sqrt{r_mr_i}\;d^{-3/2}.
$$

The power $-3/2$ is the key to today. Thicker cables have lower axial resistance, which lowers $R_\infty$ as $d^{-2}$, but also longer length constants, which raises it as $d^{1/2}$. A finite cable of length $L$ with a sealed end has a higher input resistance, because current cannot leak out beyond its tip: $R_{in} = R_\infty\coth(L/\lambda)$.

<Step n={2} title="The soma and its dendrites" minutes={15} />

A neuron's input resistance, measured at the soma, combines the soma's own membrane with the dendrites hanging off it, all in parallel. Rall expressed the balance as the **dendritic-to-somatic conductance ratio**, $\rho = G_{dendrites}/G_{soma}$. For motoneurons he estimated $\rho$ between about 5 and 25: most of the current injected at the soma flows out through the dendrites. This was the original point of today's paper. Earlier estimates of the membrane's specific resistance had treated the motoneuron as a sphere, and so had underestimated it many times over; taking the dendrites into account, Rall showed that the membrane is far less leaky than had been thought, and therefore that distant synapses are not electrically remote.

<Step n={3} title="Branch points and the three-halves rule" minutes={40} />

At a branch point, the parent cable sees its daughters as a load. If the daughters' combined input conductance equals that of the parent's own continuation, the parent cannot tell the difference: the voltage and current pass through the branch point as if it were not there. For daughters long enough to look semi-infinite, input conductance scales as $d^{3/2}$, so the condition is

$$
d_{parent}^{3/2} = \sum_j d_j^{3/2}.
$$

This is **Rall's three-halves power rule**. If, in addition, all terminal branches end at the same electrotonic distance from the soma (with sealed ends), the whole tree can be collapsed into a single **equivalent cylinder** with the parent's diameter and the tree's total electrotonic length. That answers the prediction: two equal daughters of a 4 μm parent must each be $4/2^{2/3} \approx$ 2.52 μm.

<Step n={4} title="How real trees compare" minutes={15} />

Do real neurons obey the rule? Near the soma, many come close: Rall's own analysis of cat motoneurons, and later anatomical reconstructions, found branch points with the sum of $d^{3/2}$ roughly conserved. Further out, and in many cell types, the rule fails, and terminal branches end at very different electrotonic distances. The equivalent cylinder is therefore an idealisation, valuable because it turns a tree into something you can solve by hand and reason about, and because the departures from it tell you where a tree's geometry does something special. For real trees, the practical tool is the compartmental model of tomorrow, which handles any geometry.
