<Step n={1} title="Compartments" minutes={15} />

A compartmental model cuts a neuron into short, isopotential pieces. Compartment $j$ has capacitance $C_j$, membrane currents $I_{ion,j}$, and is joined to each neighbour $k$ by an axial conductance $g_{jk}$:

$$
C_j\frac{dV_j}{dt} = -I_{ion,j} + \sum_{k}g_{jk}(V_k - V_j) + I_{inj,j}.
$$

For an unbranched cable the coupling links each compartment only to its two neighbours, so the matrix of the linear part is **tridiagonal**. For a tree, each compartment is linked to its parent and children; numbered from the tips towards the root, as Michael Hines showed in 1984, the matrix can still be solved as quickly as a tridiagonal one. Compartments must be short compared with the length constant at the frequencies of interest: NEURON's default rule makes them at most a tenth of the length constant at 100 Hz, which for a thin dendrite means tens of micrometres or less.

<Step n={2} title="Stiffness and implicit Euler" minutes={30} />

The coupling between short compartments is strong: charge moves between neighbours much faster than it leaks out. A system with such widely separated time scales is **stiff**, and forward Euler (Week 21) is stable only if the time step is shorter than the fastest of them. For a compartment of length $\Delta x$ coupled to two neighbours, that limit is roughly $\Delta t_{max} = C_j/(g_m + 2g_a)$, which shrinks as $\Delta x^2$.

**Backward (implicit) Euler** evaluates the coupling at the end of the step instead of the start: $(C/\Delta t)(V^{n+1} - V^n) = -GV^{n+1} + I$. Each step then needs a linear solve, $(C/\Delta t + G)V^{n+1} = (C/\Delta t)V^n + I$, but it is stable for any time step: the price of stability is solving a matrix equation, which tomorrow's step makes cheap.

The explicit limit is under 1 μs. At 25 μs, explicit Euler explodes to $10^{129}$ V within 2 ms, while at half the limit it gives 13.27 mV; implicit Euler at 25 μs gives 13.25 mV, within 0.2% of the fine explicit run with fifty times fewer steps. That answers the prediction. The instability comes not from the membrane, whose 20 ms time constant is harmless, but from the fast exchange of charge between neighbouring compartments, which explicit Euler overshoots and amplifies at every step.

<Step n={3} title="Solving in linear time" minutes={25} />

A general linear solve costs time proportional to $N^3$, which for a neuron of 10,000 compartments, solved every time step, would be hopeless. A tridiagonal matrix needs only one sweep down, eliminating each lower entry using the row above, and one sweep back up: time proportional to $N$. This is the Thomas algorithm, which you wrote on Day 1; Hines's ordering extends it to trees.

Even written as a Python loop, the linear-time solver beats the dense one at this size, and the gap grows as the cube of the size; compiled, as in NEURON, it costs microseconds. The exact times depend on your machine. NEURON's default method is the backward Euler you used today; an option staggers the update of the gating variables by half a step relative to the voltage, which makes the scheme second-order accurate (Crank-Nicolson style) while each step remains a single tridiagonal solve.
