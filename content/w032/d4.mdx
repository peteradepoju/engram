<Step n={1} title="Dendrites that fight back" minutes={15} />

Three discoveries changed how dendrites are viewed.

- **Back-propagating action potentials.** Stuart and Sakmann (1994) patched the soma and the apical dendrite of the same layer 5 pyramidal neuron and showed that a spike starting near the soma travels back into the dendrite, actively, through dendritic sodium channels. It carries news of the output to every synapse, a signal that synaptic plasticity can use (Week 42).
- **Local dendritic spikes.** Thin dendrites can generate their own spikes: brief sodium spikes, longer calcium spikes in the apical tuft, and **NMDA spikes**, plateaus lasting tens of milliseconds when enough nearby glutamate synapses are active together.
- **Coincidence of inputs from different layers.** Larkum and colleagues found that a back-propagating spike arriving in the apical tuft at the same time as tuft input triggers a calcium spike and a burst of output spikes: the neuron detects the coincidence of input to its two ends. In human layer 2/3 neurons, Gidon and colleagues (2020) found dendritic calcium spikes whose amplitude is largest for intermediate inputs, enough for a single neuron to compute an XOR.

<Step n={2} title="The NMDA nonlinearity" minutes={30} />

The NMDA receptor's channel is blocked by magnesium ions from outside the cell at negative voltages; depolarisation expels the magnesium. Jahr and Stevens (1990) described the unblocked fraction as

$$
B(V) = \frac{1}{1 + \frac{[\text{Mg}^{2+}]}{3.57\,\text{mM}}e^{-0.062V}},
$$

with $V$ in millivolts. The NMDA current, $g_{NMDA}B(V)(V - 0)$, therefore grows as the voltage rises, until the driving force shrinks: a region of negative slope, like the sodium current's. A branch with many active NMDA synapses can be pushed into a regenerative plateau.

With magnesium, the first two synapses depolarise the branch by 4.9 mV, but going from 6 to 8 synapses adds 9.8 mV and from 8 to 10 another 10.4: each synapse helps more once others have partly relieved the block. Beyond about 12 synapses the response saturates as the voltage approaches the synapses' reversal potential. Without magnesium the curve bends the other way from the start: two synapses already give 27 mV, and each further one adds less. The S-shape is the signature of a regenerative response, and in real neurons it produces plateaus lasting tens of milliseconds, the NMDA spikes.

Twelve synapses clustered on one branch depolarise the soma by 13.5 mV; the same twelve spread three to a branch, by only 9.3 mV. That answers the prediction. The advantage of clustering is largest in the middle range, where a clustered branch sits on the steep part of its S-curve while dispersed branches stay in its flat foot; with very few synapses, or very many, it shrinks.

<Step n={4} title="A neuron as a two-layer network" minutes={35} />

Poirazi, Brannon and Mel (2003), today's deep read, simulated a detailed model of a hippocampal pyramidal neuron with active dendrites, presented thousands of patterns of synaptic input, and asked what simple model best predicted its firing rate. A point neuron, a weighted sum through one nonlinearity, did poorly. A **two-layer model** did well: each thin branch sums its own inputs and passes them through a sigmoid, and the soma sums the branch outputs and applies a second nonlinearity. In the language of Week 1, a single pyramidal neuron behaves like a small neural network with a hidden layer.

That matters because a hidden layer can compute things a single layer cannot. Suppose inputs $a, b$ arrive on branch 1 and $c, d$ on branch 2, and the neuron should fire when $a$ and $b$ are both active, or $c$ and $d$, but not for the mixed pairs $a, c$ or $b, d$: a feature-binding task, firing only for features that belong together.

No random weights do better than three of the four cases, and the reason is not bad luck. For a point neuron to fire for $(a, b)$ and $(c, d)$ it needs $w_a + w_b \gt \theta$ and $w_c + w_d \gt \theta$; adding them, $w_a + w_b + w_c + w_d \gt 2\theta$. To stay silent for $(a, c)$ and $(b, d)$ it needs $w_a + w_c \lt \theta$ and $w_b + w_d \lt \theta$; adding these, the same sum is less than $2\theta$. Both cannot hold. With one nonlinearity per branch, the task is trivial. Beniaguev, Segev and London (2021) pushed the comparison further: to reproduce the spike output of a detailed layer 5 pyramidal neuron with NMDA synapses at millisecond precision, they needed a temporal convolutional network five to eight layers deep.
