<Step n={1} title="A travelling spike" minutes={30} />

Put Hodgkin-Huxley channels into every compartment of a cable. In units per square centimetre of membrane, each compartment obeys

$$
c_m\frac{dV_j}{dt} = \frac{d}{4r_i\Delta x^2}\left(V_{j+1} - 2V_j + V_{j-1}\right) - I_{ion}(V_j, m_j, h_j, n_j) + I_{inj},
$$

and each has its own gates. Stimulate one end: the spike there drives axial current into the next compartments, which charges them to threshold, they fire, and the wave moves on, leaving refractory membrane behind it so that it cannot turn back. Hodgkin and Huxley solved this equation, by hand, for a uniformly propagating wave and predicted a speed of 18.8 m/s at 18.5 °C; they had measured 21.2 m/s.

<Step n={2} title="Speed and diameter" minutes={25} />

A dimensional argument predicts how speed depends on diameter. The cable equation has one length scale, $\lambda \propto \sqrt d$, and one time scale, $\tau$, fixed by the membrane. If the spike's shape in space scales with $\lambda$ and in time with the membrane's dynamics, its speed must scale as $\lambda/\tau \propto \sqrt d$.

Doubling the diameter from 119 to 238 μm raises the speed from 6.1 to 8.6 m/s, and doubling again to 12.2 m/s: the fitted exponent is 0.50. That answers the prediction: quadrupling the diameter doubles the speed. To go ten times faster, an unmyelinated axon must be a hundred times thicker, which is why the squid's escape-reflex axon is half a millimetre across.

<Step n={3} title="Myelin and saltatory conduction" minutes={30} />

Vertebrates found another way. Glial cells (oligodendrocytes in the brain, Schwann cells in nerves) wrap axons in many layers of membrane, **myelin**, which cuts the capacitance and leak of the wrapped stretches, the **internodes**, by a factor of about the number of layers. Sodium channels are concentrated in short gaps between wraps, the **nodes of Ranvier**. Current from a spike at one node flows along the insulated internode, losing little charge to the membrane, and fires the next node: the spike jumps from node to node, **saltatory conduction**.

Rushton (1951), today's paper, argued that in a myelinated fibre everything scales with diameter: the thickness of the myelin and the distance between nodes both grow in proportion to the fibre's diameter, as anatomists had measured. If every length scales with $d$, the spike's journey from node to node looks the same at every size, only longer, and the time per internode stays the same: velocity grows in proportion to $d$, not $\sqrt d$. Model it with the same code: make only every $k$th compartment a node, and give the others 50 times less capacitance and leak.

With a node every 2.5 mm, the 119 μm axon conducts at 12.7 m/s, twice its bare speed. Doubling both the diameter and the internode length gives 24.0 m/s, a ratio of 1.89, close to the factor of 2 that Rushton's argument predicts and well above the 1.41 of a bare axon. The model is crude, with squid channels in a squid-sized fibre and myelin represented only by reduced capacitance and leak, but it captures the essential point: insulation turns the square-root law into a linear one.

<Step n={4} title="Thin, thick, insulated" minutes={15} />

If myelinated speed grows as $d$ and unmyelinated as $\sqrt d$, there is a crossover: below some diameter, myelin does not pay, because the wrapping takes up space that could have been axon. Rushton estimated the crossover at about 1 μm, and indeed in mammals most axons above about 1 μm are myelinated and most below about 0.2 μm are not. A human sciatic nerve fibre of 10 μm conducts at about 60 m/s; an unmyelinated axon would need to be several millimetres thick to match it, which is why the squid, with no myelin, had to evolve giant axons for its escape reflex. Myelin also saves energy, because only the nodes, a tiny fraction of the membrane, need to be recharged by pumps after each spike. And because oligodendrocytes adjust myelin with experience, conduction delays in the brain are themselves plastic, a way of tuning timing between distant areas.
