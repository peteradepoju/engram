<Step n={1} title="Chemical transmission" minutes={20} />

A spike invading a presynaptic terminal opens voltage-gated calcium channels. Calcium triggers the fusion of vesicles, each holding a few thousand transmitter molecules, with the membrane, a step that takes a fraction of a millisecond. The transmitter diffuses across a cleft about 20 nm wide and binds receptors on the postsynaptic membrane. Two kinds of receptor respond. **Ionotropic** receptors are themselves ion channels and open within a millisecond. **Metabotropic** receptors act through G proteins and second messengers, opening or closing separate channels over tens to hundreds of milliseconds. Release is probabilistic: each vesicle site releases with probability $p$, typically 0.1 to 0.9, which Week 15 modelled as a binomial process.

| Receptor | Transmitter | Ions | Rise (ms) | Decay (ms) | Reversal (mV) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| AMPA | glutamate | Na⁺, K⁺ | about 0.2 | 1 to 5 | 0 |
| NMDA | glutamate | Na⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺ | about 2 | 50 to 150 | 0, blocked by Mg²⁺ |
| GABA-A | GABA | Cl⁻ | about 0.5 | 5 to 20 | about -70 |
| GABA-B | GABA | K⁺ (via G proteins) | 20 to 50 | 100 to 300 | about -90 |

Values vary between cell types and temperatures; these are typical for cortex at physiological temperature.

<Step n={2} title="Conductance waveforms" minutes={25} />

A convenient description of a single synaptic conductance is the difference of two exponentials, one for the rise and one for the decay:

$$
g(t) = \bar g\,N\left(e^{-t/\tau_d} - e^{-t/\tau_r}\right), \qquad t_{peak} = \frac{\tau_r\tau_d}{\tau_d - \tau_r}\ln\frac{\tau_d}{\tau_r},
$$

with $N$ chosen so that the peak equals $\bar g$. When $\tau_r = \tau_d$ it becomes the alpha function of Week 21, $t e^{-t/\tau}$. The peak time follows from setting $dg/dt = 0$.

AMPA peaks at 0.5 ms, GABA-A at 1.5 ms, NMDA at 7.6 ms and GABA-B at 67 ms. For the same peak conductance, the areas, which set the charge each lets through, differ more than a hundredfold: 2.6 ms for AMPA, 88 for NMDA, 274 for GABA-B. A modest NMDA conductance carries as much current over time as a large AMPA one, which is why NMDA receptors dominate slow integration even when their peak is small.

<Step n={3} title="A kinetic receptor model" minutes={30} />

Waveforms added together assume that each release acts independently. Receptors are molecules that can be occupied, and a receptor that is already open cannot open again. Destexhe, Mainen and Sejnowski (1994), today's paper, proposed the simplest kinetic model that respects this. A fraction $r$ of receptors is open; transmitter at concentration $T$ opens closed ones at rate $\alpha T$, and open ones close at rate $\beta$:

$$
\frac{dr}{dt} = \alpha T(1 - r) - \beta r.
$$

If $T$ is a brief square pulse (1 mM for 1 ms) at each release, the equation can be solved exactly between and during pulses, so a whole network's synapses can be updated with one exponential each per step. The model rises and decays with time constants $1/(\alpha T + \beta)$ and $1/\beta$, and, unlike summed waveforms, it cannot exceed $r = 1$.

A single 1 ms pulse opens 62% of the receptors. At 20 Hz each response has decayed before the next arrives, and the kinetic model and the linear sum agree (0.62). At 500 Hz the linear sum climbs to 1.92, impossible for a fraction of receptors, while the kinetic model saturates at 0.80. Real synapses saturate for this reason, and also because a single vesicle can nearly fill the receptors under it, so at high rates summed waveforms overestimate the conductance.

<Step n={4} title="Summation in time" minutes={30} />

What the postsynaptic neuron feels depends on both the synapse and its own membrane. A brief conductance injects a brief pulse of charge, which the membrane then holds for its own time constant; a long conductance keeps injecting. So inputs arriving 50 ms apart summate weakly through AMPA receptors, whose effect has mostly decayed, and strongly through NMDA receptors, which are still open when the next input arrives.
