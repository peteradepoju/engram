<Step n={1} title="Running out of vesicles" minutes={20} />

Tsodyks and Markram (1997), today's paper, recorded from pairs of connected layer 5 pyramidal neurons in rat cortex and drove the presynaptic cell with trains of spikes. The postsynaptic responses fell during the train and recovered over about a second. Their model has one variable, the fraction $x$ of synaptic **resources** (think of vesicles ready to release) that are available. Each spike releases a fraction $U$ of what is available, producing a response proportional to $Ux$; between spikes the resources recover with time constant $\tau_{rec}$:

$$
\frac{dx}{dt} = \frac{1 - x}{\tau_{rec}}, \qquad \text{at each spike: } A = A_{max}Ux, \quad x \to x - Ux.
$$

$U$ plays the role of release probability: a synapse with high $U$ gives a large first response and depresses strongly.

<Step n={2} title="Steady state and the limiting frequency" minutes={30} />

For a regular train with interval $\Delta = 1/f$, the available resources settle where what is used at each spike equals what recovers in between. Just before each spike, $x$ satisfies $x_{ss} = 1 - (1 - (1 - U)x_{ss})e^{-\Delta/\tau}$, so

$$
x_{ss} = \frac{1 - e^{-\Delta/\tau_{rec}}}{1 - (1 - U)e^{-\Delta/\tau_{rec}}}.
$$

At high rates, $e^{-\Delta/\tau} \approx 1 - \Delta/\tau$, and $x_{ss} \approx \frac{1}{U f\tau_{rec}}$: the response to each spike falls as $1/f$. The total transmitted per second, $f \cdot A_{max}Ux_{ss}$, then approaches $A_{max}/\tau_{rec}$, independent of the rate. Tsodyks and Markram called the rate where this happens, about $1/(U\tau_{rec})$, the **limiting frequency**.

<Step n={3} title="Depression as gain control" minutes={25} />

If the steady transmission is independent of rate, what does a depressing synapse tell its target? Abbott, Varela, Sen and Nelson (1997) answered: changes. Immediately after a step in rate, the resources have not yet adjusted, so the transmitted current jumps by the ratio of the new rate to the old. A step from 10 to 20 Hz and a step from 20 to 40 Hz produce the same relative jump, before both settle back. The synapse reports relative changes in its input rate, like Weber's law in perception.

<Step n={4} title="Fitting a paired-pulse experiment" minutes={30} />

How are $U$ and $\tau_{rec}$ measured? Give two spikes separated by an interval $\Delta$ and measure the ratio of the second response to the first. After the first spike, $x = 1 - U$, and it recovers as $x(\Delta) = 1 - Ue^{-\Delta/\tau_{rec}}$, so the **paired-pulse ratio** is $A_2/A_1 = 1 - Ue^{-\Delta/\tau_{rec}}$. A straight-line fit of $\ln(1 - A_2/A_1)$ against $\Delta$ gives both parameters: the intercept is $\ln U$ and the slope $-1/\tau_{rec}$.
