<Step n={1} title="Facilitation" minutes={20} />

Facilitation is thought to come mainly from calcium that lingers in the terminal after a spike and adds to the calcium of the next one. Markram, Wang and Tsodyks (1998), today's paper, captured it by letting the release fraction itself change. A variable $u$ starts at $U$; each spike raises it, $u \to u + U(1 - u)$, and between spikes it relaxes back to $U$ with time constant $\tau_F$. The response to a spike is $A_{max}ux$, where $x$ is yesterday's resource variable, which is depleted by $ux$ at each spike and recovers with $\tau_{rec}$. With $\tau_F = 0$ this is yesterday's depressing synapse; with a small $U$ and a long $\tau_F$, facilitation dominates at first, until depletion catches up.

The depressing synapse's responses fall from 0.5 to 0.058 over ten spikes at 20 Hz. The facilitating one's rise from 0.05 to 0.23, more than fourfold, because each spike raises u and the 50 ms gaps are too short for it to relax back, while x recovers quickly (τ_rec = 100 ms) and is barely used.

<Step n={2} title="Two synapses, two filters" minutes={30} />

Drive each synapse with long regular trains at different rates and record its steady response per spike, its **efficacy** at that rate. A depressing synapse gives less per spike the faster it is driven: a low-pass filter on rate. A facilitating synapse is different: at low rates, $u$ decays back between spikes and nothing builds up; at high rates, $x$ is depleted. In between, $u$ has accumulated and $x$ has not yet run out, so efficacy peaks at a preferred rate. Treating the two effects as balancing gives a rough estimate of that rate, $f^* \approx 1/\sqrt{U\tau_{rec}\tau_F}$.

<Step n={3} title="Fitting the full model" minutes={35} />

Regular trains are convenient but not natural. Real spike trains are irregular, and the model's three parameters can be fitted to the responses of a synapse to any train whose timing is known. Markram and colleagues did this for each connection they recorded; the same procedure is used today to classify synapses.

The grid search recovers U = 0.10, τ_D = 200 ms, τ_F = 600 ms and A = 1.98, the true values, from 40 noisy responses. An irregular train helps: its mixture of short and long intervals probes recovery and facilitation at many time scales at once, which a regular train cannot. With fewer spikes or more noise, τ_D and τ_F trade off against each other, and a fit should be judged by its predictions on new trains, as in the Forge.

<Step n={4} title="One axon, different messages" minutes={15} />

Markram, Wang and Tsodyks's striking finding was that the dynamics belong to the connection, not to the presynaptic neuron. A single layer 5 pyramidal cell's axon made depressing synapses onto other pyramidal cells and facilitating synapses onto one class of inhibitory interneurons, and depressing ones onto another class. The same spike train therefore carries different messages to different targets: to neighbouring pyramidal cells, mainly the onset of activity and changes in rate; to the facilitating interneurons, mainly sustained high-rate firing, which recruits inhibition only when excitation persists. Later work traced these differences to the identity of the target cell: somatostatin-expressing interneurons receive facilitating input, parvalbumin-expressing ones depressing input, the cell types of Week 28.
