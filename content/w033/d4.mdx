<Step n={1} title="Memory without spikes" minutes={20} />

In delayed-response tasks, neurons in prefrontal cortex often fire persistently while an animal holds an item in mind, and attractor networks explain this as reverberation through strong recurrent connections. But persistent firing is expensive, and in some recordings the delay activity is sparse, or comes in brief bursts, or vanishes and returns. Mongillo, Barak and Tsodyks (2008), today's paper, noticed that synapses between prefrontal pyramidal neurons facilitate strongly, with $\tau_F$ around a second or more. If an item is stored by a brief burst of activity in a population, the population's recurrent synapses facilitate; when the population falls silent, the facilitation decays only slowly. The item is held in the **pattern of synaptic efficacy**, not in spiking, and a weak, non-specific input later reactivates the population whose synapses are still strong.

Two hundred milliseconds after loading, a probe finds the synapse 2.7 times as strong as baseline; at 500 ms, 3.4 times, as depression recovers while facilitation persists; at 1 s, 2.9 times; at 2 s, 2.0; at 4 s, still 1.3. <Step n={3} title="Refreshing the trace" minutes={25} />

A trace that fades in a few seconds can be kept alive. In Mongillo and colleagues' network, the loaded population, with its facilitated recurrent synapses, is close to firing; occasional brief **population spikes** reactivate it, and each reactivation tops up the facilitation before it has decayed. The memory is then held by a low-rate rhythm of reactivations rather than continuous firing. How rare can the reactivations be?

Mongillo and colleagues showed that such periodic population spikes arise naturally in their network once an item is loaded.

<Step n={4} title="Bursts as a unit of information" minutes={30} />

Many neurons fire some of their spikes in bursts, groups of two or more within a few milliseconds. Lisman (1997) argued that bursts might be a unit of information in their own right, read out by synapses that respond to them reliably while ignoring isolated spikes. A facilitating synapse with a short facilitation time does exactly this: facilitation built by the first spike of a burst boosts the next ones, but has decayed by the time an isolated spike arrives. A depressing synapse does the opposite.

The facilitating synapse transmits 1.55 times as much per spike when the same number of spikes arrives in bursts of three, because the second and third spikes find u raised by the first. The depressing synapse transmits only 0.79 times as much per spike in bursts: the first spike uses resources the others need. Two targets of the same neuron could therefore read different things from one spike train, one its bursts, the other its isolated spikes, as Day 3 suggested.
