<Step n={1} title="The modulatory systems" minutes={20} />

| Modulator | Main source | Typical receptors | Associated with |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dopamine | ventral tegmental area, substantia nigra | D1-like, D2-like (G-protein coupled) | reward prediction, motivation, movement |
| Noradrenaline | locus coeruleus | α and β adrenergic | arousal, vigilance, responses to surprise |
| Serotonin | raphe nuclei | many 5-HT types; one is ionotropic | mood, patience, behavioural state |
| Acetylcholine | basal forebrain, brainstem | muscarinic (metabotropic), nicotinic (ionotropic) | attention, learning, sleep and waking |

A few thousand neurons in the locus coeruleus, for example, project to almost the whole cortex. Their transmitter is often released from varicosities along the axon rather than at classical synapses, and diffuses to receptors some distance away (volume transmission). Acting through G proteins, these receptors close or open potassium channels, change calcium channels and so release probability, and gate long-term plasticity. The effects are slower and broader than fast transmission, and they change how a circuit computes rather than what it is told.

<Step n={2} title="Modulating release" minutes={30} />

Many modulators act presynaptically, often by reducing calcium entry and so the release fraction $U$ (adenosine and GABA-B receptors do this at many cortical synapses). The first response falls in proportion. But a synapse that releases less also depletes less, and in a train the two effects compete.

The first response falls to 40% of control, as expected. But after 20 spikes the modulated synapse responds at 0.049 against the control's 0.057, 86% of it, and the ratio of last to first response rises from 0.11 to 0.25. That answers the prediction. The modulator has turned a synapse that announced the start of a train and then fell almost silent into one that passes the train more evenly, with little change in its steady transmission. The same molecule acting on a different parameter could have done the opposite.

<Step n={3} title="Modulating excitability" minutes={30} />

Acetylcholine, acting on muscarinic receptors, closes the M-current, the slow potassium current behind much spike-frequency adaptation (Week 30's channel zoo). In a model, that is a smaller increment of an adaptation conductance at each spike. Take a leaky integrate-and-fire neuron with a spike-triggered potassium conductance, as in Week 21, and halve or more the adaptation.

Reducing the adaptation increment to 30% nearly doubles the rates, from 23 to 45 Hz at 300 pA and from 78 to 145 Hz at 600 pA, and the slope of the f-I curve rises from 18 to 33 Hz per 100 pA. Acetylcholine therefore acts like a gain knob on the neurons it reaches, one proposed mechanism for how attention, which engages cholinergic systems, boosts responses to attended stimuli.

<Step n={4} title="One wiring diagram, many circuits" minutes={20} />

Eve Marder's work on the stomatogastric ganglion of crabs and lobsters, a circuit of about 30 neurons whose every connection is known, gave the clearest demonstration of what modulators do. The same anatomical circuit produces different rhythms, with different neurons active in different phases, depending on which of more than 20 modulators are present. Each modulator changes particular conductances and synapses in particular cells, and so reconfigures the circuit into a functionally different one. Today's paper, Marder (2012), draws the lesson: a connectome tells you which neurons could talk to each other, but what they say depends on the modulatory state, and the same is true of the much larger circuits of mammals. Combined with Week 30's degeneracy, it also explains why circuits must be robust: modulators move them around parameter space all the time, and they must keep working.

<Callout kind="note">

Dopamine is also a teaching signal: its phasic responses resemble the reward prediction errors of reinforcement learning, and it gates synaptic plasticity in the striatum and cortex. That role, central to NeuroAI, is the subject of Weeks 69 to 71. Here the point is the more general one: modulators change the parameters of neurons and synapses, and learning signals are one special case.
