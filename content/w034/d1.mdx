<Step n={1} title="Point processes" minutes={20} />

A spike train is a list of times $t_1 \lt t_2 \lt \dots$. Three equivalent descriptions are useful. The **interspike interval (ISI) distribution** $p(\tau)$ gives the probability density of the gap between successive spikes. The **survivor function** $S(\tau) = \int_\tau^\infty p(s)\,ds$ is the probability that no spike has yet occurred $\tau$ after the last one. And the **hazard function** $h(\tau) = p(\tau)/S(\tau)$ is the instantaneous rate of firing, given that the neuron has been silent for $\tau$. For a Poisson process the hazard is constant, so the past does not matter; a refractory neuron has zero hazard just after a spike, rising afterwards.

If successive intervals are independent and identically distributed, the process is a **renewal process**: each spike resets the clock. The gamma family is a convenient set of renewal models, with ISI density $p(\tau) \propto \tau^{k-1}e^{-k\tau/\mu}$, mean $\mu$ and coefficient of variation $1/\sqrt k$: $k = 1$ is Poisson, $k \gt 1$ more regular, $k \lt 1$ burstier.

<Step n={2} title="Renewal processes, CV and Fano" minutes={30} />

Two numbers are often reported for a neuron's variability: the CV of its intervals and the Fano factor of its counts, variance over mean in a window of fixed length. For a renewal process they are linked: in windows much longer than the mean interval, $F \to CV^2$. Intuitively, a regular neuron fires almost the same number of spikes in every long window.

<Step n={3} title="Time rescaling" minutes={35} />

Real neurons' rates change with the stimulus, so their intervals are not identically distributed. Suppose you have a model of the rate, $\lambda(t)$. The **time-rescaling theorem** says: if the spikes really come from a Poisson process with that rate, then the rescaled intervals

$$
\tau_k = \int_{t_{k-1}}^{t_k}\lambda(t)\,dt = \Lambda(t_k) - \Lambda(t_{k-1})
$$

are independent exponential variables with mean 1, whatever $\lambda(t)$ looks like. Stretch time where the rate is high and compress it where it is low, and every inhomogeneous Poisson process becomes a unit-rate one. Brown and colleagues (2002), today's paper, turned this into a goodness-of-fit test: transform $z_k = 1 - e^{-\tau_k}$, which should be uniform on [0, 1], and compare its empirical distribution with the uniform one using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) statistic, the largest gap between the two cumulative distributions. With $n$ spikes, a gap above about $1.36/\sqrt n$ rejects the model at the 5% level.

Rescaled by the true rate, the 1,205 intervals give a KS distance of 0.025, inside the critical value of 0.039: the model passes. Rescaled by a constant rate equal to the mean, they give 0.093 and the model is rejected, because the true train has too many short intervals in its high-rate phases and too many long ones in its low-rate phases, more variability than any constant-rate Poisson process can produce. In the plot, the failing model's curve bends away from the diagonal in an S shape.

<Step n={4} title="What the test is for" minutes={15} />

The time-rescaling test is general: any model that gives a conditional rate, $\lambda(t \mid \text{history, stimulus})$, can be checked this way, including the generalised linear models of next week, whose rate depends on the neuron's own recent spikes. That makes it the standard check of whether a point-process model has captured a spike train's structure, not only its average rate. It also shows where a model fails: a KS plot that bends above the diagonal at short intervals means the model predicts too few short intervals, usually a sign of missing bursting; one that bends below means missing refractoriness.

Two cautions. A model fitted to the same data it is tested on will pass more easily than it should, so the test belongs on held-out data (Week 17). And with very many spikes, every model fails, because no model is exactly right; then the size and shape of the departure matter more than the p-value.
