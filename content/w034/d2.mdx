<Step n={1} title="Two sources of variability" minutes={20} />

A Poisson neuron with a fixed rate has a Fano factor of exactly 1. Cortical neurons often show more, and a natural explanation is that their rate is not fixed: attention, arousal and the state of the network change it from trial to trial. A neuron whose spikes are Poisson given a rate that itself varies randomly across trials is **doubly stochastic**. If the rate on a trial gives an expected count $\Lambda$, with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma_\Lambda^2$ across trials, the law of total variance (Week 16) gives

$$
\text{Var}(N) = \mathbb E[\text{Var}(N \mid \Lambda)] + \text{Var}(\mathbb E[N \mid \Lambda]) = \mu + \sigma_\Lambda^2, \qquad F = 1 + \frac{\sigma_\Lambda^2}{\mu}.
$$

The first term is the point-process variability; the second, the variability of the rate. Writing the rate's coefficient of variation as $c = \sigma_\Lambda/\mu$, $F = 1 + c^2\mu$.

Before the stimulus the neuron fires at about 10 Hz with a Fano factor of 1.5 to 1.6; after it, at about 40 Hz with a Fano factor of 1.0 to 1.1. The rate quadrupled while the variability relative to the mean fell, the signature Churchland and colleagues found. The drop happens even though, by the formula above, a higher mean would raise the Fano factor if the rate variability stayed the same: the fall in rate variability, from 50% to 15%, wins.

<Step n={3} title="Separating the two sources" minutes={25} />

The law of total variance suggests a way to estimate the rate variability directly: $\sigma_\Lambda^2 = \text{Var}(N) - \mathbb E[N]$, the excess of the count variance over what Poisson spiking alone would give. Churchland and colleagues (2011) called this the variance of the underlying rate and showed that it is what the stimulus quenches.

Partitioning works best when rate variability is a large part of the total, a limitation Goris, Movshon and Simoncelli (2014) addressed by fitting a model across many stimuli.

<Step n={4} title="Comparing like with like" minutes={30} />

There is a trap. By the formula $F = 1 + c^2\mu$, a doubly stochastic neuron's Fano factor rises with its mean count even if its rate variability $c$ does not change. A stimulus that raises rates would then raise Fano factors, and one that lowers rates would lower them, with no change in the underlying variability. Churchland and colleagues guarded against this with **mean matching**: they compared Fano factors only across sets of neurons whose distributions of mean counts had been made identical, by discarding neurons, at each time point.

With the rate's variability fixed at 30%, the Fano factor rises from 1.05 at a mean of 1 spike to 1.35, 1.77 and 2.43 at means of 4, 8 and 16, following 1 + c²μ. That answers the prediction. A Fano factor that changes along with the rate tells you little by itself; only at matched means, or with the rate-variance estimate, can a change in variability be separated from a change in rate.

<Key title="Before you go">
<ul>
<li>Cortical variability combines Poisson-like spiking with trial-to-trial changes in rate.</li>
<li>The law of total variance gives F = 1 + c²μ and lets the rate variance be estimated.</li>
<li>Stimulus onset quenches variability widely across cortex.</li>
<li>Because F depends on the mean, comparisons need matched means.</li>
