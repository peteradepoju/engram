<Step n={1} title="Orientation tuning" minutes={20} />

Hubel and Wiesel recorded from single neurons in cat visual cortex while moving slits and bars of light across a screen. Most cells responded only when a bar had a particular orientation, and often only in a particular part of the visual field; some, the simple cells, had receptive fields with separate on and off regions, others, the complex cells, did not. Today the same measurement is usually made with drifting gratings at 8 or 12 orientations, repeated a few to a few dozen times.

Orientation has a period of 180°: a bar at 10° is the same as one at 190°. A convenient tuning curve with this symmetry is a **von Mises** function of twice the angle,

$$
f(\theta) = b + A\,e^{\kappa(\cos 2(\theta - \theta_p) - 1)},
$$

with baseline $b$, amplitude $A$, preferred orientation $\theta_p$ and concentration $\kappa$, which sets the width (larger $\kappa$, sharper tuning).

<Step n={2} title="Fitting a tuning curve" minutes={30} />

For fixed $\theta_p$ and $\kappa$, the model is linear in $b$ and $A$, so those two can be solved by least squares in closed form and only a two-dimensional grid is needed for the rest. With NumPy, the whole grid can be evaluated at once.

The fit gives a baseline of 2.3, an amplitude of 19.1, a preferred orientation of 70° and κ = 2.25, close to the values used to simulate the data (2, 18, 70° and 2). The whole grid of 4,320 combinations is evaluated in one set of array operations, which is what makes the bootstrap that follows affordable.

<Step n={3} title="How sure is the preferred orientation?" minutes={25} />

A fitted preferred orientation is a point estimate. The bootstrap (Week 18) estimates its uncertainty without any formula: resample trials with replacement within each orientation, refit, and repeat; the spread of the refitted values is the sampling distribution. Because orientation is circular, the percentile interval must be read with care when it straddles 0° and 180°; here the preference is well away from the wrap.

With 10 trials per orientation, the 95% interval for the preferred orientation runs from 68° to 72°; with 3 trials, from 66° to 71°. This neuron is strongly tuned, so its preference is well determined even from few trials. With weaker tuning or lower rates the interval widens quickly, and for a broadly tuned neuron it can cover much of the circle: a reason never to report preferred orientations without intervals.

<Step n={4} title="Selectivity and its bias" minutes={30} />

To compare neurons, tuning is often summarised by an **orientation selectivity index**. A robust version uses all orientations: treat each mean response as a vector at twice its angle and measure how far their resultant is from the origin,

$$
\text{OSI} = \frac{\left|\sum_j R_j e^{2i\theta_j}\right|}{\sum_j R_j},
$$

which is 0 if responses are equal at all orientations and 1 if only one orientation drives the cell (it is one minus the circular variance). But noise makes mean responses unequal even in an untuned cell, and an index built from magnitudes can only be pushed upwards. The fix is a null distribution: shuffle the orientation labels across trials, recompute, and ask whether the real index is unusually large.

solution={String.raw`
  def osi(c):
      m = c.mean(1)
      return abs((m * np.exp(2j * np.deg2rad(ori))).sum()) / m.sum()

  bias = {n: np.mean([osi(r.poisson(2.0, (8, n))) for _ in range(300)]) for n in (5, 50)}
  print(f"untuned neuron, mean OSI: {bias[5]:.3f} with 5 trials, {bias[50]:.3f} with 50 trials;  tuned neuron OSI {osi(counts):.2f}")

  def shuffle_p(c, n_shuf=500):
      real, flat = osi(c), c.ravel()
      null = [osi(r.permutation(flat).reshape(c.shape)) for _ in range(n_shuf)]
      return np.mean(np.array(null) >= real)

  untuned = r.poisson(2.0, (8, 5))
  p_tuned, p_untuned = shuffle_p(counts), shuffle_p(untuned)
  print(f"shuffle p-values: tuned neuron {p_tuned:.3f} (OSI {osi(counts):.2f}), untuned neuron {p_untuned:.3f} (OSI {osi(untuned):.2f})")

An untuned neuron's index averages about 0.1 with 5 trials per orientation and about 0.03 with 50: noise alone produces apparent selectivity, which shrinks only slowly with more data. That answers the prediction. The shuffle test separates the cases cleanly: the tuned neuron's index of 0.52 exceeds all 500 shuffles, while the untuned neuron's 0.11 is typical of its shuffles (p = 0.46). A fixed threshold, such as calling every neuron with an index above 0.1 tuned, would have counted the untuned neuron as selective.

<ul>

<li>Selectivity indices are biased upwards with few trials; shuffle controls fix the inference.</li>
