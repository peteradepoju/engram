<Step n={1} title="Two kinds of correlation" minutes={20} />

For two neurons recorded together across many stimuli and trials, there are two correlations. The **signal correlation** is the correlation between their tuning curves, their mean responses across stimuli: high for neurons with similar preferences. The **noise correlation**, $r_{sc}$, is the correlation of their trial-to-trial fluctuations around those means for a fixed stimulus. Noise correlations in cortex are typically 0.05 to 0.25, larger for nearby neurons with similar tuning, and they change with attention, learning and arousal.

To compute $r_{sc}$ for one stimulus, take each neuron's counts across trials, subtract the mean, and correlate. Pooling across stimuli requires converting counts to z-scores within each stimulus first, so that differences in mean between stimuli do not masquerade as correlation.

<Step n={2} title="Measuring r_sc" minutes={30} />

Correlations often come from shared input that fluctuates on some time scale, tens to hundreds of milliseconds. Counted in windows much shorter than that, each neuron's own Poisson-like spiking dominates the count and the shared part is swamped; in longer windows the shared fluctuations accumulate. So the measured $r_{sc}$ depends on the counting window, one of the main points of Cohen and Kohn (2011), today's paper.

The same two neurons have a count correlation of 0.03 in 10 ms windows, 0.10 in 50 ms, 0.18 in 200 ms and 0.18 in 1 s. Their shared input never changed; only the window did. <Step n={3} title="Pooling correlated neurons" minutes={25} />

Why do such small correlations matter? Average the responses of $N$ neurons, each with noise variance $\sigma^2$ and pairwise correlation $\rho$. The variance of the average is

$$
\text{Var}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_i n_i\right) = \frac{\sigma^2}{N}\left(1 + (N - 1)\rho\right) \xrightarrow{N \to \infty} \rho\sigma^2.
$$

With $\rho = 0$ the noise falls without limit; with any positive $\rho$ it levels off at $\rho\sigma^2$, reached once $N$ is a few times $1/\rho$. Zohary, Shadlen and Newsome (1994) used this argument to explain why monkeys discriminating motion do not perform far better than single neurons: pooling hundreds of correlated neurons helps little more than pooling a few dozen.

<Step n={4} title="Help or harm?" minutes={30} />

Averaging is not the only way to read a population, and with an optimal linear decoder correlations can help as well as hurt. Take two neurons and a small change in the stimulus that changes their mean responses by $f' = (f_1', f_2')$. The best linear discrimination is measured by the linear Fisher information, $I = f'^\top\Sigma^{-1}f'$, where $\Sigma$ is the noise covariance (Week 17). If both neurons increase with the stimulus, positive noise correlation makes their shared noise look like signal and reduces $I$. If one increases and the other decreases, the decoder takes their difference, which cancels shared noise, and positive correlation increases $I$. What matters is the alignment of the noise with the direction in which the signal moves, the idea behind the information-limiting correlations of Week 17.

With no correlation both pairs carry 2 units of information. A correlation of 0.3 cuts the same-sign pair to 1.54 and raises the opposite-sign pair to 2.86; a negative correlation does the reverse. Correlations are neither good nor bad in themselves: what matters is whether the shared noise looks like the signal. Noise that moves the population in the same direction as a change in the stimulus, the information-limiting correlations of Week 17, is the kind no decoder can remove.
