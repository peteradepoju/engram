<Step n={1} title="What counts as a code" minutes={20} />

A **rate code** carries information in how many spikes a neuron fires in a window, averaged over time, trials or a population of similar neurons (Gerstner's three definitions of rate). A **temporal code** carries information in when spikes occur: the latency of the first spike after a stimulus, the phase of spikes relative to an ongoing rhythm (as in hippocampal place cells, Week 11), or precise patterns across neurons. The distinction is not absolute, since a rate measured in a 5 ms window is also a statement about timing, but it matters in practice: a rate code needs time to count, a temporal code needs a reference against which to time.

Gollisch and Meister (2008), today's paper, recorded from many ganglion cells at once in the salamander retina while flashing gratings at different spatial phases. Spike counts said rather little about the phase within the first 100 ms; the latency of the first spike said a great deal, and the differences in latency between pairs of cells said even more.

From the spike count, the stimulus is decoded on 33% of test trials, barely above chance at 25%; from the first-spike latency, on 75%. The count changes by less than one spike across the four stimuli, against Poisson noise of about 1.5 spikes; the latency changes by 6 ms per step, against 3 ms of jitter. In a 50 ms window, timing is the better code here, as Gollisch and Meister found for many retinal cells.

<Step n={3} title="Relative latency" minutes={30} />

There is a catch. A latency is measured from the moment the stimulus appeared, and the brain has no clock that marks that moment: an eye movement lands at a slightly different time on each fixation, a sound arrives unannounced. Add an unknown onset jitter common to all cells on a trial, and absolute latencies become unreliable. But if two cells respond with opposite dependence on the stimulus, one earlier and one later as the stimulus grows, the difference between their latencies is unaffected by the shared jitter. Gollisch and Meister found exactly such pairs, and the relative latency was the most informative quantity they measured.

<Step n={4} title="Information present, information used" minutes={15} />

Showing that information is present in spike timing is not showing that the brain uses it. A downstream neuron with a 20 ms time constant cannot distinguish latencies a millisecond apart; a decoder that needs to compare two cells' first spikes needs circuitry that does so, such as coincidence detection with suitable delays. Evidence for use comes from other kinds of experiment: reaction times too short for rate counting, as in Thorpe's work on ultra-rapid visual categorisation; behaviour that tracks timing manipulations; or circuits, such as the auditory brainstem's coincidence detectors for sound localisation, whose anatomy implements a timing code. The question for any proposed code is the one Week 36 takes up: what does a decoder have to do to read it?
