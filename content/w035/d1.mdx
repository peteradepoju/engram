<Step n={1} title="Correlated stimuli" minutes={20} />

For a linear-nonlinear neuron driven by a Gaussian stimulus with covariance $\Sigma$, the spike-triggered average is not the filter $k$ but

$$
\text{STA} \propto \Sigma k.
$$

With white noise, $\Sigma = I$ and the STA points along $k$ (Week 10). With correlated noise the STA is the filter blurred by the stimulus's own correlations. The remedy follows from the formula: multiply by $\Sigma^{-1}$. In practice, with stimulus matrix $X$ (one row per time bin) and responses $y$,

$$
\hat k = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y,
$$

which is exactly the least-squares regression of Week 5: the **whitened STA**. It removes the bias, but $X^\top X$ may have very small eigenvalues, and dividing by them amplifies noise.

With white noise the STA and the whitened STA are equally good, both about 0.996 similar to the true filter in shape. With ρ = 0.9 the STA drops to about 0.76: it is the filter smeared by the stimulus correlations, longer and smoother than the real thing, which answers the prediction. Whitening brings the similarity back to about 0.91 from the same spikes, though not to the white-noise value, for a reason the next step explains.

<Step n={3} title="What the stimulus can reveal" minutes={20} />

Why does whitening need more data? The eigenvectors of $\Sigma$ are directions in filter space, and the eigenvalues say how much the stimulus varies along each. A correlated stimulus varies a lot along smooth, slowly changing directions and very little along rapidly alternating ones. A filter component along a low-variance direction barely affects the response, so the data say little about it, and its estimate is noisy, by a factor of one over the square root of the eigenvalue.

For white noise the eigenvalues all sit near 1 (from about 0.93 to 1.03, sampling noise only), so every direction is probed equally. For ρ = 0.9 they run from about 0.05 to 11.5, a ratio of about 220. Whitening divides by these eigenvalues, so the noise along the weakest directions is amplified by a factor of about 15 in standard deviation compared with the strongest. With natural stimuli, whose spectra fall steeply with frequency, the problem is worse, and the high-frequency parts of a receptive field are the hardest to estimate.

<Step n={4} title="Regularisation by cross-validation" minutes={35} />

With a few thousand bins of a correlated stimulus, the whitened estimate is noisy along the poorly sampled directions. **Ridge regression** (Week 5) adds a penalty: $\hat k_\lambda = (X^\top X + \lambda I)^{-1}X^\top y$. As $\lambda$ grows, the estimate moves from the whitened STA towards a scaled STA, trading variance for bias. The right $\lambda$ is the one that predicts held-out data best. David, Vinje and Gallant fitted V1 receptive fields from natural movies in exactly this way, and found that the fitted filters differed from those measured with white noise: the stimulus statistics changed the receptive field itself.

  X = design(stimulus(0.9, 2000 + D - 1))
  lin = X @ k

The validation error falls from about 0.289 with no penalty to a minimum of about 0.241 at λ = 10,000, then rises again at 100,000, where the estimate is pulled too far towards the smooth STA. The chosen ridge estimate is about 0.84 similar to the true filter, against 0.69 for the unregularised whitened estimate and 0.73 for the plain STA from the same 1,500 bins. Neither extreme wins: with little data, the best receptive field estimate is a compromise chosen by held-out prediction, and the compromise depends on how much data you have.
