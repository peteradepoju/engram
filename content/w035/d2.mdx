<Step n={1} title="When the STA fails" minutes={20} />

The **energy model** of a complex cell, proposed by Adelson and Bergen in 1985, takes two linear filters with the same orientation and frequency but phases 90° apart (a quadrature pair), squares their outputs and adds them:

$$
r(t) = \alpha\left[(k_1 \cdot x_t)^2 + (k_2 \cdot x_t)^2\right].
$$

The response is the local "energy" of the stimulus at the filters' frequency, independent of phase. Because $x$ and $-x$ give the same response, the spike-triggered stimuli are distributed symmetrically about zero, and their average carries no information. What changes is their spread: along $k_1$ and $k_2$, spike-triggered stimuli are larger in magnitude than the stimulus in general, so their variance increases in those directions.

<Step n={2} title="Spike-triggered covariance" minutes={35} />

The **spike-triggered covariance** (STC) is the covariance of the stimuli that preceded spikes. Subtract the covariance of all stimuli and take eigenvectors: directions where spikes are associated with more variance than usual have positive eigenvalues (excitatory axes), directions with less have negative eigenvalues (suppressive axes), and the rest are near zero.

The STA has length about 0.12, close to zero, which answers the prediction: flat noise. The covariance tells the real story. Two eigenvalues of the spike-triggered covariance, about 0.97 and 1.08, stand far above the rest, which lie between about -0.2 and 0.2, and the top two eigenvectors lie almost entirely (overlap 0.98) in the plane of the two true filters. STC recovers the subspace, not necessarily the individual filters: any rotation of the two filters within the plane gives the same energy model, so the eigenvectors can be any orthogonal pair in it.

<Step n={3} title="Which eigenvalues are real?" minutes={25} />

With finite data every eigenvalue scatters around zero, so "well above the rest" needs a null. Shift the spike train in time relative to the stimulus by a random amount (circular shift), which keeps the number and timing statistics of spikes but breaks their relation to the stimulus, recompute the STC, and record the largest and smallest eigenvalues. Repeating this gives a null band; eigenvalues outside it are significant. Schwartz and colleagues recommend a nested version, projecting out each significant axis and testing again, but the simple band is a good start.

Across 20 shifts, the null eigenvalues stay between about -0.21 and 0.25. The two real eigenvalues near 1 are far above this band, and no eigenvalue is significantly below it, so the analysis finds exactly two excitatory axes and no suppressive ones, as it should for this model. A neuron with a suppressive surround or divisive normalisation would show one or more eigenvalues below the band: stimulus directions along which spikes are less variable than chance.

<Step n={4} title="The nonlinearity in the subspace" minutes={15} />

Once the relevant subspace is found, the nonlinearity can be estimated within it, as Week 10 did for one filter: bin the projections of all stimuli onto the recovered axes, and divide the spike count in each bin by the number of stimuli. For the energy model the result is a bowl, rising with the distance from the origin in every direction, which you can see in the figure above as a ring of spike-triggered stimuli. With two axes this is a two-dimensional histogram; with more, the data run out quickly, which is why the GLMs of tomorrow, and the deep encoding models of Day 5, impose more structure on the nonlinearity.

STC has limits. It needs Gaussian stimuli, since with natural stimuli the stimulus's own higher-order structure contaminates the covariance, and it finds the subspace, not the individual filters within it: any rotation of $k_1$ and $k_2$ in their plane fits equally well. Rust, Schwartz, Movshon and Simoncelli (2005) used it to find up to a dozen excitatory and suppressive axes in V1 neurons, far more than the textbook simple and complex cells.
