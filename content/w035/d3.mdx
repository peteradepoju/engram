<Step n={1} title="The conditional intensity" minutes={20} />

A GLM for a spike train in small time bins writes the expected count in bin $t$ as

$$
\lambda_t = \exp\left(b + k \cdot x_t + h \cdot y_{t-1:t-H}\right),
$$

where $x_t$ is the recent stimulus, $k$ the stimulus filter, $y_{t-1:t-H}$ the neuron's own spikes in the previous $H$ bins and $h$ the **spike-history filter**. Large negative values of $h$ at short lags produce refractoriness; positive values a little later produce rebound or bursting. Because $\lambda_t$ depends on the past spikes, this is no longer a Poisson process in the strict sense but a conditionally Poisson one, the conditional intensity of a point process (Week 34). The log-likelihood,

$$
\mathcal L = \sum_t \left(y_t\log\lambda_t - \lambda_t\right),
$$

is concave in $(b, k, h)$, so Newton's method finds the unique maximum. Truccolo, Eden, Fellows, Donoghue and Brown (2005), today's paper and this week's deep read, used exactly this framework to relate motor cortical spiking to movement, the neuron's own history and the activity of its neighbours.

The neuron fires about 2,270 spikes in 60 s, about 38 Hz, and only about 1% of intervals are 2 ms or less, against about 7% for a Poisson neuron at the same rate: the deep negative history filter makes a spike in the next bin or two very unlikely. A few 1 ms intervals remain, because the history filter lowers the rate rather than forbidding spikes. In this model refractoriness is relative, not absolute, which is also true of real neurons for all but the first millisecond or so.

<Step n={3} title="Basis functions and the design matrix" minutes={25} />

A history filter with one weight per millisecond has 40 parameters, most of them describing fine detail at long lags that the data cannot pin down. A common solution is to represent $h$ as a weighted sum of a few smooth bumps, **raised-cosine basis functions**, narrow at short lags and wider at long ones, spaced evenly on a logarithmic time axis. The GLM then fits the weights of the bumps, and the design matrix gets one column per basis function: the past spike train filtered by that bump.

Six basis functions now stand in for 40 history lags, so the history part of the design matrix has 6 columns rather than 40. The bumps are narrow at short lags, where the history filter changes quickly, and broad at long lags, where it changes slowly. That spacing lets a handful of weights describe both the sharp refractory dip and the gentle rebound, and the smoothness it imposes is a form of regularisation, as Day 1's ridge penalty was.

<Step n={4} title="Fitting by Newton's method" minutes={35} />

The gradient and Hessian of the Poisson log-likelihood with respect to the weights $w$ of design matrix $X$ are $X^\top(y - \lambda)$ and $-X^\top\text{diag}(\lambda)X$ (Week 17). Newton's method steps by the inverse Hessian times the gradient and converges in a few dozen iterations.

  def fit_glm(X, y, iters=25):
      X = np.c_[X, np.ones(len(y))]
      w = np.zeros(X.shape[1]); w[-1] = np.log(y.mean())
      for _ in range(iters):
          lam = np.exp(X @ w)
          grad = X.T @ (y - lam)
          hess = (X * lam[:, None]).T @ X
          w = w + np.linalg.solve(hess + 1e-6 * np.eye(len(w)), grad)
      return w

The fitted history filter has the right shape: strongly negative for the first 3 to 4 ms and then positive, peaking at about 0.66 near 9 ms against a true peak of 0.77 at 10 ms. Its depth at 1 ms is underestimated (-2.2 against -3.0) because the broad basis functions cannot follow the steepest part of the true curve. The LNP fit, with no history, must explain the same spikes with the stimulus alone, so just after a spike it predicts the neuron's ordinary stimulus-driven rate. That answers the prediction: too high, since the real neuron is nearly silent then. Day 4 measures what this costs the LNP in held-out likelihood.
