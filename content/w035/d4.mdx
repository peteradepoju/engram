<Step n={1} title="Why likelihood" minutes={15} />

For spike trains, mean squared error is a poor measure of fit: spikes are sparse, and a model that predicts zero everywhere can have a small squared error. The natural measure for a probabilistic model is its **log-likelihood on held-out data**, how probable it makes the spikes that actually occurred. To make numbers comparable across neurons and recordings, it is reported relative to a homogeneous Poisson model with the neuron's mean rate, and divided by the number of spikes and by $\ln 2$:

$$
\text{bits per spike} = \frac{\mathcal L_{model} - \mathcal L_{Poisson}}{N_{spikes}\ln 2}.
$$

It is the information each spike carries about the model's predictors, in the sense of Week 20, as far as the model can tell.

On the last 15 seconds, which neither model saw, the LNP gains about 0.20 bits per spike over a constant rate and the GLM about 0.40: the history terms double the information the model extracts. The LNP is not useless, it captures the stimulus, but it is blind to the structure the neuron imposes on its own spike train. Bits per spike can be negative on held-out data, which happens when a model overfits so badly that it does worse than a constant rate; regularisation from Day 1 is the cure.

<Step n={3} title="Time rescaling for GLMs" minutes={30} />

Week 34's time-rescaling test applies to any model that gives a conditional intensity, including one that depends on the neuron's own spikes. In discrete time, the rescaled interval between successive spikes is the sum of the predicted intensities over the bins between them, plus a share of the bin in which the later spike falls. Summing the whole spike bin biases the test when bins are coarse relative to the rate, so Haslinger, Pipa and Brown (2010) proposed drawing that share at random: if the spike bin has intensity $\lambda_s$, add $-\ln(1 - u(1 - e^{-\lambda_s}))$ with $u$ uniform on (0, 1). If the model is right, the corrected sums are unit exponentials.

The LNP's KS distance is about 0.12, well beyond the 99% critical value of 0.07: it predicts the right number of spikes but at the wrong times, with too many short intervals. The GLM's is about 0.05, inside the band. Without the discrete-time correction, even the GLM drifts towards the edge, which is a reminder that a goodness-of-fit test is itself a model with assumptions. Note that the two tests answer different questions: bits per spike ranks models, and time rescaling asks whether the best one is good enough.

<Step n={4} title="Coupling between neurons" minutes={30} />

Neurons recorded together influence each other, through direct connections or shared input. A **coupled GLM** adds, for each other neuron $j$, a coupling filter $c_j$ applied to $j$'s recent spikes. Pillow and colleagues (2008, Week 17's paper) fitted such a model to a complete population of primate retinal ganglion cells and found that coupling improved predictions of single spikes and of the population's correlations. Here, simulate two neurons in which neuron B receives brief excitation from neuron A's spikes, and ask whether fitting B with a coupling term recovers it.

Adding A's spikes raises B's held-out score from about 0.48 to 0.78 bits per spike, and the fitted coupling filter is positive over the first 5 ms with its peak near 2 to 3 ms, the shape that was put in, though smoothed by the six basis functions (the true peak of 1.5 at 3 ms comes back as about 1.2). With 100 neurons, each with 99 coupling filters, the parameter count grows quickly, so fits to large populations rely on a compact basis and on regularisation that keeps most couplings small.

<Callout kind="warning">

A fitted coupling filter is not proof of a synapse. Two neurons that share an unrecorded input will show coupling in a GLM fitted only to each other, which is the omitted-variable problem of causal inference. Coupling filters describe statistical dependence given the variables in the model; inferring connections needs either the whole circuit or interventions, a theme the course returns to when it reaches causal inference.

</Callout>
