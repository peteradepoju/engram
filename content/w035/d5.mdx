<Step n={1} title="The readout idea" minutes={20} />

An encoding model built from a deep network has two parts. The **features** are the activations of one layer of a network, computed for each stimulus and held fixed. The **readout** is a regularised linear map from those features to each neuron's response, fitted on some stimuli and tested on others. If a layer's features are a good basis for what a brain area computes, a linear readout from them predicts the area's neurons well.

Yamins, Hong, Cadieu, Solomon, Seibert and DiCarlo (2014) took networks optimised to categorise objects in images, never shown any neural data, and found that the top layer predicted single IT neurons, and an intermediate layer predicted V4, better than earlier models. Across many networks, object recognition performance and IT predictivity rose together. This is **goal-driven modelling**: train for the task the brain area seems to serve, then test against neurons.

This is the structure of a complex cell, or of an LN-LN subunit model of a retinal ganglion cell, and a small version of what convolutional networks do.

The linear model explains about 17% of the held-out variance. It is not zero, because the edge detector's rectified output has a linear component that the pixels can follow, but most of the response depends on where and how strongly edges appear, which no single weighting of pixels can express. In the terms of Day 2, the neuron's relevant subspace is large and its nonlinearity is not a simple function of one projection.

<Step n={3} title="Features from a network" minutes={35} />

Now compare three feature sets, each followed by the same ridge readout. **Random ReLU features** are a one-layer network with random weights, 300 units. A **filter bank** is 16 localised, oriented filters (Gabor-like, at four frequencies and four phases) applied at every position and rectified, a stand-in for the early layers of a trained convolutional network, whose first-layer filters look much like these. **Pooled bank** features sum each filter's rectified output over positions, the operation of a pooling layer.

The ordering is the point: pixels about 0.17, 300 random rectified features about 0.47, the full filter bank about 0.75 and the 16 pooled features about 0.77. Random features help because rectification creates nonlinear features, but they mix pixels from the whole stimulus and are not shared across positions. The bank, with no knowledge of g, gets most of the way, and pooling, by building in the neuron's invariance to position, does slightly better with a fiftieth of the features. Pooling helps here because this neuron is perfectly position-invariant; a neuron that preferred one location would favour the unpooled bank.

<Callout kind="think">

None of the 16 filters is the neuron's own filter g. The bank works because g is close to a combination of its members and because the bank has the right architecture: local, rectified, repeated across positions and pooled. This is the sense in which a trained network can predict neurons it never saw. Its features need not match any single neuron; they need to span the right space, with the right nonlinearities in the right places.

</Callout>

<Step n={4} title="The noise ceiling" minutes={25} />

No model can predict the trial-to-trial noise. If each stimulus is shown twice, the agreement between the two repeats estimates how much of the response variance is stimulus-driven: the explainable variance. A model's R² divided by this **noise ceiling** is the fraction of explainable variance it captures, the number reported in most comparisons of networks with neural data, including the benchmarks of Brain-Score (Schrimpf and colleagues, 2018).

The ceiling comes out near 0.79, against a true value of 0.8, and the pooled bank reaches about 99% of it: the readout has found almost all the stimulus-driven variance. Pixels reach about 22% of it, so the answer to the prediction is much less than half. For real IT neurons, the best networks in 2014 explained about half of the explainable variance; the gap that remains is a measure of what current models miss.

<Callout kind="warning">

High predictivity is not the same as understanding. A readout from hundreds of features can fit many neurons, so a good score shows that the network's features span the neurons' responses, not that the network computes as the brain does. Yamins and colleagues' strongest evidence was the correlation across models between task performance and predictivity, and later work has found cases where better object recognition stopped bringing better predictions of IT. Representational similarity analysis (Kriegeskorte, Mur and Bandettini, 2008) and controlled stimuli are complementary tests.

</Callout>
