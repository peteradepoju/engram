<Step n={1} title="The population vector" minutes={25} />

Georgopoulos, Schwartz and Kettner (1986) recorded neurons in monkey motor cortex during reaches and found that each fired most for one direction, its **preferred direction**, and less for others, roughly as a cosine. They proposed a simple readout: give each neuron a vote along its preferred direction, weighted by how far its rate is above baseline, and add the votes.

$$
\hat{\mathbf v} = \sum_i (r_i - b_i)\, \mathbf p_i
$$

The direction of $\hat{\mathbf v}$ is the decoded movement direction. It needs no model beyond the preferred directions, and it worked well enough to predict reaches from a few hundred neurons. But it is not optimal, and it relies on the preferred directions being spread evenly around the circle.

With evenly spread preferred directions the population vector errs by about 7° on average, against about 4° for maximum likelihood. Crowd two thirds of the neurons into one quarter of the circle and the population vector's error triples to about 21°: the crowded neurons outvote the rest and drag every estimate towards their quarter, which answers the prediction. Maximum likelihood barely notices (about 5°), because it knows where each neuron's preference lies and weighs its evidence accordingly. The population vector can be corrected for uneven preferences, by the optimal linear estimator of Salinas and Abbott (1994) for example, but only by using more of the encoding model.

<Step n={2} title="Bayes' rule for spikes" minutes={20} />

Suppose each neuron $i$ fires as a Poisson process with rate $f_i(x)$ that depends on the variable $x$, and the neurons are independent given $x$. In a window of length $\tau$, the probability of the counts $n_1, \dots, n_N$ is

$$
P(\mathbf n \mid x) = \prod_i \frac{(\tau f_i(x))^{n_i}}{n_i!}\, e^{-\tau f_i(x)}.
$$

Bayes' rule turns this around:

<Derivation>
<div>

$P(x \mid \mathbf n) \propto P(x)\, P(\mathbf n \mid x)$.

</div>
<div>

Taking logs and dropping terms that do not depend on $x$: $\log P(x \mid \mathbf n) = \log P(x) + \sum_i n_i \log f_i(x) - \tau \sum_i f_i(x) + \text{const}$.

</div>
<div>

Each spike adds the log of its neuron's tuning curve; the last sum penalises positions where many neurons should have fired but did not. With a flat prior, the peak of the posterior is the maximum-likelihood estimate used in the cell above.

</div>
</Derivation>

Unlike the population vector, this decoder uses the actual shape of every tuning curve, so clustered preferences or irregular fields do not bias it, and it gives a whole distribution, so its uncertainty is visible.

<Step n={4} title="Windows and continuity" minutes={20} />

Short windows follow the animal closely but contain few spikes; long windows have more spikes but blur a moving animal. Zhang and colleagues added a second step: the rat cannot jump across the track between windows, so the previous estimate is a prior for the next. A simple version of this idea, a Bayesian filter, spreads the previous posterior by a Gaussian of width about the distance the animal could have moved, and multiplies it into the new likelihood.

With 50 ms windows the flat-prior decoder errs by about 6.9 cm, and the continuity prior brings that down to about 4.6 cm, a third less. The benefit shrinks as windows lengthen (3.4 against 4.7 cm at 100 ms, almost none from 500 ms), because each window already has enough spikes on its own. With a flat prior, the error falls steeply from 50 ms to about 500 ms and then levels off, where blurring of the moving animal starts to cancel the gain from extra spikes. Zhang and colleagues found that the two-step method improved reconstruction from real place cells too; when you read the paper, check how the improvement depends on the time window.

<Key title="Before you go">
<ul>
<li>The population vector is simple and works for evenly spread preferred directions; otherwise it is biased.</li>
<li>Bayesian decoding with Poisson tuning curves uses every spike and every silence.</li>
<li>Window length trades too few spikes against a moving target.</li>
<li>A continuity prior carries information across windows and helps most when windows are short.</li>
</ul>
</Key>
