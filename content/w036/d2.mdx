<Step n={1} title="The Cramér-Rao bound" minutes={20} />

For a parameter $\theta$ and data $\mathbf n$ with likelihood $P(\mathbf n \mid \theta)$, the **Fisher information** is the expected curvature of the log likelihood:

$$
I_F(\theta) = -\mathbb E\left[\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\theta^2}\log P(\mathbf n \mid \theta)\right].
$$

A sharply peaked likelihood means the data pin $\theta$ down. The **Cramér-Rao bound** says that any unbiased estimator has variance at least $1/I_F(\theta)$. For independent Poisson neurons with tuning curves $f_i(\theta)$, counted in a window $\tau$, the Fisher information has a closed form:

<Derivation>
<div>

$\log P(\mathbf n \mid \theta) = \sum_i \left(n_i \log(\tau f_i) - \tau f_i\right) + \text{const}$.

</div>
<div>

Differentiating twice: $\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\theta^2} \log P = \sum_i \left(n_i \frac{f_i'' f_i - f_i'^2}{f_i^2} - \tau f_i''\right)$.

</div>
<div>

Taking the expectation with $\mathbb E[n_i] = \tau f_i$, the $f_i''$ terms cancel, leaving $I_F(\theta) = \tau \sum_i \frac{f_i'(\theta)^2}{f_i(\theta)}$.

</div>
</Derivation>

Each neuron contributes the square of its tuning slope divided by its rate (its Poisson variance). Information adds across independent neurons and grows linearly with the counting window.

The bound falls as one over the square root of the population size, from about 15° with 10 neurons to under 5° with 100, and the maximum-likelihood decoder tracks it closely: about 8% above the bound with 10 neurons, 2 to 5% above with 50 or 100. With few neurons and short windows the likelihood is often irregular and the decoder is slightly worse than the bound; as the population grows the likelihood becomes close to Gaussian and maximum likelihood becomes **efficient**, achieving the bound. That is why the Cramér-Rao bound is a useful benchmark: a good decoder gets there.

The most informative neuron prefers a direction about 52° from the stimulus, on the steep flank of its tuning curve, and the neuron preferring the stimulus itself contributes almost nothing: at the peak of a tuning curve the slope is zero, so a small change in the stimulus barely changes the rate. Orientation is a doubled angle (it repeats every 180°), so 52° on this circle corresponds to about 26° in orientation, which answers the prediction: for telling 45° from 47°, the neurons that matter prefer orientations roughly 20° to 30° away. Schoups, Vogels, Qian and Orban (2001) found a trace of this in monkeys trained on fine orientation discrimination: training steepened the tuning slopes, at the trained orientation, of V1 neurons whose preferences lay near it but not at it.

<Step n={4} title="Correlations" minutes={30} />

Real neurons are not independent given the stimulus (Week 34, Day 4). For Gaussian noise with covariance $\Sigma$ the information available to a linear readout is the **linear Fisher information**

$$
I = \mathbf f'^\top \Sigma^{-1} \mathbf f',
$$

which reduces to the formula above when $\Sigma$ is diagonal with the Poisson variances. Abbott and Dayan (1999) asked how different correlation structures change it. Compare four: independent neurons; **uniform** correlations, every pair at 0.1; **limited-range** correlations, strongest between neurons with similar preferences (0.2 at the same preference, falling with distance); and **differential** correlations, noise along $\mathbf f'$ itself, $\Sigma = \Sigma_0 + \varepsilon\, \mathbf f' \mathbf f'^\top$, the kind Moreno-Bote and colleagues (2014) showed must limit information.

With independent neurons, information grows in proportion to the population, about 1,445 at 1,000 neurons. Uniform correlations of 0.1 do not hurt: they slightly increase it, to about 1,605, because noise shared equally by all neurons can be subtracted out by comparing neurons with opposite slopes. This was Abbott and Dayan's central point: correlations need not limit a population code, and their effect depends on their structure. Limited-range correlations, by contrast, make information level off near 60 however many neurons are added. Differential correlations cap it at $1/\varepsilon = 1{,}000$: with 1,000 neurons the information is 591, and it can approach but never pass that limit, because noise along $\mathbf f'$ is indistinguishable from a real change in the stimulus.

<Callout kind="note">

The saturation with limited-range correlations here depends on their exact shape. Moreno-Bote and colleagues (2014) showed that, in a large population, only the component of the noise along $\mathbf f'$ (the differential part) limits information; other correlation structures saturate only to the extent that they contain such a component, as the smooth limited-range kernel here does. Measuring that component directly in data is hard, because it can be a tiny fraction of the total covariance and still dominate the information.

</Callout>

<Key title="Before you go">
<ul>
<li>Fisher information bounds the precision of any unbiased decoder, and the ML decoder approaches it.</li>
<li>Neurons contribute through the slope of their tuning curves, so the flanks carry fine discrimination.</li>
<li>Correlations can leave information unchanged, increase it, or cap it, depending on their structure.</li>
<li>Only noise that looks like a change in the stimulus fundamentally limits information.</li>
