<Step n={1} title="The state-space model" minutes={20} />

The Kalman filter assumes two linear Gaussian models. The **state** $\mathbf x_t$, here the hand's position and velocity in two dimensions, evolves as

$$
\mathbf x_{t+1} = A\mathbf x_t + \mathbf w_t, \qquad \mathbf w_t \sim \mathcal N(0, W),
$$

and the **observations** $\mathbf z_t$, the spike counts of $N$ neurons in bin $t$, depend linearly on the current state:

$$
\mathbf z_t = H\mathbf x_t + \mathbf c + \mathbf q_t, \qquad \mathbf q_t \sim \mathcal N(0, Q).
$$

The first equation is a prior on movement, the second a linear encoding model, Week 35's tuning curves in their simplest form. Given both, the Kalman filter computes the exact posterior over $\mathbf x_t$ given all spikes so far, a Gaussian with mean $\hat{\mathbf x}_t$ and covariance $P_t$. Spike counts are not Gaussian, but in 50 to 100 ms bins with many neurons the approximation works well, which is part of what Wu and colleagues showed.

The fitted $A$ reads like the physics that generated the movement. Position carries over almost unchanged (0.996) and gains about 0.045 times the velocity per bin, close to the bin width of 0.05 s. Velocity decays by about 10% per bin (0.902, against $1 - 2\times 0.05 = 0.9$ in the simulation) and is pulled back towards the centre in proportion to position (about -0.08, against $-1.5 \times 0.05$). Nothing told the regression about physics; the structure is in the data.

<Step n={3} title="Predict and update" minutes={40} />

Each time step has two parts. **Predict**: push the previous estimate through the dynamics, $\hat{\mathbf x}^- = A\hat{\mathbf x}$, and grow its uncertainty, $P^- = AP A^\top + W$. **Update**: compare the spikes that arrived with those the prediction expected, and move the estimate by the **Kalman gain** times the difference:

<Derivation>
<div>

$S = HP^-H^\top + Q$, the covariance of the predicted counts.

</div>
<div>

$K = P^-H^\top S^{-1}$, the gain: large when the prediction is uncertain or the neurons are reliable.

</div>
<div>

$\hat{\mathbf x} = \hat{\mathbf x}^- + K(\mathbf z - \mathbf c - H\hat{\mathbf x}^-)$ and $P = (I - KH)P^-$.

</div>
</Derivation>

This is Bayes' rule for Gaussians, the same multiplication of prior and likelihood as Day 1's continuity prior, done in closed form.

  def kalman(Zs):
      x, P, out = np.zeros(4), np.eye(4) * 10, []
      for z in Zs:
          x = A @ x
          P = A @ P @ A.T + W
          S = H @ P @ H.T + Q
          K = P @ H.T @ np.linalg.inv(S)
          x = x + K @ (z - c - H @ x)
          P = (np.eye(4) - K @ H) @ P
          out.append(x.copy())
      return np.array(out)

  Xk = kalman(Z[te])
  R2 = {"Kalman": r2(X[te], Xk)}
  print("Kalman R squared (x, y, vx, vy):", np.round(R2["Kalman"], 3))

The Kalman filter decodes velocity with $R^2$ of about 0.80 and 0.88 for the two axes, and position with about 0.92 and 0.90, better than velocity, even though the neurons are tuned mainly to velocity. The dynamics do the work: each step adds the decoded velocity times the bin width to the position estimate, so position is effectively the integral of a whole history of spikes, and the integral averages away much of the noise.

The current-bin decoder reads velocity reasonably ($R^2$ about 0.75) but position poorly (about 0.23), which answers the prediction: the weak position tuning is almost all it has to go on. The Wiener filter, with 500 ms of history, recovers much of the position (0.74) because its lags let it integrate, and the Kalman filter does best on both (0.91 and 0.84), with only 40 neurons and far fewer parameters than the Wiener filter's 400 weights per output. Wu and colleagues found the same ordering with real motor cortex data, and a further advantage: the Kalman filter gives an uncertainty with every estimate.

<Key title="Before you go">
<ul>
<li>The Kalman filter combines a movement model and a linear encoding model into an exact Gaussian posterior.</li>
<li>All four matrices are fitted by least squares when the state is known in training.</li>
<li>Predict, then correct by the gain times the surprise in the spikes.</li>
<li>Dynamics let it estimate what the neurons barely encode, such as position from velocity.</li>
</ul>
