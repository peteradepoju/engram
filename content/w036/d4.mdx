<Step n={1} title="A linear baseline" minutes={25} />

Use the same smooth reaching movements as yesterday, but with a population whose tuning a linear decoder cannot fully exploit. Thirty neurons have **axial** tuning: their rate depends on $\cos 2(\theta - \theta_i)$, so they respond the same to opposite directions, and their modulation grows with speed. Ten neurons have weak ordinary cosine tuning. Many neurons in motor and sensory areas mix variables nonlinearly like this. The baseline is a Wiener filter: ridge regression from the counts in the last three 50 ms bins to the two components of velocity.

The Wiener filter reaches $R^2$ of about 0.53 for horizontal velocity and 0.40 for vertical. It is not useless, because the ten conventionally tuned neurons and the speed dependence carry some linear information, but the thirty axial neurons, three quarters of the population, contribute little: a rise in their rate means "along my axis", in either direction, and no weighted sum of rates can turn that into a signed velocity. That answers the first half of the prediction: poorly, though not at all would be too strong.

<Step n={2} title="A network decoder from scratch" minutes={45} />

A network with one hidden layer of 64 rectified units maps the same 120 inputs to the two velocity components:

$$
\mathbf h = \text{ReLU}(W_1^\top \mathbf z + \mathbf b_1), \qquad \hat{\mathbf v} = W_2^\top \mathbf h + \mathbf b_2.
$$

With squared error loss, backpropagation (Week 12) gives the gradients in four lines. If $\mathbf d = (\hat{\mathbf v} - \mathbf v)/B$ for a mini-batch of size $B$, then $\nabla W_2 = H^\top \mathbf d$, $\nabla \mathbf b_2 = \sum \mathbf d$, the error at the hidden layer is $\boldsymbol\delta = (\mathbf d W_2^\top) \odot [\mathbf h \gt 0]$, and $\nabla W_1 = Z^\top \boldsymbol\delta$, $\nabla \mathbf b_1 = \sum \boldsymbol\delta$. Training uses Adam with a little weight decay, and holds back the last fifth of the training data as a **validation set**: every 100 steps it checks the validation error and keeps the best weights, a simple form of early stopping.

      W1 = g.normal(0, 1 / np.sqrt(D), (D, H)); b1 = np.zeros(H)
      W2 = g.normal(0, 1 / np.sqrt(H), (H, 2)); b2 = np.zeros(2)
      ps = [W1, b1, W2, b2]; m = [np.zeros_like(p) for p in ps]; v = [np.zeros_like(p) for p in ps]
      vs = V.std(0); best = (np.inf, None)
      predict = lambda x: (np.maximum(0, x @ W1 + b1) @ W2 + b2) * vs
      for k in range(1, steps + 1):
          j = g.integers(0, nfit, 128); x = Zs[j]; y = V[j] / vs

The network reaches about 0.69 and 0.63, an improvement of about 0.2 in $R^2$ over the Wiener filter from the same spikes. Its hidden units can combine an axial neuron with a weakly direction-tuned one, in effect using the weak neurons to resolve the sign and the strong axial neurons to sharpen the estimate, a conjunction no linear readout can form. The Wiener filter saturates early: from 1,000 to 8,000 training bins (50 seconds to 7 minutes of data) it improves only from about 0.38 to 0.47, because a linear model with 120 inputs is already well estimated. The network starts ahead even at 1,000 bins (0.48), thanks to early stopping, and keeps improving to about 0.66, so its lead grows from 0.1 to 0.19. Glaser and colleagues also tested their decoders with reduced amounts of training data; when you read the paper, compare their results with this curve and ask which features of the real recordings change the picture.

<Step n={4} title="Practice and pitfalls" minutes={15} />

Glaser and colleagues' comparison is careful in ways worth copying. Hyperparameters, such as the number of hidden units, the dropout rate and the number of lagged bins, were chosen by optimisation on validation data, never on the test set. The test set came from a later, contiguous block of the recording, not from bins shuffled across it, because neighbouring 50 ms bins are strongly correlated and shuffling would let a decoder be tested on near-copies of its training data. And they reported results for several datasets and amounts of data, since rankings changed between them.

<Callout kind="warning">

A better decoder is not a better explanation. A network that decodes velocity well tells you the information is present in the recorded neurons and can be extracted by some nonlinear function; it does not tell you how the brain reads it, or that the brain reads it at all. Glaser and colleagues made this point themselves: machine-learning decoders are tools for engineering (brain-computer interfaces) and for measuring information, while hypothesis-driven models remain the tool for asking how a code works.

</Callout>

<Key title="Before you go">
<ul>
<li>A linear decoder can only use what is linearly available; nonlinear mixing hides information from it.</li>
<li>A one-hidden-layer network decoder is four lines of backpropagation plus an optimiser.</li>
<li>Validation sets choose hyperparameters and stopping points; test sets are touched once.</li>
<li>Flexible decoders need data, and their success says the information is there, not how it is used.</li>
