<Step n={1} title="The loop" minutes={20} />

In an intracortical brain-computer interface (BCI), an array of electrodes in motor cortex records a few dozen to a few hundred neurons. A decoder, often a Kalman filter or a linear filter, turns their activity every 15 to 50 ms into a cursor velocity or a robotic arm command. The user watches the result and adjusts what they intend. So the decoder's input depends on its own past output: the system is a feedback loop with the user inside it.

Two consequences follow. First, a decoder with a consistent error, a rotation or a gain, can still work, because the user re-aims to compensate, just as you adapt within minutes to a computer mouse with an unusual speed. Second, the data used to fit a decoder matter: if it is fitted to activity recorded while the user watched a computer move the cursor, it may not match the activity the user produces when actually in control.

<Step n={2} title="A cursor in closed loop" minutes={30} />

Forty neurons are cosine tuned to the user's **intended** velocity. The user always intends to move straight towards the target at 8 cm/s, slowing within 3 cm of it. A linear decoder, fitted on calibration data in which the intended velocity was known, turns each 50 ms bin of spikes into a velocity, and the cursor moves by that velocity. A trial succeeds when the cursor comes within 1.5 cm of a target 10 cm away.

Every trial reaches its target, even with a 60° rotation. With no rotation the median trial takes about 1.25 s, close to the 10 cm at 8 cm/s that a perfect straight path would need plus the slowing near the target. A 30° rotation costs only about 0.2 s, and a 60° rotation about doubles the time, to 2.7 s. That answers the prediction: offline, a 30° error looks large, but in closed loop the cursor's heading error keeps changing the direction the user aims, and the path curves round onto the target. The simulated user here does not even learn; real users also adapt their neural activity over minutes to days, which helps further.

<Step n={3} title="Recalibration" minutes={25} />

If a decoder is wrong, refit it. But to what? During closed-loop use the user's true intention is unknown. Gilja and colleagues (2012), in the ReFIT method, made a simple assumption: at every moment, the user intends to move straight towards the target. They recorded neural activity during closed-loop use with the old decoder, replaced each decoded velocity with a vector of the same speed pointing at the target, and refitted the decoder to those intended velocities.

Refitting on about 3,000 bins of closed-loop data, roughly 2.5 minutes, cuts the median trial time under the 45° rotation from about 1.8 s to about 1.45 s. The refit absorbs most of the rotation, because the decoder is now trained on the activity the user actually produces while in control, labelled with what the user was trying to do. It does not quite reach the unrotated 1.25 s, since the assumed intention is cruder than the real one (constant speed, even near the target). Gilja and colleagues combined this refitting with a second change, treating the cursor position as known to the user rather than estimated, and substantially outperformed the decoders that came before.

<Step n={4} title="Handwriting and separability" minutes={30} />

Willett, Avansino, Hochberg, Henderson and Shenoy (2021) recorded from two arrays in the hand area of motor cortex of a man paralysed below the neck, while he imagined writing letters by hand. A recurrent network decoded the characters from the neural activity, and he typed at about 90 characters per minute with high accuracy, roughly twice the speed of earlier point-and-click BCI typing. Part of the explanation is in the movements themselves. Straight movements to different targets differ only in direction, so the neural patterns they evoke are similar and easily confused under noise. Letters are drawn with curved paths that change direction over time, so the patterns they evoke differ in their whole time course and are much further apart.

  t = np.linspace(0, 1, S)
  straight = [np.outer(np.ones(S), [np.cos(a), np.sin(a)]) * 6 for a in np.linspace(0, 2 * np.pi, K, endpoint=False)]

      v = sum(np.outer(np.sin((k + 1) * np.pi * t + r.uniform(0, np.pi)), c[k]) for k in range(4))
      return 6 * v / np.sqrt((v ** 2).sum(1).mean())

  curved = [curvy() for _ in range(K)]

  def trial(v):
      w = np.clip(t + r.normal(0, 0.12) * np.sin(np.pi * t), 0, 1)        # random time warping
      vv = np.array([np.interp(w, t, v[:, i]) for i in range(2)]).T
      return r.poisson(np.maximum(8 + vv @ U.T, 0) * dt)

  def classify(templates):
      means = [np.mean([trial(v) for _ in range(10)], 0) for v in templates]
      ok = 0
      for k, v in enumerate(templates):
          for _ in range(10):
              z = trial(v)
              ok += np.argmin([((z - m) ** 2).sum() for m in means]) == k
      return ok / (len(templates) * 10)

With 26 straight movements the nearest-template classifier is right only about 36% of the time; with 26 curved trajectories of the same average speed it is right about 73% of the time, from the same neurons, noise and timing jitter. The reason is in the distances: the closest pair of straight templates differ by about 42 in squared spike counts, the closest pair of curved ones by about 360. Willett and colleagues made the same argument with their participant's real neural data, showing that the patterns for handwritten letters were more widely separated than those for straight movements, and adding a recurrent network and a language model to correct the errors that remained.

<Callout kind="note">

The people who take part in BCI trials are volunteers living with paralysis from spinal cord injury, stroke or motor neuron disease, and their contributions are central to the field. Willett and colleagues' participant is known by his trial identifier, T5. Reading the papers, notice how much of the engineering is about daily use rather than peak accuracy: how often decoders must be recalibrated, how long a session can last, and whether the system works at home without an engineer present.

<Key title="Before you go">
<ul>
<li>A BCI is a feedback loop: the user corrects consistent decoder errors.</li>
<li>Closed-loop performance, not offline accuracy, is what matters for users.</li>
<li>Refitting to inferred intention, as in ReFIT, straightens paths and speeds trials.</li>
<li>Movements with rich time courses are more separable, which is why imagined handwriting is fast.</li>
</ul>
