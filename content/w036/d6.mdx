The bound falls from about 15° with 10 cells to about 1.5° with 1,000, as one over the square root of the population size. With 10 cells the decoder is about 17% worse than the bound, because in 100 ms only a handful of spikes arrive and the likelihood is often flat or has two peaks; from 30 cells on, it is within a few percent, and at 300 and 1,000 cells it sits on the bound (small differences either side are sampling noise from 1,500 trials). Maximum likelihood is efficient for large populations, as Day 2 found.

**Part B: two kinds of shared noise.** Now add shared variability of two kinds. In the **gain** condition, on each trial every cell's rate is multiplied by the same random factor (log-normal, about 40% spread), as happens with changes in arousal or attention. In the **jitter** condition, every cell responds to the same slightly wrong direction, the true one plus Gaussian noise of 3°, as would happen if noise entered upstream, in the signal all the cells receive. Both create positive noise correlations across the population. Which one limits decoding?

Shared gain fluctuations cost little: at 1,000 cells the error rises from about 1.5° to 1.6°, and it keeps falling with population size. Input jitter is different: the error falls at first but levels off at about 3.3° with 1,000 cells, more than twice the independent value and just above the 3° of the jitter itself, however many cells are added. The reason is the direction of the noise. A gain change scales every rate up or down together, which moves the population response along $\mathbf f$, a direction the decoder can tell apart from a change in head direction. Jitter moves it along $\mathbf f'$, exactly as a real change in direction would, so no decoder can separate the two: these are the differential correlations of Day 2, arising naturally from noise upstream.

<Prove title="1. The scalar Kalman update is a product of Gaussians" task="A prior $x \sim \mathcal N(\mu, p)$ is combined with a measurement $z = x + v$, $v \sim \mathcal N(0, q)$. Show that the posterior mean is $\mu + K(z - \mu)$ with $K = p/(p + q)$, and that the posterior variance is $(1 - K)p$.">

The posterior is proportional to $\exp\left(-\frac{(x - \mu)^2}{2p} - \frac{(z - x)^2}{2q}\right)$, a Gaussian in $x$.

Collecting the terms in $x^2$ and $x$, the precision is $\frac{1}{p} + \frac{1}{q} = \frac{p + q}{pq}$, so the posterior variance is $\frac{pq}{p + q} = \left(1 - \frac{p}{p + q}\right)p = (1 - K)p$.

The mean is the variance times $\left(\frac{\mu}{p} + \frac{z}{q}\right)$: $\frac{q\mu + pz}{p + q} = \mu + \frac{p}{p + q}(z - \mu)$. The gain is the fraction of the total uncertainty that belongs to the prior, which is the matrix formula of Day 3 in one dimension.

</Prove>

<Prove title="2. Differential correlations cap information" task="With $\Sigma = \Sigma_0 + \varepsilon\,\mathbf f'\mathbf f'^\top$ and $J_0 = \mathbf f'^\top\Sigma_0^{-1}\mathbf f'$, show that $\mathbf f'^\top\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf f' = \frac{J_0}{1 + \varepsilon J_0}$.">

By the Sherman-Morrison formula, $\Sigma^{-1} = \Sigma_0^{-1} - \frac{\varepsilon\,\Sigma_0^{-1}\mathbf f'\mathbf f'^\top\Sigma_0^{-1}}{1 + \varepsilon\,\mathbf f'^\top\Sigma_0^{-1}\mathbf f'}$.

Multiplying by $\mathbf f'^\top$ on the left and $\mathbf f'$ on the right: $J = J_0 - \frac{\varepsilon J_0^2}{1 + \varepsilon J_0} = \frac{J_0}{1 + \varepsilon J_0}$.

As the population grows, $J_0$ grows without bound and $J$ approaches $1/\varepsilon$. Part B's jitter is exactly this: input noise of variance $\sigma^2$ shifts every rate by $\mathbf f'\,\delta$, adding $\sigma^2\mathbf f'\mathbf f'^\top$ to the covariance, so the error can never fall below about $\sigma$, here 3°.

</Prove>
