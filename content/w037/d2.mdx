<Step n={1} title="The signal" minutes={20} />

A genetically encoded calcium indicator such as GCaMP6 fluoresces more brightly when it binds calcium. Each action potential lets calcium into the cell body, the indicator's fluorescence jumps within tens of milliseconds, and it decays over hundreds of milliseconds as calcium is pumped out. A fluorescence trace is therefore roughly the spike train convolved with a fast rise and a slow exponential decay, scaled, plus noise. Because the decay is slow, spikes close together sum into one larger transient, and single spikes in a busy neuron are hard to see.

Two further problems come from the tissue. The light collected from a neuron's region of interest includes light from the **neuropil**, the tangle of axons and dendrites around it, which carries its own fluctuating signal. And the baseline drifts with bleaching, movement and changes in focus. The standard pipeline, used by the Allen Brain Observatory and by tools such as Suite2p, deals with each in turn.

After neuropil correction, ΔF/F correlates with the true calcium at about 0.97; without it, at about 0.79. The uncorrected trace carries the slow neuropil oscillations, which look like long, shallow responses and would be read as activity of the cell. The factor 0.7 is a convention (the Allen pipeline estimates a factor for each cell instead), and the running 10th percentile assumes the cell is quiet most of the time, which fails for very active cells, a reason to check the baseline by eye on a few traces before trusting it.

<Step n={3} title="From fluorescence to events" minutes={25} />

If calcium follows $c_t = \gamma c_{t-1} + s_t$, the spikes can be recovered by inverting the recursion: $\hat s_t = c_t - \gamma c_{t-1}$. With noise this produces negative values and jitter, so practical methods add constraints. Clipping at zero is the simplest; OASIS (Friedrich, Zhou and Paninski, 2017), used in Suite2p and CaImAn, solves the non-negative problem exactly and quickly. The Allen Brain Observatory reports such **events** alongside ΔF/F. Events are not spikes: their size reflects how many spikes arrived, and isolated single spikes are often missed.

Smoothed to about 100 ms, the events correlate with the true spikes at about 0.95, against 0.50 for ΔF/F itself, whose slow decay smears each spike over a second or more. That also answers the prediction: two spikes 100 ms apart produce one transient about twice the height, because the first has barely decayed when the second arrives; deconvolution separates them only if the noise is low and the sampling fast. Real data are harder than this simulation, since the indicator's rise time, saturation and nonlinearity all vary between cells.

<Step n={4} title="Tuning and reliability" minutes={30} />

The Allen Brain Observatory showed every mouse the same stimuli, including drifting gratings in eight directions. Simulate a field of view of 40 neurons: 24 direction tuned with a range of preferred directions and response strengths, and 16 that do not respond to gratings. Each grating is shown for 2 s, followed by 1 s of grey, 15 times per direction in random order. For each cell, compute the mean ΔF/F during each grating, its preferred direction and **direction selectivity index**, and decide whether it responds at all, using a test against the grey periods.

The test finds 21 of the 24 tuned cells and none of the 16 untuned ones; the three it misses have the weakest responses, a reminder that "not responsive" means "not detectably responsive with these trials". The median direction selectivity index of responsive cells is about 0.4, moderate selectivity with some response to the opposite direction, as the simulation intended. de Vries and colleagues found that response reliability, not just tuning, varied enormously across mouse visual cortex, and that many neurons responded only to some stimulus classes, which is why they defined responsiveness separately for each.

<Callout kind="warning">

Choosing the preferred direction from the same trials that are then tested against baseline biases the test: with eight directions to pick from, the best of eight noisy means is above baseline by chance more often than one fixed direction would be. Here the threshold is strict enough that untuned cells rarely pass, but the clean fix is to choose the preferred direction on half the trials and test on the other half, the cross-validation habit from Week 17 applied to a selection step.

</Callout>

<Key title="Before you go">
<ul>
<li>Fluorescence is a slow, indirect, contaminated view of spiking.</li>
<li>Neuropil correction, a running baseline and ΔF/F come first; events come from deconvolution.</li>
<li>Tuning and responsiveness need tests, and selection steps need held-out trials.</li>
<li>Standardised surveys such as the Allen Brain Observatory make claims about whole areas possible.</li>
</ul>
