<Step n={1} title="Spike sorting" minutes={20} />

Each Neuropixels probe has nearly a thousand recording sites along a 1 cm shank, of which 384 are recorded at once (Jun and colleagues, 2017, from Week 2). A spike from one neuron appears on several neighbouring sites with a characteristic shape. **Spike sorting** finds these shapes, assigns each detected spike to a template, and calls each template a **unit**. Kilosort, the most widely used sorter, does this by template matching with drift correction. The result is a units table with spike times, templates, depths and, after alignment with histology, brain areas.

Sorting makes mistakes in both directions: two neurons with similar waveforms may be merged into one unit (contamination), and one neuron may be split into several, or lost for part of the session as the probe drifts. Quality metrics measure these failures from the data themselves.

<Step n={2} title="Refractory violations" minutes={30} />

A neuron cannot fire again within its refractory period, so intervals shorter than about 1.5 ms in a unit's spike train must involve spikes from another source. Hill, Mehta and Kleinfeld (2011) turned the count of such **ISI violations** into an estimate of contamination. If a fraction $f$ of $N$ spikes in a recording of length $T$ come from an unrelated source firing independently, the expected number of violations within a window $t_r$ is about $2 t_r f N^2 / T$. The Allen Brain Observatory reports the observed violations divided by $2 t_r N^2/T$. Counting the pairs of spikes that can fall within $t_r$ of each other (contaminant with neuron, and contaminant with contaminant) shows that this metric is

$$
m = f - \tfrac{1}{2}f^2,
$$

close to $f$ when contamination is small but reaching only 0.5 for a unit made entirely of unrelated spikes. Inverting it gives an estimate of the contamination itself: $\hat f = 1 - \sqrt{1 - 2m}$.

Even a unit with 20% contamination has only about 0.6% of its intervals under 1.5 ms, which answers the prediction: well under 1%. Violations are rare because the contaminating spikes must land within 1.5 ms of another spike, and at 10 Hz that happens about 1.5% of the time for each unrelated spike. The raw metric tracks contamination at 5% and 10% but falls behind at higher levels (0.30 for 40%), as $m = f - f^2/2$ predicts; the inverted estimate recovers about 0.24 and 0.36, within sampling noise of the truth for 30 minutes of data. With fewer spikes, a low-rate unit gives a noisy metric, so the same threshold means less for it.

<Step n={3} title="A units table" minutes={25} />

The other common metrics catch other failures. The **presence ratio** is the fraction of one-minute blocks of the session in which the unit fired at all; a unit that drifts out of range for half the session scores about 0.5. The **amplitude cutoff** estimates the fraction of spikes lost below the detection threshold from the shape of the amplitude distribution. The AllenSDK's default filters keep units with ISI violations below 0.5, presence ratio above 0.9 and amplitude cutoff below 0.1. A metric of 0.5 is what a completely random spike train produces, so that default removes only units that are almost entirely contamination; many studies use stricter thresholds. Simulate a table of 80 units with a range of contamination and drift, and filter on estimated contamination below 0.2 and presence ratio above 0.9.

The two filters keep 93% of the good units and remove all but 3% of the bad ones, 39 units of 80. The presence ratio catches the units that vanished part way through, which the contamination estimate cannot see, and the contamination estimate catches the merged units, which fire throughout. Note how much the choice of threshold matters: with the permissive default metric of 0.5, most of the heavily contaminated units here would have been kept.

<Step n={4} title="Latency and hierarchy" minutes={30} />

Siegle and colleagues showed full-field flashes, gratings and natural stimuli while recording from the dorsal lateral geniculate nucleus (LGd) and six visual cortical areas: primary visual cortex (VISp) and the higher areas VISl, VISrl, VISal, VISpm and VISam. Among the properties that varied systematically across areas was **response latency**, the time from stimulus onset to the first reliable rise in firing. The median latencies rise from about 39 ms in LGd to 48 ms in VISp, around 51 to 59 ms in the lateral and anterior areas, and 62 to 72 ms in VISpm and VISam, recovering the simulated order almost exactly. Single units vary by several milliseconds, so ten per area and medians are needed; real recordings, with hundreds of units per area, give tighter estimates but also bring differences in layer and cell type that a careful analysis must account for.

The latencies here are made up to resemble a hierarchy; read Siegle and colleagues for the real values and for how they combined latency with other measures, such as receptive field size, preference for stimulus speed and the time scale of responses, into a single hierarchy score, which they compared with the anatomical hierarchy derived from connectivity data by Harris and colleagues (2019).
