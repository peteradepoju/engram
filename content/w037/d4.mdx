<Step n={1} title="The task" minutes={20} />

In the IBL task, a mouse sees a grating on the left or right of a screen and turns a wheel to bring it to the centre, earning water for a correct choice. Contrasts range from 100% down to 0%, where there is nothing to see and the mouse must guess. Signed contrast (negative on the left, positive on the right) runs along the horizontal axis of the psychometric curve, and the vertical axis is the probability of choosing right.

A standard form for the curve has four parameters:

$$
P(\text{right} \mid c) = \gamma_L + (1 - \gamma_L - \gamma_R)\, \Phi\!\left(\frac{c - \mu}{\sigma}\right),
$$

where $\Phi$ is the cumulative Gaussian, $\mu$ the **bias** (the contrast at which the core function crosses one half), $\sigma$ the **threshold** or inverse slope, and $\gamma_L$ and $\gamma_R$ the **lapse rates**, the probabilities of choosing left or right regardless of the stimulus, from inattention, impulsivity or motor error. Wichmann and Hill (2001) showed that leaving lapses out of the model biases the other parameters, even when lapses are rare.

  import numpy as np
  import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
  from scipy.optimize import minimize
  from scipy.special import erf
  r = np.random.default_rng(2001)
  cons = np.array([-1, -0.25, -0.125, -0.0625, 0, 0.0625, 0.125, 0.25, 1])

  def p_right(c, mu, sigma, gl, gr):
      return gl + (1 - gl - gr) * 0.5 * (1 + erf((c - mu) / (np.sqrt(2) * sigma)))

  c = np.repeat(cons, 100)
  y = (r.random(len(c)) < p_right(c, 0.02, 0.12, 0.08, 0.05)).astype(float)

  def nll(p, c, y):
      mu, log_sigma, gl, gr = p
      if not (0 <= gl < 0.5 and 0 <= gr < 0.5):
          return 1e9
      q = np.clip(p_right(c, mu, np.exp(log_sigma), gl, gr), 1e-9, 1 - 1e-9)
      return -np.sum(y * np.log(q) + (1 - y) * np.log(1 - q))

  def fit(c, y, start=(0, np.log(0.2), 0.05, 0.05)):
      return minimize(nll, start, args=(c, y), method="Nelder-Mead", options={"maxiter": 3000, "xatol": 1e-5, "fatol": 1e-6}).x

  p_fit = fit(c, y); sig_fit = np.exp(p_fit[1])
  p0 = minimize(lambda q: nll([q[0], q[1], 0, 0], c, y), [0, np.log(0.2)], method="Nelder-Mead").x
  sig_nolapse = np.exp(p0[1])
  print(f"with lapses: bias {p_fit[0]:+.3f}, threshold {sig_fit:.3f}, lapses {p_fit[2]:.3f} / {p_fit[3]:.3f}")
  print(f"without lapses: threshold {sig_nolapse:.3f}")
  cc = np.linspace(-1, 1, 400)
  plt.plot(cons, [y[c == k].mean() for k in cons], "ok", label="data")
  plt.plot(cc, p_right(cc, p_fit[0], sig_fit, p_fit[2], p_fit[3]), label="with lapses")
  plt.plot(cc, p_right(cc, p0[0], sig_nolapse, 0, 0), "--", label="without lapses")
  plt.xlabel("signed contrast"); plt.ylabel("P(right)"); plt.legend(); plt.show()

With lapses in the model, the fit recovers a threshold of about 0.10 (true 0.12) and lapse rates of about 0.07 on each side (true 0.08 and 0.05). Without them, the threshold comes out at about 0.45, nearly five times too large, which answers the prediction: shallower. The model without lapses has only one way to explain wrong choices at 100% contrast, a curve that has not yet reached 0 or 1 there, so it flattens the whole curve. A mouse that is highly sensitive but sometimes inattentive would be reported as insensitive.

<Step n={3} title="How sure are we?" minutes={20} />

A threshold of 0.12 from 900 trials is an estimate. The **bootstrap** (Week 18) gives its uncertainty without further assumptions: resample trials with replacement within each contrast, refit, and repeat. The spread of the refitted thresholds approximates the sampling distribution. Wichmann and Hill's second paper on the psychometric function is devoted to doing this well.

The 95% interval runs from about 0.074 to 0.126 and contains the true value. It is asymmetric, wider above the estimate than below, because the threshold is a scale parameter and its sampling distribution is skewed; the bootstrap captures this without any formula. Most of the information about the threshold comes from the few contrasts near it (6.25% to 25%), so adding trials there would narrow the interval more than adding trials at 0% or 100%.

<Step n={4} title="Priors and blocks" minutes={30} />

Once trained, IBL mice face blocks of trials in which the stimulus appears on the right 80% of the time, alternating unpredictably with blocks where it appears on the left 80% of the time. A Bayesian observer should then lean towards the likely side when the stimulus is weak, and hardly at all when it is strong. Fit the curve separately in each block and measure the shift.

The bias moves from about +0.06 in left blocks to about -0.06 in right blocks, and at zero contrast the mouse chooses right 73% of the time in right blocks against 31% in left blocks. At 100% contrast the two curves nearly coincide: when the evidence is strong, the prior barely matters. The International Brain Laboratory found shifts of this kind in its mice, and used them as a behavioural readout of a prior that its Brain Wide Map then searched for in neural activity.

<Callout kind="think">

A shift in bias between blocks shows that the mouse uses the block, but not how it knows which block it is in. It could track the true block, as a Bayesian observer would, or simply repeat recent rewarded choices. These make different predictions just after a block switch: the Bayesian observer adapts over several trials as evidence accumulates, while a choice-history strategy also responds to unrewarded trials in ways the prior alone does not. Separating them needs trial-by-trial models, which you will build in later weeks on decision making.

<li>A psychometric curve has bias, threshold and two lapse rates.</li>
