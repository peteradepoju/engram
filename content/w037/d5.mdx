<Step n={1} title="The structure of data" minutes={20} />

Most neuroscience datasets are **nested**: trials within neurons, neurons within sessions, sessions within animals, animals within groups. Each level adds its own variability. A treatment that is applied to whole animals, such as a genetic manipulation or a drug, can only be compared between animals; the number of independent observations for that comparison is the number of animals, however many neurons each one contributes.

The Steinmetz dataset you met in Week 2 has exactly this structure: 39 sessions from 10 mice, with hundreds of neurons per session across dozens of brain areas. So does the IBL Brain Wide Map, on a larger scale. A claim about an area needs to hold across animals, not just across the neurons of one or two.

Across 300 experiments with no true difference, the t-test across 1,200 neurons gives p below 0.05 in about 58% of them, which answers the prediction: more than half. The test on mouse means stays near the nominal 5% (7% here, within sampling error of 300 experiments). <Step n={3} title="How much information is there?" minutes={25} />

The **intraclass correlation** (ICC) is the fraction of the total variance that lies between clusters, here between mice: $\rho = \sigma^2_{mouse} / (\sigma^2_{mouse} + \sigma^2_{within})$. With $m$ neurons per mouse, the variance of a mouse's mean is inflated relative to independent neurons by the **design effect** $1 + (m - 1)\rho$, and the **effective sample size** of $N$ neurons is $N / (1 + (m - 1)\rho)$. Even a small ICC matters when $m$ is large.

The intraclass correlation is only about 0.08 (estimated 0.079 against a true 0.088): mice explain less than a tenth of the variance. Yet with 120 neurons per mouse the design effect is about 10, so 600 neurons carry roughly the information of 58 independent ones about the overall mean, and for a comparison between groups of mice the information is limited further by the five mice themselves. Report the ICC when you analyse nested data; it tells readers how much the nesting matters.

<Step n={4} title="Testing at the right level" minutes={30} />

With ten animals, the cleanest test of a group difference is a **permutation test** on animal means: compute the difference between groups, then recompute it for every way of splitting the ten animals into two groups of five (there are 252), and ask what fraction is at least as extreme. It makes no assumption about distributions, and it respects the nesting by construction, because whole animals move between groups. Multilevel (mixed-effects) models do the same job with more flexibility, using every neuron while estimating the variance at each level; in Python, `statsmodels`' `MixedLM` fits them, and the Colab notebook for today shows how.

  def perm_test(means_a, means_b):
      pooled = np.r_[means_a, means_b]; n = len(means_a)
      obs = np.mean(means_a) - np.mean(means_b)
      null = [pooled[list(ix)].mean() - np.delete(pooled, list(ix)).mean() for ix in combinations(range(2 * n), n)]

With a true difference of 1.0 between groups, the permutation test gives p of about 0.03; with no difference, about 0.96. Only 252 relabellings exist, so the p-value moves in steps of 1/252, and the test is exact: under the null hypothesis that group labels do not matter, every relabelling is equally likely, and no assumption of normality or equal variance is needed.

<Callout kind="note">

With five animals per group, the smallest possible two-sided p-value of an exact permutation test is 2/252, about 0.008, whatever the data. Small numbers of animals limit what any test can show. This is not a reason to analyse neurons as if they were animals; it is a reason to plan the number of animals before the experiment, with a power analysis at the animal level.

</Callout>

<Key title="Before you go">
<ul>
<li>Neurons within an animal share variance; they are not independent for comparisons between animals.</li>
<li>The ICC and design effect say how much information nested data hold.</li>
<li>Test at the level of the independent unit: animal means, permutation over animals, or mixed models.</li>
<li>Plan the number of animals, not just the number of neurons.</li>
</ul>
