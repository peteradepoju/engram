<Step n={1} title="The retina" minutes={20} />

Light is absorbed by photoreceptors, rods for dim light and three kinds of cones for daylight and colour. Their signals pass through bipolar cells to **retinal ganglion cells**, whose axons form the optic nerve, with horizontal and amacrine cells adding lateral connections at each stage. About a hundred million photoreceptors in each human eye converge onto about a million ganglion cells, so the retina must compress what it sends, and it does so selectively. Kuffler (1953) mapped ganglion cell receptive fields by moving a small spot of light across the retina and found the arrangement that dominates early vision: a centre and an antagonistic surround. ON-centre cells fire when light falls on the centre and are suppressed by light on the surround; OFF-centre cells do the opposite.

A convenient model is the **difference of Gaussians** (DoG): a narrow positive Gaussian for the centre minus a broader, weaker Gaussian for the surround,

$$
k(\mathbf x) = \frac{1}{2\pi\sigma_c^2} e^{-|\mathbf x|^2/2\sigma_c^2} - w\,\frac{1}{2\pi\sigma_s^2} e^{-|\mathbf x|^2/2\sigma_s^2},
$$

with $\sigma_s$ larger than $\sigma_c$ and $w$ a little below 1.

The response grows as the spot fills the centre, peaks for a spot about 1.2° across, and then falls as the spot spreads over the surround; the largest spot, 8° across, evokes only about a fifth of the peak. That answers the prediction: it rises and then falls. Kuffler saw exactly this, and the shape of the curve is the simplest way to measure the strength and extent of a cell's surround in an experiment. The response never falls to zero because the surround's weight here, 0.85, is less than the centre's.

<Step n={3} title="Edges, not light" minutes={25} />

Because the centre and surround nearly cancel, a DoG cell barely responds to uniform illumination; it responds to differences, to where light changes across space. Filter an image with the kernel and the regions of constant brightness fade to near zero, while edges stand out, with a bright band on the light side and a dark band on the dark side. These are close to the **Mach bands** that people see at the edges of graded steps of brightness. Separate ON and OFF cells, by rectifying the filtered signal positively and negatively, report the two sides of each edge.

Far from the edge the output is zero on the dark side and only 0.15 on the bright side, the small part of uniform light that leaks through because the surround is slightly weaker than the centre. At the edge, the ON response peaks a few pixels into the bright side at about 0.39, more than twice the bright plateau, and the OFF response peaks a few pixels into the dark side. The image the brain receives is dominated by its edges, and most of the bandwidth of the optic nerve is spent on them.

<Step n={4} title="Adaptation" minutes={30} />

Light intensities in the world span about ten orders of magnitude, from starlight to sunlight, while a ganglion cell's firing rate spans perhaps two. The retina copes by **adapting**: its gain falls as the background brightens, so that it responds to contrast, the relative change in light, rather than absolute intensity. Perception follows **Weber's law** over much of this range: the smallest detectable change in intensity is a fixed fraction of the background. A simple model is divisive gain control,

$$
r = \frac{I - I_b}{I_b + I_0},
$$

where $I_b$ is the background and $I_0$ a constant that sets where adaptation begins. Above $I_0$ the response to a given contrast is the same at every background; below it, the cell responds to absolute changes in light.

With divisive gain, the response to a 10% flash is about 0.1 at every background well above $I_0$, over four orders of magnitude, while the linear model's response grows a millionfold over the six decades tested. Real adaptation happens at several stages and speeds, in the photoreceptors themselves within seconds and in the retinal circuit, and it adapts to contrast as well as to mean light, but the principle is the same: send the brain relative changes, scaled to the current conditions.

<Callout kind="note">

The retina is far richer than a bank of DoG filters. Primates have around twenty types of ganglion cell, each tiling the retina with its own receptive field size and preference: midget and parasol cells carry fine detail and motion to the LGN, while others detect object motion, approaching objects or the direction of image motion. Gollisch and Meister (2010) review how such specific computations are performed within the retina itself, before any signal reaches the brain.

</Callout>
