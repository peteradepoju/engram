<Step n={1} title="The pathway" minutes={20} />

The optic nerves meet at the optic chiasm, where fibres from the nasal half of each retina cross, so that each side of the brain receives the opposite half of the visual field. Most fibres end in the LGN, whose layers keep the two eyes and the main ganglion cell classes separate, and whose cells have centre-surround receptive fields much like the retina's. LGN axons project to layer 4 of V1. In V1, Hubel and Wiesel (1962) found **simple cells**, with separate ON and OFF subregions arranged side by side and elongated along one axis, so that a bar at one orientation and position drives them strongly; and **complex cells** (Day 3), which prefer an orientation but respond to it anywhere within their receptive field.

<Step n={2} title="Gabor receptive fields" minutes={30} />

Jones and Palmer (1987) mapped the two-dimensional receptive fields of simple cells in cat V1 with small flashed spots and fitted them with **Gabor functions**:

$$
g(x, y) = \exp\!\left(-\frac{x'^2}{2\sigma_x^2} - \frac{y'^2}{2\sigma_y^2}\right)\cos(2\pi f x' + \phi),
$$

where $x' = x\cos\theta + y\sin\theta$ and $y' = -x\sin\theta + y\cos\theta$ rotate the coordinates to the preferred orientation $\theta$, $f$ is the preferred spatial frequency and $\phi$ the phase, which decides whether the field has an ON centre flanked by OFF regions (even, $\phi = 0$) or an ON and an OFF region side by side (odd, $\phi = \pi/2$). The fits left little structure in the residuals. A simple cell's response is approximately a rectified linear function of the stimulus: the dot product of the image with this receptive field, set to zero when negative.

The cell responds best to gratings at 30°, the orientation of its receptive field, and almost not at all at 120°, where the grating's stripes run along the Gabor's ON and OFF bands and excitation and inhibition cancel. A note on convention: in this code an orientation θ is the direction along which the grating varies, so its stripes run at θ + 90°; papers use both conventions, and it is worth checking which one before comparing numbers. The tuning has a half-width of a few tens of degrees, set by the length of the envelope along the stripes ($\sigma_y$): a longer field integrates along a longer line and is more selective.

<Step n={3} title="Spatial frequency" minutes={20} />

A Gabor's sinusoid also makes the cell selective for **spatial frequency**, the number of grating cycles per degree. Its tuning is roughly a Gaussian on a log scale, centred on $f$, with a bandwidth set by the number of cycles that fit in the envelope: a long envelope with many cycles gives narrow tuning, a short one broad tuning. V1 cells span a range of preferred frequencies, together covering what the eye can resolve, and each has a bandwidth of about one to one and a half octaves.

The cell prefers 1 cycle per degree, the frequency of its sinusoid, with a bandwidth of about 1.3 octaves, close to typical V1 values. Doubling the envelope's width across the stripes narrows the bandwidth to about 0.5 octaves. This is the uncertainty principle of Week 11 in action: a filter cannot be narrow in both space and frequency, and Gabor functions are the ones that achieve the best joint resolution, which was part of the original appeal of Gabor models of V1.

<Step n={4} title="Orientation from alignment" minutes={35} />

Where does orientation come from, if V1's inputs from the LGN are not oriented? Hubel and Wiesel proposed that a simple cell sums the inputs of several LGN cells whose receptive field centres lie along a line. Their sum is an elongated receptive field, and a bar along that line covers all the centres at once while a bar across it covers only one. Reid and Alonso (1995) later recorded LGN and V1 cells together in cats and found that connected pairs had overlapping receptive fields of matching sign, as the model requires.

The sum of five centre-surround fields in a vertical row is an elongated ON region flanked by OFF regions, and it responds best to vertical stripes, with an orientation selectivity index of about 0.44, which answers the prediction. Its tuning is broader than a Gabor's of similar size, because each LGN field responds a little at every orientation, and tuning sharpens if more inputs are added along the row or the output threshold is raised.

<Callout kind="think">

The feedforward model explains where orientation comes from, but not everything about it. Real orientation tuning keeps its width as contrast changes, whereas a thresholded linear model broadens at high contrast (the iceberg effect), and cortical cells receive far more input from other cortical cells than from the LGN. Models in which recurrent cortical connections sharpen or stabilise tuning, and the normalisation of Day 3, address these gaps. The debate between feedforward and recurrent accounts ran for decades; most current models include both.
