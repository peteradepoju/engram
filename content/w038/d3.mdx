<Step n={1} title="The complex cell" minutes={20} />

Hubel and Wiesel found that many V1 cells could not be mapped into ON and OFF subregions: a bar of the right orientation drove them anywhere within the receptive field, light or dark. Adelson and Bergen (1985) and others proposed the **energy model**: a complex cell sums the squared outputs of two simple-cell-like filters with the same orientation and frequency but phases 90° apart, a **quadrature pair**. Because $\cos^2 + \sin^2 = 1$, the sum of squares of the two filters' responses to a grating does not depend on the grating's phase, which is phase invariance. A drifting grating sweeps through all phases, so a simple cell's response rises and falls at the drift frequency, while a complex cell's stays nearly constant. The ratio of the first harmonic of the response to its mean, **F1/F0**, separates them: above 1 for simple cells, below 1 for complex cells (Skottun and colleagues, 1991).

As the grating drifts, the simple cell's response rises and falls once per cycle, half-wave rectified, with an F1/F0 of about 1.57, the value for a pure rectified sinusoid. The complex cell's response is flat: F1/F0 is zero to numerical precision, because the even and odd filters' squared responses trade off exactly. Real complex cells are not perfectly invariant (their F1/F0 values are small but not zero), and the distribution of F1/F0 across V1 is bimodal, which is one reason the simple and complex categories have lasted.

<Step n={3} title="Normalisation and contrast" minutes={30} />

Heeger's normalisation model divides each cell's energy $E_i$ by the summed energy of a pool of cells with all orientations, plus a constant:

$$
R_i = \frac{E_i}{\sigma^2 + \sum_j E_j}.
$$

For a grating of contrast $c$, every energy scales as $c^2$, so $R_i = \frac{c^2 \hat E_i}{\sigma^2 + c^2 \sum_j \hat E_j}$, where hats mean energies at full contrast. That is the **Naka-Rushton** function $c^2/(c_{50}^2 + c^2)$ times a fixed tuning curve: the response saturates, with half saturation at $c_{50} = \sigma/\sqrt{\sum_j \hat E_j}$, and the shape of the tuning does not change with contrast, because contrast only multiplies the whole curve. Both properties are seen in V1 and are hard to get from a fixed threshold.

The contrast response rises steeply from 5% to 30% and then saturates, reaching 88% of its maximum at 50% contrast, with half saturation near 20%, as set by $\sigma^2$. The normalised tuning curves at 10% and 100% contrast are identical to two decimal places: contrast scales the response without changing its selectivity. The threshold model, by contrast, has five orientations above half its maximum at 20% contrast and nine at 100%: at high contrast more of the tuning curve rises above the threshold, which is the iceberg effect that V1 does not show.

<Step n={4} title="Suppression from outside the tuning" minutes={25} />

The normalisation pool includes cells of every orientation, so a stimulus that excites none of a cell's own filters can still enlarge its denominator. Adding an orthogonal grating, a **mask**, to the preferred one should therefore reduce the response, even though the mask alone gives none: **cross-orientation suppression**, one of the clearest signatures of normalisation in V1 and in perception.

The orthogonal mask alone gives no response at all, yet adding it to the preferred grating cuts the response from about 0.46 to 0.25, a suppression of 46%, which answers the prediction: it goes down. In this model the suppression comes entirely from the denominator. In V1 it is fast and broadly tuned, and Freeman, Durand, Kiper and Carandini (2002) argued that much of it arises from the thalamic inputs to cortex rather than from inhibition between cortical cells, a reminder that one equation can have several circuit implementations.

<Callout kind="note">

Normalisation is not only a V1 phenomenon. Carandini and Heeger (2012) collected evidence for the same computation in the retina, olfactory system, auditory cortex, attention, multisensory integration and value coding in decision making, and proposed that it is a canonical neural computation, one that the brain applies in many places for the same reasons: to keep responses in range, to make them invariant to nuisance variables such as contrast, and to sharpen selectivity by competition.

</Callout>
