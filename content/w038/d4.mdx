<Step n={1} title="Motion as orientation in space-time" minutes={20} />

Take one row of an image and stack it over time: a plot with space on one axis and time on the other. A stationary bar is a vertical stripe in this **space-time** plot; a bar moving at constant speed is a tilted stripe, whose slope is its velocity. A drifting grating is a set of tilted stripes. Detecting motion is therefore like detecting orientation, but in space-time, and the tools of Day 2 carry over: a receptive field oriented in space-time responds best to stimuli moving at the matching velocity in the matching direction.

A **separable** receptive field, the product of a spatial profile and a temporal profile, is never oriented in space-time, so it cannot prefer one direction. Direction selectivity needs an **inseparable** receptive field, which can be built by adding two separable ones whose spatial and temporal profiles are each in quadrature: the same trick as $\cos a \cos b + \sin a \sin b = \cos(a - b)$.

The separable filter gives exactly the same response to both directions (a direction index of 0), which answers the prediction: no. The oriented filter responds strongly to rightward drift and almost not at all to leftward, a direction index of about 1. In the plots, the separable filter is a checkerboard of blobs aligned with the axes, while the oriented one has stripes tilted in space-time, matching the slope of a rightward-moving grating. V1 simple cells with inseparable space-time receptive fields, mapped with reverse correlation, look just like this.

<Step n={3} title="Motion energy" minutes={25} />

A single oriented filter still depends on the grating's phase, so its response flickers as the grating moves. Adelson and Bergen squared and summed the outputs of a quadrature pair of oriented filters, exactly as in the complex cell of Day 3, to get **motion energy**: a steady signal for motion in the preferred direction. Subtracting the energy of the opposite direction gives **opponent motion energy**, positive for one direction and negative for the other, which matches the direction tuning of many V1 complex cells and of perceived motion.

Rightward motion energy is constant across the grating's phase to four decimal places: the quadrature pair removes the flicker, as it removed phase dependence in the complex cell. Opponent energy is large and positive for the rightward grating and equal and negative for the leftward one, a clean signed signal for direction. The model also predicts illusions: two frames of a pattern shifted a little, or a stationary pattern whose contrast reverses in the right sequence, give motion energy and are seen as moving.

<Step n={4} title="Speed and the dorsal stream" minutes={30} />

A filter oriented in space-time with spatial frequency $f_x$ and temporal frequency $f_t$ prefers gratings moving at speed $v = f_t / f_x$. Measured with gratings of one spatial frequency, its response peaks at that speed. V1 cells, however, are tuned to temporal frequency more than to speed: change the grating's spatial frequency and their preferred speed changes with it. Neurons in area **MT** (middle temporal), the motion area of the dorsal stream, which receives input from direction-selective V1 cells, come closer to true speed tuning, and many MT cells respond to the motion of whole patterns rather than of their components.

With gratings of 1 cycle per degree, the filters prefer 8 degrees per second, the ratio of their temporal to spatial frequency. With gratings of 2 cycles per degree, the preferred speed halves to 4 degrees per second, because the unit is really tuned to the temporal frequency of 8 Hz, as many V1 neurons are. Recordings in MT find that many neurons hold their preferred speed better across spatial frequencies than V1 neurons do, though the degree varies from cell to cell, which suggests that MT combines V1 inputs tuned to different spatial and temporal frequencies along a line of constant speed.

<Callout kind="think">

A V1 cell sees motion through a small aperture, and through an aperture only the motion perpendicular to an edge is visible: a diagonal line moving right looks the same as one moving down and right. This **aperture problem** means each V1 cell reports only a component of an object's motion. Movshon, Adelson, Gizzi and Newsome (1985) showed that some MT cells respond to the direction of a moving plaid as a whole, the pattern, rather than to its two component gratings, integrating component signals into object motion. How they do it, by intersecting constraints or by a weighted vector average, is still studied.
