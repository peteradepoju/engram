<Step n={1} title="The cochlea" minutes={20} />

Sound enters the ear canal, vibrates the eardrum, and is passed by three small bones to the fluid-filled **cochlea**, a coiled tube about 3.5 cm long in humans. Running its length is the **basilar membrane**, stiff and narrow at the base and floppy and wide at the apex. A sound sets up a travelling wave on the membrane that peaks at a place set by its frequency: high frequencies near the base, low ones near the apex. Georg von Békésy measured these waves in cadaver cochleas and received the 1961 Nobel Prize for it. Inner hair cells along the membrane convert its motion into transmitter release onto auditory nerve fibres, each of which therefore responds to a band of frequencies around its **characteristic frequency**. Outer hair cells amplify the motion, sharpening the tuning and compressing the range of levels.

The result is **tonotopy**: a map of frequency onto place, kept from the cochlea through the brainstem to auditory cortex, just as retinotopy is kept in vision. The cochlea is, to a first approximation, a bank of overlapping band-pass filters.

<Step n={2} title="The ERB scale" minutes={25} />

How wide are the cochlea's filters? Listeners' ability to hear a tone in noise depends on how much noise falls inside the filter centred on the tone, so measuring detection thresholds in noise with notches of different widths reveals the filter's shape. Glasberg and Moore (1990) summarised such measurements with the **equivalent rectangular bandwidth**:

$$
\text{ERB}(f) = 24.7\,(4.37 f/1000 + 1)\ \text{Hz},
$$

about 35 Hz at 100 Hz, 133 Hz at 1 kHz and 456 Hz at 4 kHz. Filters are narrow in absolute terms at low frequencies and broad at high ones, but roughly constant as a fraction of frequency above about 500 Hz. Spacing filters one ERB apart gives the **ERB-number** scale, $21.4\log_{10}(4.37 f/1000 + 1)$, which runs from about 3 at 100 Hz to about 39 at 16 kHz.

Thirty-two filters equally spaced on the ERB-number scale between 100 Hz and 6 kHz start about 33 Hz apart and end about 560 Hz apart. Neighbouring filters sit about one ERB apart everywhere, so the bank devotes as many filters to the octave from 200 to 400 Hz as to a much wider band at the top. The human cochlea has roughly 40 ERBs from bottom to top, a useful number to remember when designing auditory models.

<Step n={3} title="Gammatone filters" minutes={30} />

The **gammatone** filter, introduced to auditory modelling by Patterson and colleagues, has an impulse response that is a tone inside a gamma-shaped envelope:

$$
g(t) = t^{n-1} e^{-2\pi b t} \cos(2\pi f_c t), \qquad t \ge 0,
$$

with order $n = 4$ and $b = 1.019\,\text{ERB}(f_c)$, which makes its equivalent rectangular bandwidth equal to the ERB. It fits the impulse responses of auditory nerve fibres measured by reverse correlation (de Boer and de Jongh, 1978) and is the standard linear model of the cochlea.

The impulse response rings at 1 kHz under an envelope that rises and decays over a few milliseconds, and its power spectrum peaks at 1000 Hz with an equivalent rectangular bandwidth of 132.7 Hz, matching the formula's 132.6 Hz: the factor 1.019 is exactly what makes an order-4 gammatone's ERB equal to $b/1.019$. A filter at 4 kHz would be about three and a half times wider and its impulse response correspondingly shorter, the time-frequency trade-off of Week 11 built into the ear.

<Step n={4} title="Resolving harmonics" minutes={30} />

A voiced vowel or a musical note is a **harmonic complex**: energy at multiples of a fundamental frequency $F_0$. Harmonics are equally spaced in hertz, but cochlear filters widen with frequency, so low harmonics each fall in their own filter (**resolved**) while high harmonics share filters and interact (**unresolved**), producing beats at $F_0$ instead. Pass a harmonic complex with $F_0 = 150$ Hz through the filter bank, measure the root-mean-square output of each filter, the **excitation pattern**, and count the harmonics that make separate peaks in it.

By this criterion the first six harmonics of a 150 Hz voice make separate peaks in the excitation pattern and the rest merge into a smooth ridge, which answers the prediction: the 2nd and 3rd are separated, the 12th and 13th are not. Psychophysical estimates put the boundary somewhat higher, around the 8th to 10th harmonic, because the living cochlea's filters are sharper at moderate levels than a linear gammatone bank. Resolved harmonics give the strongest sense of pitch, and when only unresolved harmonics are present listeners still hear the pitch, weaker, from the beats at F0.

<Callout kind="note">

The gammatone filter bank is linear, but the cochlea is not. Outer hair cells amplify quiet sounds far more than loud ones, compressing a range of about 120 dB into the much smaller range of neural firing, and their gain makes filters sharper at low levels. Auditory nerve fibres also phase-lock: below about 4 kHz they tend to fire at a particular phase of the sound wave, so the timing of spikes carries frequency information beyond the place code, which tomorrow's sound localisation depends on.
