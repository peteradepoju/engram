<Step n={1} title="Two ears" minutes={20} />

Sound travels at about 343 m/s, and the ears are about 18 cm apart around the head, so a sound directly to one side arrives at the far ear about 0.65 ms later: the maximum **interaural time difference** (ITD). Humans can detect ITDs as small as 10 to 20 microseconds, far shorter than an action potential. The head also casts an acoustic shadow, making the far ear quieter: the **interaural level difference** (ILD), large for high frequencies, whose wavelengths are short compared with the head, and small for low ones, which bend around it. The **duplex theory**, going back to Lord Rayleigh, holds that we use ITDs for low frequencies and ILDs for high ones.

Jeffress (1948) proposed that the brain measures ITD with **delay lines and coincidence detectors**. Axons from the two ears run in opposite directions along a row of neurons, so each neuron receives the two inputs with a different relative delay; a neuron fires most when the sound's ITD exactly cancels its internal delay. The array is a map of ITD, and so of direction. In barn owls, Carr and Konishi (1990) found axonal delay lines and a map of ITD in the nucleus laminaris much as Jeffress described. In mammals the story differs, as you will see.

The cross-correlation of broadband noise has one sharp peak, at 302 microseconds against a true 300 (the samples at 96 kHz are about 10 microseconds apart), and adding independent noise at each ear at equal power changes the peak's height but not its position. Each internal delay in the array is one coincidence detector, and the curve you plotted is the pattern of activity across the array: Jeffress's place code, read by finding the most active neuron.

<Step n={3} title="From delay to direction" minutes={20} />

For a spherical head of radius $a$ and a distant source at azimuth $\theta$ (0 straight ahead, 90° to the side), Woodworth's formula gives the ITD as the extra path around the head:

$$
\text{ITD}(\theta) = \frac{a}{c}\left(\theta + \sin\theta\right),
$$

with $\theta$ in radians, $a \approx 8.75$ cm and $c = 343$ m/s. It is not linear: the ITD changes fastest near the midline, which is one reason localisation is sharpest straight ahead.

The model head gives a maximum ITD of 656 microseconds at 90°, and the estimated 302 microseconds corresponds to an azimuth of 35°. <Step n={4} title="When timing fails" minutes={35} />

Noise has a single sharp peak in its cross-correlation, but a pure tone's cross-correlation is itself a cosine, with peaks one period apart. If the period is shorter than the range of possible ITDs, more than one internal delay matches equally well and the direction is ambiguous. This **phase ambiguity** is one reason ITD cues are useful mainly below about 1.5 kHz in humans, and why high frequencies rely on level differences (and on timing of the slow envelope of complex sounds).

At 500 Hz the period is 2 ms, longer than the whole range of ITDs, so the cross-correlation has one peak in range, at the true delay. At 2 kHz the period is 0.5 ms, and the cross-correlation has two peaks of equal height within the range of possible ITDs, at +300 and about -200 microseconds, which answers the prediction: from timing alone the brain cannot tell left from right. For such tones listeners rely on level differences, which the head shadow makes large at high frequencies, as the duplex theory says.

<Callout kind="research">

In small mammals such as gerbils, ITD-sensitive neurons in the medial superior olive mostly prefer delays outside the range the head can produce, and the steep slopes of their tuning curves, not their peaks, sit across the physiological range. McAlpine, Jiang and Palmer (2001) and Brand and colleagues (2002) argued that mammals represent ITD by comparing the overall activity of two broadly tuned populations, one on each side of the brain, rather than by a Jeffress-style place map.
