<Step n={1} title="The olfactory pathway" minutes={20} />

In the fruit fly, about 50 types of **olfactory receptor neuron** (ORN) each express one receptor protein, and each type responds to many odours with different strengths. Neurons of one type converge on one **glomerulus** in the antennal lobe, where **projection neurons** (PNs) carry the signal on: a code of about 50 channels. PNs then project to about 2,000 **Kenyon cells** in the mushroom body, each of which receives input from about six or seven randomly chosen glomeruli (Caron and colleagues, 2013). A single inhibitory neuron, APL, gives feedback to all Kenyon cells, and the result is a sparse code in which only about 5 to 10% of Kenyon cells respond to any odour. Mammals have a similar plan on a larger scale: about 1,000 receptor types, glomeruli in the olfactory bulb, and expansion into piriform cortex.

The two key operations are a **dimension expansion** (50 to 2,000) through random, sparse connections, and a **winner-take-all** that keeps only the most active cells. Dasgupta and colleagues recognised this as a form of locality-sensitive hashing (LSH), a family of algorithms for finding similar items quickly in large databases by giving similar items similar short codes.

<Step n={2} title="Normalising concentration" minutes={25} />

A stronger dose of the same odour activates every receptor type more, so the raw ORN pattern changes with concentration. In the antennal lobe, lateral inhibition divides each glomerulus's output by the total activity (Olsen, Bhandawat and Wilson, 2010), the divisive normalisation of Week 38, so the pattern across PNs depends on which odour it is more than on how much. Dasgupta and colleagues model this step by centring each odour's vector on its mean.

Over a hundredfold change in concentration, the raw response vector grows more than fortyfold in length, but after normalisation the first five channels keep the same pattern of high and low values; the cosine similarity between the normalised patterns at the lowest and highest concentrations is about 0.91. What remains of the change comes from receptor saturation: at high concentration the strongest channels approach their ceiling and the pattern flattens, a limit that no later normalisation can undo. With 20 tags, the fly hash finds about 82% of each odour's true nearest neighbours, against 59% for sign LSH, which answers the prediction: random wiring preserves similarity, and the expansion with winner-take-all preserves it better than the same number of random sign bits. With 50 tags both reach about 86%: the advantage is largest when the code is short. Dasgupta and colleagues found the same pattern on image datasets used to benchmark similarity search, which is why the paper was published as computer science as much as neuroscience.

<Step n={4} title="How many inputs per cell?" minutes={25} />

Each Kenyon cell in the fly receives about six or seven inputs. What happens if it receives one, or all fifty? With one input, a Kenyon cell merely copies a single channel and the expansion adds nothing. With all fifty, every Kenyon cell computes the same sum, and the code cannot distinguish anything. Litwin-Kumar, Harris, Axel, Sompolinsky and Abbott (2017) analysed this trade-off for expansion layers in the fly mushroom body and the cerebellum, and found that small numbers of inputs per cell maximise the dimension of the expanded representation, close to the anatomical values.

With one input per Kenyon cell, precision is only about 0.33: each cell copies one channel, and keeping the top 20 cells just keeps the strongest few channels. Precision rises steeply to 0.82 with six inputs and continues to about 0.92 with 25, then collapses to 0.12 when every cell sums all 50, because all cells then compute the same number.
