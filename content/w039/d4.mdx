<Step n={1} title="Redundancy" minutes={20} />

Barlow (1961) proposed that sensory neurons should transmit information efficiently, removing the redundancy of their inputs, so that each spike says something new (Week 20). For images, the main redundancy is spatial correlation: knowing one pixel tells you a lot about its neighbours. In frequency terms, correlation means power concentrated at low spatial frequencies. Field (1987) and others measured the power spectrum of natural images and found it falls as roughly $1/f^2$: an image of a forest and an image of a city both have far more power in broad, slow variations than in fine detail.

A filter that **whitens** the image, multiplying each frequency's amplitude by $f$ to flatten the spectrum, removes the correlations: its outputs at different positions are uncorrelated. Whitening boosts high frequencies, which in space means emphasising differences between neighbours, a centre minus a surround. But high frequencies are also where noise dominates, because the signal there is weak. Boosting them blindly would amplify noise. The optimal filter must trade the two.

<Step n={3} title="Whitening with noise" minutes={35} />

Atick and Redlich (1992) asked for the linear filter that transmits the most information about images with spectrum $S(f)$ through a channel with input noise of power $N$ (from photoreceptors) and a limit on output power. Their solution has two factors. A **Wiener filter**, $S/(S + N)$, first suppresses frequencies where noise outweighs signal. Then a **whitening** factor, $1/\sqrt{S + N}$, flattens what is left:

$$
K(f) = \frac{S(f)}{S(f) + N}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{S(f) + N}}.
$$

Where signal is strong ($S \gg N$), $K \approx S^{-1/2} \propto f$: whitening, which boosts high frequencies. Where noise is strong ($S \ll N$), $K \approx S/N^{3/2}$, which falls with frequency: smoothing. The filter is band-pass, rising and then falling, and the frequency at which it turns over moves lower as noise rises.

At signal-to-noise ratios of 1 and 100, the optimal receptive field has a narrow centre one pixel in half-width and a negative surround reaching 13 to 18% of the peak: a centre-surround field, derived from nothing but the image spectrum and the noise. At very low signal-to-noise ratio the surround almost vanishes (about 1% of the peak) and the centre widens to three pixels: the filter now averages over a larger region to beat the noise. That answers the prediction: the surround should weaken in dim light, which is what Barlow, Fitzhugh and Kuffler found in the cat retina in 1957.

In the retina this means the optic nerve's limited capacity is spent on the parts of the image that are not predictable from their neighbours.

<Callout kind="think">

Whitening is only the beginning of efficient coding. A linear filter can remove only second-order correlations; natural images also have higher-order structure (edges, contours, textures) that whitening leaves untouched. Sparse coding and independent component analysis go further, and when applied to whitened natural images they produce oriented, localised, Gabor-like filters (Olshausen and Field, Week 13), the receptive fields of V1. Tomorrow you apply the same idea to sounds.
