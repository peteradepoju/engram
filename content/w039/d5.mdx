<Step n={1} title="Beyond whitening" minutes={20} />

Whitening (Day 4) makes the outputs of a filter bank uncorrelated, but uncorrelated is not independent: two signals can have zero correlation and still be strongly related, for example when large values of one tend to coincide with large values of the other. Natural signals are full of such higher-order structure. **Independent component analysis** looks for a linear transform whose outputs are as statistically independent as possible. By the central limit theorem, mixtures of independent signals look more Gaussian than the signals themselves, so ICA can find the independent components by finding projections that are as **non-Gaussian** as possible, usually as sparse or heavy-tailed as possible.

Bell and Sejnowski (1997) applied ICA to natural images and found Gabor-like filters, as Olshausen and Field had found with sparse coding (Week 13). Lewicki (2002) applied it to sounds, in three classes: environmental sounds (rustling, crunching, snapping), animal vocalisations, and human speech.

The tonal snippets look like smooth, beating waves that fill all 4 ms; the transient ones are mostly flat with brief bursts. After whitening to 32 dimensions, each dataset's covariance is the identity: every remaining direction has equal variance and no correlation with any other. From here on, whatever structure ICA finds must be higher-order, invisible to the covariance.

<Step n={3} title="FastICA" minutes={35} />

On whitened data $Z$, FastICA (Hyvärinen, 1999) looks for an orthogonal matrix $W$ whose rows give maximally non-Gaussian outputs $Y = WZ$. With the nonlinearity $g = \tanh$, each iteration updates all rows at once,

$$
W \leftarrow \frac{1}{N} g(Y) Z^\top - \text{diag}\!\left(\overline{g'(Y)}\right) W, \qquad g'(y) = 1 - \tanh^2 y,
$$

then makes the rows orthonormal again by symmetric decorrelation, $W \leftarrow (WW^\top)^{-1/2} W$, computed with the singular value decomposition. The learned **basis functions**, the waveforms that the independent components multiply to rebuild a sound, are the columns of $(WV)^{-1}$.

  from scipy.stats import kurtosis

  def fastica(Z, k, iters=200, seed=0):
      g = np.random.default_rng(seed)
      W = np.linalg.qr(g.normal(size=(k, k)))[0]
      for it in range(iters):
          Y = W @ Z
          gY = np.tanh(Y)
          W_new = gY @ Z.T / Z.shape[1] - np.diag((1 - gY ** 2).mean(1)) @ W
          U, S, Vt = np.linalg.svd(W_new)
          W_new = U @ Vt
          if np.max(np.abs(np.abs(np.diag(W_new @ W.T)) - 1)) < 1e-6:
              return W_new, it
          W = W_new
      return W, iters

  Z_t, V_t = whiten(X_trans, 32)
  W_t, n_it = fastica(Z_t, 32)
  kurt_ica = np.mean(kurtosis(W_t @ Z_t, axis=1))
  kurt_pca = np.mean(kurtosis(Z_t, axis=1))
  print(f"converged after {n_it} iterations; mean excess kurtosis: PCA components {kurt_pca:.2f}, ICA components {kurt_ica:.2f}")

FastICA converges in under two hundred iterations, and its components are far heavier-tailed than the principal components (mean excess kurtosis about 24 against about 6): each component is near zero most of the time and large occasionally, the signature of a sparse, independent source. For the transient sounds, each component has learned to respond to a brief ringing at one frequency, wherever it falls.

Tonal sounds give basis functions that fill the whole snippet (median spread 17.5 samples, almost all of the 4 ms), like Fourier components: narrow in frequency, long in time. Transient sounds give brief wavelets (median 4.9 samples, about 0.3 ms), which answers the prediction. An equal-power mixture gives something in between: mostly longer functions, with a minority of short ones and a median of about 13 samples. Lewicki found that speech, which mixes harmonic vowels with transient consonants, gave filters between the two extremes whose bandwidth grew with frequency much as the auditory nerve's does.
