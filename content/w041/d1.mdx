<Step n={1} title="The motor system" minutes={20} />

Voluntary movement involves a hierarchy of structures. **Motor cortex** (primary motor cortex, premotor and supplementary motor areas) plans and issues commands; **corticospinal** axons carry them to the spinal cord, where **motor neurons** drive muscles, and spinal circuits add reflexes and pattern generators. The **cerebellum** refines timing and coordination and is thought to hold internal models of the body (Day 3); the **basal ganglia** help select and invigorate actions. Sensory feedback from proprioceptors in muscles and joints, and from vision, returns with delays of roughly 30 to 150 ms, so fast movements cannot rely on it alone.

The problem is massively redundant: many paths, speed profiles, joint configurations and muscle activations reach the same target. Yet people choose very consistently. **Optimal control** explains such choices as minimising a cost. This week moves from costs on the movement itself (today) to costs on noise and effort (Day 2), estimation (Day 3), feedback control (Day 4) and learning (Day 5).

<Step n={2} title="The minimum-jerk trajectory" minutes={30} />

**Jerk** is the rate of change of acceleration, the third derivative of position. Flash and Hogan (1985) proposed that reaches minimise the integrated squared jerk,

$$
J = \int_0^T \dddot{x}(t)^2\,dt,
$$

for a movement from $x_0$ to $x_f$ in time $T$, starting and ending at rest with zero acceleration. The calculus of variations gives the solution (the Euler-Lagrange equation for this cost): the sixth derivative of $x$ must vanish, so $x(t)$ is a fifth-order polynomial, and the six boundary conditions fix its coefficients. With $\tau = t/T$,

$$
x(t) = x_0 + (x_f - x_0)\left(10\tau^3 - 15\tau^4 + 6\tau^5\right).
$$

The shape is the same for every distance and duration: only the scale changes.

The hand starts and ends at rest, and its velocity rises and falls in a symmetric bell, peaking at 0.94 m/s exactly halfway through, 1.875 times the average speed of 0.5 m/s, which answers the prediction. Acceleration is positive for the first half and negative for the second, smoothly passing through zero at the peak of velocity. Measured reaches match this shape closely, and because the formula has no free parameters once distance and duration are set, the match is a genuine test.

<Step n={3} title="Smoother than the alternatives" minutes={25} />

Is the fifth-order polynomial really the minimum? Compare its jerk cost with other movements that satisfy the same boundary conditions: the minimum-jerk path plus a small bump that vanishes, with its first two derivatives, at both ends. Every such perturbation should increase the cost, and the increase should grow with the square of its size, the signature of a minimum.

The minimum-jerk path costs about 822 units of squared jerk; adding a small bump that respects the boundary conditions raises the cost to about 1016, and doubling the bump raises it to about 1600. The extra cost grows fourfold when the bump doubles, as it must near a minimum, where the cost is quadratic in any small deviation. Any other way of making the same movement in the same time is jerkier.

<Step n={4} title="Reaching in two dimensions" minutes={30} />

In two dimensions, minimising the jerk of $x(t)$ and $y(t)$ separately gives the same polynomial in each coordinate, so the hand moves along a **straight line** with the bell-shaped speed profile, whatever the direction. Flash and Hogan stressed that this is a prediction about the hand's path in space, not about joint angles: a straight hand path requires curved, coordinated joint trajectories. Movements through a **via-point** are also predicted, by minimising jerk subject to passing through it, and give curved paths with two speed peaks.

The direct reach stays on the straight line to within numerical precision, with the same bell-shaped speed as the one-dimensional reach. The via-point reach passes exactly through its via-point and has two speed peaks with a slowing in between, the pattern Flash and Hogan found for movements through intermediate targets. Joining two separate segments is a simplification: the true minimum-jerk solution through a via-point does not stop there, but its speed still dips, so the qualitative prediction is the same.

<Callout kind="think">

Minimum jerk describes what reaches look like, but it does not say why the brain would care about jerk, which is not something muscles or nerves can sense directly. Its successors replace jerk with quantities that matter to the animal: the variance caused by noise in motor commands (tomorrow), effort, and the cost of missing the goal. Many of them predict nearly the same smooth trajectories, so the shape of a reach alone cannot tell them apart; their predictions differ for perturbations, noise and feedback.

</Callout>
