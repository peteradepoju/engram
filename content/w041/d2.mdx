<Step n={1} title="Signal-dependent noise" minutes={20} />

Hold a weight steady and your force fluctuates; hold a heavier one and it fluctuates more, roughly in proportion. The coefficient of variation of force (its standard deviation divided by its mean) is roughly constant over a wide range: motor noise is **signal dependent**. One reason is the way muscles are controlled: force is increased by recruiting more and larger motor units and by firing them faster, and the large units recruited at high forces each contribute big, variable twitches (Jones, Hamilton and Wolpert, 2002, made this argument quantitatively).

Signal-dependent noise has a striking consequence. With additive noise, of the same size whatever the command, the best plan would use large commands freely, since they cost no extra variability. With noise proportional to the command, large commands are expensive in accuracy. Harris and Wolpert (1998) proposed that the brain chooses the commands that minimise the variance of the final position, subject to reaching the target on average.

With signal-dependent noise the coefficient of variation is 0.1 at every level of command, a twentyfold range; with additive noise it falls from 0.5 to 0.025, because the same absolute noise matters less and less as the command grows. Measured forces in human muscles behave like the first case over most of their range, with additive noise visible only for very weak contractions.

<Step n={3} title="The minimum-variance plan" minutes={40} />

Model the arm as a mass driven by a muscle force that follows the neural command with a lag of 40 ms, in time steps of 5 ms: $\mathbf x_{t+1} = A\mathbf x_t + B u_t$, with state position, velocity and force. Each command $u_t$ carries noise of standard deviation $k|u_t|$. Because the system is linear, the position at any later time is a weighted sum of the commands, and its variance is $k^2 \sum_t w_t^2 u_t^2$, where $w_t$ is how much command $t$ moves that position. Harris and Wolpert minimised the variance of the position during a short period after the movement, subject to the mean arriving at the target and stopping there. That is a quadratic cost with linear constraints, solved in one step with Lagrange multipliers (Week 13):

$$
\min_{\mathbf u}\ \mathbf u^\top \text{diag}(\mathbf a)\,\mathbf u \ \text{ subject to } C\mathbf u = \mathbf b \quad\Rightarrow\quad \mathbf u = \text{diag}(\mathbf a)^{-1} C^\top\left(C\,\text{diag}(\mathbf a)^{-1}C^\top\right)^{-1}\mathbf b,
$$

where $a_t$ is the summed squared influence of command $t$ on the position during the hold period, and $C\mathbf u = \mathbf b$ says the final position, velocity and force are the target, 0 and 0.

<Step n={4} title="Speed against accuracy" minutes={25} />

Movement time grows with the index of difficulty, and a straight line in $\log_2(2D/W)$ explains about 88% of the variance across 12 combinations of distance and width. The model's relation is actually a power law (duration grows as a power of D/W), which a logarithm fits well over a limited range; its slope, about 0.56 s per bit, is several times the human value because the noise level here was chosen for illustration, not fitted. What the model explains is the form of the trade-off, from noise alone, not its exact numbers.

<Callout kind="note">

The same principle applied to the eyes predicts the main sequence of saccades: the systematic relation between saccade size, duration and peak velocity, and the skewed velocity profiles of large saccades. That Harris and Wolpert could explain eye and arm movements with one assumption, which is about a measurable property of motor neurons and muscles rather than an abstract cost, is why the model was influential. Later models added effort costs and feedback (Day 4) to explain what happens during movement.

</Callout>
