<Step n={1} title="Internal models" minutes={20} />

When the brain sends a motor command, it can keep a copy, the **efference copy** (von Holst and Mittelstaedt, 1950) or **corollary discharge** (Sperry, 1950). A **forward model** uses that copy and the current estimate of the body's state to predict the next state, the way a physics simulation does. Forward models are thought to explain why you cannot tickle yourself (the predicted sensation is cancelled), why the world stays still when your eyes move, and how fast movements can be corrected before feedback arrives. The cerebellum is the leading candidate for where they are learned and stored. The reverse, an **inverse model**, maps a desired movement to the command that produces it.

Prediction alone accumulates errors: any mismatch between model and body, and any noise in the body, adds up with nothing to correct it. Sensory feedback corrects, but it is delayed and noisy. The optimal combination is a Kalman filter (Week 36), with the forward model as its prediction step and delayed feedback as its measurement.

The forward model's estimate drifts away from the true position: its error averages about 17 mm in the first second and about 43 mm in the last, which answers the prediction: it grows. A 10% underestimate of the arm's gain means every centimetre moved adds a millimetre of error, and the small random variability of the real hand adds a slow random walk on top. Nothing in dead reckoning can notice, let alone correct, either kind of error.

Using delayed feedback alone, the RMS error is about 26 mm, and it is largest when the hand moves fastest, because in 100 ms a fast hand travels further from where the feedback says it was. It never drifts, since every measurement is a fresh report, but it is always late, and for a hand moving at 50 cm/s a 100 ms lag is a 5 cm error.

<Step n={4} title="Combining them" minutes={40} />

The combined estimator keeps an estimate of where the hand was $d$ steps ago, corrects it with the delayed measurement, and then runs the forward model forward over the commands sent since, to estimate where the hand is now:

<Derivation>
<div>

Delayed estimate: $\tilde x_{t-d} \leftarrow \tilde x_{t-d}^{-} + K\,(y_t - \tilde x_{t-d}^{-})$, where $\tilde x_{t-d}^{-}$ is the forward model's prediction of $x_{t-d}$ and $K$ a gain between 0 and 1.

</div>
<div>

Current estimate: $\hat x_t = \tilde x_{t-d} + \sum_{s=t-d}^{t-1} \Delta t\,\hat g\,v_s$, the forward model run over the last $d$ commands.

</div>
<div>

The first step corrects drift with feedback; the second removes the lag with prediction. With a steady-state Kalman gain this is the optimal linear estimator for this problem when the model is correct, and still a good one when it is slightly wrong.

</div>
</Derivation>

      for i in range(1, n):
          if i > d:
              xd = xd + dt * gain_model * v[i - d - 1]                 # forward model predicts x[i - d]

Combining them cuts the RMS error to about 3.5 mm, roughly a tenth of either estimate alone, and the error stays bounded over the whole 3 s. The correction step pins the delayed estimate to the measurements, so the forward model's miscalibration cannot accumulate; the prediction step covers only the last 100 ms, so its miscalibration costs only the error of 100 ms of movement. Real proprioception is less noisy than in this simulation, and the brain also has vision, but the logic of combining them is the same.

<Callout kind="history">

Wolpert, Ghahramani and Jordan (1995) had people move their hand in the dark for different durations and then judge where it was. The bias in their estimates grew for about a second and then fell, and the variance rose and then levelled off. A Kalman filter with a slightly miscalibrated forward model and delayed proprioception reproduced both curves: early on the forward model's error builds up faster than feedback can correct it; later, feedback catches up. The result was among the first quantitative evidence that the brain estimates the body's state the way an optimal observer would.

</Callout>
