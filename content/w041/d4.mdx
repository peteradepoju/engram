<Step n={1} title="Feedback control" minutes={20} />

A **feedback controller** chooses each command from the current state: $u_t = \pi_t(\mathbf x_t)$. Optimal feedback control chooses the policy $\pi$ that minimises the expected cost of the whole movement. For linear dynamics $\mathbf x_{t+1} = A\mathbf x_t + B\mathbf u_t + \text{noise}$ and a quadratic cost

$$
J = \mathbf x_T^\top Q_f\,\mathbf x_T + \sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbf u_t^\top R\,\mathbf u_t,
$$

which penalises the final error and the effort spent, the optimal policy is linear, $\mathbf u_t = -K_t\mathbf x_t$, with gains found by a **backward Riccati recursion**: start from $S_T = Q_f$ and work back,

$$
K_t = (R + B^\top S_{t+1}B)^{-1}B^\top S_{t+1}A, \qquad S_t = A^\top S_{t+1}(A - BK_t).
$$

This is the **linear quadratic regulator** (LQR); with additive Gaussian noise the gains are unchanged (certainty equivalence), and with a Kalman filter for the state (Day 3), it becomes LQG. Todorov and Jordan (2002) argued that this framework, not trajectory planning, describes how the motor system coordinates movement.

The reach ends within a hundredth of a millimetre of the target and almost at rest. The feedback gains are not constant: the position gain starts at about 16 and climbs steeply in the last tenth of a second to well over a thousand. Early in the movement there is time to fix errors cheaply later, so the controller tolerates them; near the end every error must be fixed at once, whatever it costs. The velocity profile is smooth and single-peaked, though not a perfect minimum-jerk bell, because this cost penalises force rather than jerk.

After the push, the feedback controller still ends within a fraction of a millimetre of the target; replaying the planned commands, the hand overshoots by about 87 mm. The feedback path does not return to the unperturbed trajectory: it brakes and lands on the target along a new path, the cheapest from where the hand now is. People respond to pushes in the same way, and experiments by Scott and colleagues found that the size of their corrections depends on the shape of the target, wide targets attracting smaller corrections than narrow ones, as optimal feedback control predicts.

<Step n={4} title="Minimal intervention" minutes={30} />

Now a redundant task. Two effectors, $x_1$ and $x_2$, each moved by its own command, must end with $x_1 + x_2 = 1$; how the total is shared is irrelevant. Both start at zero, and noise perturbs each effector at every step. Compare two controllers with the same effort cost. The **optimal** one penalises only the task error, $(x_1 + x_2 - 1)^2$. A **servo** controller, following a desired trajectory as trajectory-planning theories suggest, penalises each effector's distance from its own share, $(x_1 - 0.5)^2 + (x_2 - 0.5)^2$. Run each 300 times and measure the variance of the end points along the task-relevant direction ($x_1 + x_2$) and the irrelevant one ($x_1 - x_2$).

Both controllers achieve the same task accuracy, with end-point variance of about 0.0008 along the task direction. The optimal controller's end points, though, spread along the line $x_1 + x_2 = 1$, with variance across effectors about 77 times larger than along the task; the servo controller's end points form a round cloud, and it spends about 10% more effort correcting deviations that never mattered. That answers the prediction: each finger varies far more than the total. Latash, Scholz, Todorov and many others have found this structure in real movements, from finger forces to pointing and posture.

<Callout kind="research">

Scholz and Schöner (1999) introduced the uncontrolled manifold analysis to measure exactly this structure in real movements: split joint-angle variability into the part that leaves the task variable unchanged and the part that changes it. Todorov and Jordan showed that optimal feedback control predicts it.
