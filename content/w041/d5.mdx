<Step n={1} title="Adaptation" minutes={20} />

In Shadmehr and Mussa-Ivaldi's (1994) experiment, people held a robot handle and reached to targets while the robot applied a velocity-dependent force perpendicular to the movement, a **curl field**. At first, reaches curved in the direction of the force; with practice they straightened, as people learned to produce a compensating force before feedback could act, evidence for a learned internal model of the field (Day 3's forward models, now inverse). When the field was unexpectedly removed, reaches curved the other way, mirroring the original errors: an **aftereffect**. Adaptation to visual rotations, prisms and saccade target jumps follows similar rules.

A **state-space model** describes such learning trial by trial. The motor output on trial $n$ is an internal estimate $x_n$ of the perturbation $f_n$; the error is $e_n = f_n - x_n$; and the estimate is updated by a fraction of the error, while slowly decaying:

$$
x_{n+1} = A\,x_n + B\,e_n,
$$

with retention $A$ just below 1 and learning rate $B$ between 0 and 1. In an **error-clamp** trial the robot holds the hand on a straight path, so the error is zero by design and only retention acts.

<Step n={3} title="Two processes and spontaneous recovery" minutes={40} />

Smith, Ghazizadeh and Shadmehr (2006) proposed that the output is the sum of two states, both driven by the same error:

$$
x^{f}_{n+1} = A_f x^f_n + B_f e_n, \qquad x^{s}_{n+1} = A_s x^s_n + B_s e_n, \qquad x_n = x^f_n + x^s_n,
$$

with the fast process learning quickly and forgetting quickly ($A_f = 0.59$, $B_f = 0.21$) and the slow process learning slowly and retaining well ($A_s = 0.992$, $B_s = 0.02$), the values they fitted to their data. Run the paradigm of the prediction: adapt for 200 trials, de-adapt in the opposite field until the output returns to zero, then clamp the error at zero for 100 trials.

Both models de-adapt in a few trials, bringing their output to zero. In the clamp, the single-rate model's output stays at zero: it has one state, and that state is zero. The two-rate model's output climbs back to about 0.45 within a few dozen trials, which answers the prediction: it drifts back towards the first adaptation. During de-adaptation the fast state swung strongly negative while the slow state stayed positive, summing to zero; in the clamp the fast state decays quickly and reveals the slow state beneath. Smith and colleagues observed exactly this spontaneous recovery in people adapting to force fields.

<Step n={4} title="Savings" minutes={20} />

People relearn a perturbation faster the second time, even after their behaviour has returned to baseline: **savings**. In the two-rate model, washing out the behaviour need not wash out the slow state; a rapid de-adaptation drives the fast state negative while the slow state stays positive, so relearning starts with a head start hidden beneath a zero output.

The model needs 22 trials to reach half adaptation the first time and only 2 when relearning after de-adaptation, even though its output started from zero both times. The difference is the slow state, still near its adapted value after the brief de-adaptation, so only the fast state has to recover. Savings is the clearest sign that behaviour returning to baseline does not mean memory returning to baseline.

<Callout kind="think">

Two processes fit the data, but they are not the only explanation. Savings can also arise from learning which context you are in (Heald, Lengyel and Wolpert, 2021, proposed a contextual inference model in which memories are kept separate and recalled when the context seems familiar), and from changes in the learning rate itself with experience. These accounts predict different behaviour when cues signal the context or when perturbations switch at different speeds, which is what experiments now test.

</Callout>
