<Step n={1} title="Hebb's postulate" minutes={20} />

Hebb (1949) wrote that when an axon of cell A "repeatedly or persistently takes part in firing" cell B, "some growth process or metabolic change takes place" so that A's efficiency in firing B increases. In a rate model with inputs $\mathbf x$ and output $y = \mathbf w\cdot\mathbf x$, the simplest version is

$$
\Delta \mathbf w = \eta\, y\,\mathbf x.
$$

Averaged over many inputs, $\langle y\mathbf x\rangle = \langle \mathbf x\mathbf x^\top\rangle\mathbf w = C\mathbf w$, where $C$ is the input correlation matrix. So, on average, $\dot{\mathbf w} = \eta C\mathbf w$: a linear system (Week 21) whose solution grows along the eigenvector of $C$ with the largest eigenvalue, without limit. A **covariance rule**, $\Delta\mathbf w = \eta (y - \bar y)(\mathbf x - \bar{\mathbf x})$ (Sejnowski, 1977), replaces correlations with covariances and allows depression as well as potentiation, but it is just as unstable.

Week 9 met one fix, Oja's rule, which keeps the weight vector at unit length and turns the neuron into a principal component analyser. Today compares it with another fix, **subtractive normalisation**, which keeps the sum of the weights fixed, and finds that the choice decides whether inputs cooperate or compete.

After 2,000 small steps the weight vector is more than $10^{62}$ times longer than it started, and it points along the top eigenvector of $C$, the uniform direction in which every input from both eyes has the same weight. Plain Hebbian learning finds the dominant correlation, which is useful, but it never stops growing, which is not: real synapses have limited resources, and something must trade one synapse's gain against another's loss.

<Step n={3} title="Competition by normalisation" minutes={40} />

Two ways to stop the growth. **Multiplicative** normalisation scales all weights together; Oja's rule, $\Delta\mathbf w = \eta(y\mathbf x - y^2\mathbf w)$, does this implicitly and, on average, converges to the top eigenvector of $C$. **Subtractive** normalisation subtracts the same amount from every weight's change, so the sum of weights stays fixed, and clips each weight between 0 and a maximum: $\Delta\mathbf w = \eta\,(C\mathbf w - \overline{C\mathbf w})$, then $\mathbf w \leftarrow \text{clip}(\mathbf w, 0, 1)$. Miller and MacKay (1994) showed that the two are not equivalent: subtractive normalisation makes inputs compete, so that groups of correlated inputs win or lose together.

The two constraints give opposite answers from the same correlations. Under subtractive normalisation the right eye's weights all reach the maximum of 1 and the left eye's all fall to 0: the eye that happened to start slightly ahead takes everything, which answers the prediction. Under Oja's rule both eyes keep equal weights of about 0.22, the principal component of a matrix in which the two eyes are symmetric. Which eye wins under subtractive normalisation depends on the tiny random differences in the starting weights, which is why neighbouring cortical neurons can end up preferring different eyes.

Learning from one noisy pattern at a time gives the same outcome: after 20,000 samples one eye's weights are near 1 and the other's near 0. The averaged equation describes the slow drift of the weights when the learning rate is small, while individual updates jitter around it. This is the same separation of time scales that lets stochastic gradient descent follow the gradient of the average loss.

<Callout kind="history">

Hubel and Wiesel found that closing one eye of a kitten during a critical period in early life shifts the ocular dominance of cortical neurons towards the open eye, and that artificially making both eyes' inputs uncorrelated (by inducing a squint) produces neurons driven almost entirely by one eye or the other. Both results follow from competitive Hebbian learning: deprivation gives the open eye's inputs more correlated drive, and decorrelating the eyes removes the common drive that would otherwise keep both. Miller, Keller and Stryker's model added lateral interactions in cortex and produced realistic stripes of ocular dominance columns.

</Callout>
