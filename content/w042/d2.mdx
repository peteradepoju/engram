<Step n={1} title="Timing matters" minutes={20} />

Markram, Lübke, Frotscher and Sakmann (1997) paired spikes in connected cortical neurons and found that potentiation required the presynaptic spike to come first. Bi and Poo (1998) mapped the dependence on timing in detail in cultured hippocampal neurons: with $\Delta t = t_{post} - t_{pre}$, the change in synaptic strength is positive and largest for small positive $\Delta t$, negative for small negative $\Delta t$, and decays to nothing beyond about 40 ms on either side. A standard model is the exponential **STDP window**:

$$
\Delta w(\Delta t) =
\begin{cases}
A_+ e^{-\Delta t/\tau_+}, & \Delta t \gt 0,\\
-A_- e^{\Delta t/\tau_-}, & \Delta t \lt 0,
\end{cases}
$$

with $\tau_\pm \approx 20$ ms. If presynaptic and postsynaptic spikes were unrelated, the average change would be proportional to the window's total area, $A_+\tau_+ - A_-\tau_-$; making this slightly negative, so that depression wins for random timing, keeps the rule stable.

The window is positive for pre-before-post timing and negative for the reverse, and its area is -0.030 by numerical integration, matching $A_+\tau_+ - A_-\tau_-$. With depression 30% stronger than potentiation, a synapse whose input fires independently of the neuron loses strength on average, so only inputs that help cause the neuron's spikes can grow. Bi and Poo also found that the size of the change depended on the synapse's initial strength, weak synapses potentiating more, an effect this additive rule ignores.

<Step n={3} title="Selecting correlated inputs" minutes={40} />

Song, Miller and Abbott (2000) gave a leaky integrate-and-fire neuron many Poisson inputs and let STDP act on all of them. Inputs that tend to fire together drive the neuron more reliably, so their spikes more often precede its spikes and are potentiated, while the rest are depressed. Implement STDP efficiently with **traces**: each input keeps a trace that jumps by 1 at each of its spikes and decays with $\tau_+$; the neuron keeps one that decays with $\tau_-$. At a postsynaptic spike every weight grows by $A_+$ times its input's trace; at a presynaptic spike that weight shrinks by $A_-$ times the postsynaptic trace.

After 100 seconds, the 50 inputs that share common events have weights near the maximum (about 0.99) and the 50 independent ones have fallen to about 0.27, many near zero: the neuron has picked out the correlated group from equally active noise. Its output rate settles near 19 Hz, regulated by the depression-dominated window: if the neuron fires too much, more random input spikes fall after its spikes and are depressed. Song, Miller and Abbott emphasised this self-stabilising competition, which needs no separate normalisation step.

<Step n={4} title="Firing earlier" minutes={20} />

When the same sequence of inputs repeats, STDP strengthens the inputs that arrive before the neuron fires and weakens those after. The neuron then reaches threshold sooner, which strengthens still earlier inputs: its spike moves forward until it sits near the start of the sequence. The neuron has learned to anticipate the pattern from its first few inputs, a simple form of prediction.

The neuron first fires 28 ms into the sequence; after 20 repetitions it fires at 10 ms and settles at 8 ms, which answers the prediction: earlier. The first five inputs have reached the maximum weight and the later ones have been depressed to zero: the neuron now responds as soon as the sequence begins. Mehta, Quirk and Wilson (2000) reported that hippocampal place fields shift backwards along a familiar route with experience, as this mechanism predicts.

<Callout kind="note">

Pair-based STDP is a simplification. Real plasticity depends on firing rate (at high rates, post-before-pre pairings can potentiate), on dendritic location, on the voltage and calcium at the synapse, and on spike triplets rather than pairs. Pfister and Gerstner's triplet rule (2006) and Clopath and colleagues' voltage-based rule (2010) reproduce much more of the data, and both connect STDP back to the BCM rule of tomorrow.

</Callout>
