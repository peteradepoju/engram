<Step n={1} title="The BCM rule" minutes={20} />

Bienenstock, Cooper and Munro (1982) wrote the change in each weight as the presynaptic activity times a function of the postsynaptic activity:

$$
\Delta w_i = \eta\, x_i\, \phi(y, \theta), \qquad \phi(y, \theta) = y\,(y - \theta).
$$

For $y$ below the **modification threshold** $\theta$, $\phi$ is negative and active synapses are depressed; above it, they are potentiated. The key is that $\theta$ is not fixed. It tracks the recent average of $y^2$:

$$
\tau_\theta\,\dot\theta = \frac{y^2}{y_0} - \theta.
$$

If the neuron has been very active, $\theta$ rises and potentiation becomes harder; if quiet, $\theta$ falls and potentiation becomes easier. Because $\theta$ grows faster than $y$ (as $y^2$), runaway activity always catches up with itself. Dudek and Bear (1992) later found the predicted pattern in hippocampus: low-frequency stimulation produced long-term depression, high-frequency stimulation potentiation, with a crossover in between.

The initial response is about 0.6 for either pattern. With the threshold at 0.4 that response lies above it, so every presentation potentiates, the response grows, $y(y-\theta)$ grows with it, and the weights run away (the loop stops them at a norm of 100). With the threshold at 1.0 the response lies below it, every presentation depresses, and the weights decay towards zero. A fixed threshold has only these two fates, and which one you get depends on where the neuron happens to start.

The neuron ends up responding about 2.0 to one pattern and about 0.2 to the other, with the weights concentrated on the synapses that carry the winning pattern. Nothing in the rule chose which pattern; a small random excess early on decided it, and the sliding threshold then turned the advantage into selectivity. Once the threshold sits near the mean of $y^2$, the strong response is above it and the weak one below, so each presentation of the winner potentiates and each presentation of the loser depresses, until the two effects balance. Run the cell with a different seed and the neuron may choose the other pattern.

<Step n={4} title="Metaplasticity" minutes={25} />

The sliding threshold predicts **metaplasticity**: the same stimulus can potentiate or depress a synapse depending on the neuron's recent history. After a long period of high activity, $\theta$ is high and a moderate response falls below it; after a quiet period, $\theta$ is low and the same response lies above it. Kirkwood, Rioult and Bear (1996) found the corresponding effect in visual cortex: in animals reared in the dark, the crossover between depression and potentiation shifted to lower stimulation frequencies, and light exposure shifted it back.

After the active history the threshold sits at about 2.25, so a test response of 0.8 depresses the synapse; after the quiet history it sits at about 0.09 and the same response potentiates. This is the BCM account of monocular deprivation: inputs from a closed eye still produce weak, uncorrelated activity, which falls below a threshold set by the open eye's strong drive, and so the closed eye's synapses weaken.

<Callout kind="think">

The BCM rule is a rate rule, but it can be derived from spike-based plasticity. Izhikevich and Desai (2003) and Pfister and Gerstner (2006) showed that a triplet STDP rule, averaged over Poisson spike trains, gives a BCM-like dependence on postsynaptic rate, with the threshold set by the rate of a slower process. The rules of Day 1, Day 2 and today are different views of one underlying synaptic machinery seen at different time scales.

</Callout>
