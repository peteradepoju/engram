<Step n={1} title="Activity sets the gain of every synapse" minutes={20} />

Turrigiano, Leslie, Desai, Rutherford and Nelson (1998) grew rat visual cortical neurons in culture and changed their activity for 48 hours. Tetrodotoxin (TTX), which blocks sodium channels and so silences firing, made the miniature excitatory postsynaptic currents (mEPSCs, the response to single vesicles) larger. Bicuculline, which blocks inhibition and raises firing, made them smaller. Firing rates, measured after the drug was washed out, had moved back towards their original level.

The striking result was the shape of the change. The whole distribution of mEPSC amplitudes was stretched by a single factor, as if each synapse's strength had been multiplied by the same number. This matters because a multiplicative change preserves the ratios between synapses, and so preserves the information stored in their relative strengths, while it rescales the total drive.

$$
w_i \leftarrow w_i \left(1 + \frac{r_\text{target} - \bar r}{r_\text{target}\,\tau_s}\right),
$$

where $\bar r$ is a running average of the neuron's rate and $\tau_s$ sets how fast scaling acts. Later work found the sensors: neurons track their own activity through calcium and change the number of AMPA receptors at every synapse (Turrigiano, 2008).

Cutting the input to 30% drops the mean rate from 5 Hz to 1.5 Hz. Scaling then multiplies every weight by about 3.3, exactly the factor that undoes the cut, and the rate returns to 5 Hz. Because one factor is applied to each neuron's whole row, the ratios between its weights are untouched: the neuron is as selective as before, only louder. Note that the scaling stops by itself, because the factor is 1 once the running rate reaches the target.

<Step n={3} title="Multiplicative or additive?" minutes={25} />

How did Turrigiano and colleagues know the change was multiplicative? They could not follow single synapses. Instead they recorded a few hundred mEPSCs in control and treated cells, sorted each set, and plotted the treated amplitudes against the control amplitudes of the same rank. If each synapse had been multiplied by $a$, the points fall on a line through the origin with slope $a$; if each had gained a fixed amount $b$, they fall on a line of slope 1 offset by $b$.

The multiplicative line fits the ranked points with an error of about 1.1 pA and recovers a slope of about 1.54, close to the true 1.5. The additive line, with its best offset of 8 pA, misses badly at both ends: it is too high for small events and too low for large ones, because a common multiplication stretches large synapses by more picoamperes than small ones. This is the shape Turrigiano and colleagues saw in their data.

<Callout kind="warning">

The rank-order method assumes every synapse changes in the same way. Kim, Tsien and Alger (2012) pointed out that small mEPSCs below the detection threshold distort the ranked plot and proposed a corrected test, and Hanes and colleagues (2020) found that the ratio of treated to control amplitudes changes across the distribution, so the scaling factor is not the same for every synapse. Treat "multiplicative" as a good first approximation, not a law.

</Callout>

<Step n={4} title="Hebb with homeostasis" minutes={35} />

Now put the two together. A neuron with 20 inputs learns by a covariance Hebbian rule, $\Delta w_i = \eta (x_i - \bar x_i) y$; the first ten inputs share a common signal. On its own the rule runs away. Add scaling, and vary its time constant.

Without homeostasis the rate passes 10,000 Hz within about 1,800 presentations. With slow scaling ($\tau_s = 1000$) the neuron survives, but its rate sits near 26 Hz, about five times the target, because Hebbian growth keeps outrunning the correction. Only when scaling acts within a few presentations ($\tau_s$ of 20 or 5) does the rate stay near 5 Hz. In every stable case the correlated inputs end up about ten times stronger than the independent ones: scaling did not undo learning, it only fixed the overall level.

<Callout kind="research">

Zenke, Gerstner and Ganguli (2017) called this the temporal paradox of Hebbian learning and homeostatic plasticity. In models, homeostasis must act within seconds to minutes to stabilise Hebbian learning, yet synaptic scaling in experiments takes hours to days. Their proposed resolution is that fast compensatory processes, such as heterosynaptic plasticity and the rate dependence built into triplet STDP or BCM, do the moment-to-moment stabilising, while slow scaling sets the long-run operating point. Which fast processes actually do this in cortex is still open.

</Callout>
