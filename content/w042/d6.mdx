**Part A: Hebbian PCA.** Oja (1982) showed that his normalised Hebbian rule, $\Delta\mathbf w = \eta y(\mathbf x - y\mathbf w)$, converges to the unit-length principal eigenvector of the input covariance. Sanger (1989) extended it to several outputs: with $\mathbf y = W\mathbf x$, the generalised Hebbian algorithm

$$
\Delta W = \eta\left(\mathbf y\mathbf x^\top - \operatorname{LT}(\mathbf y\mathbf y^\top)\,W\right),
$$

where $\operatorname{LT}$ keeps the lower triangle including the diagonal, makes output $k$ learn the $k$-th principal component. Train both on 20,000 samples from a three-dimensional Gaussian and compare with the eigenvectors.

Oja's neuron ends with a weight vector of length 1.00 whose direction matches the first principal component to four decimal places. Sanger's two outputs line up with the first and second components, with cross-overlaps of about 0.02. The lower-triangular term is what orders them: each output subtracts what the earlier outputs already explain, so output 2 learns the leading component of what is left. A purely local rule, one neuron at a time, has performed an eigendecomposition.

After normal rearing the neuron is selective for $\mathbf x_2$ and perfectly binocular: each eye contributes a total weight of 1.25, concentrated on the same synapses. After deprivation the closed eye's total falls to about 0.18 and the open eye's rises to about 2.1. The closed eye's noise still drives the neuron a little, but those weak responses fall below a threshold set by the open eye's strong drive, so they cause depression; the open eye then takes over to keep the response at the level the threshold demands. This is the ocular dominance shift Wiesel and Hubel found after lid suture, produced by a rule with no explicit competition between the eyes.

<Step n={3} title="Derivation set" minutes={10} />

<Prove title="1. Oja's rule converges to a unit vector" task="For Oja's rule averaged over inputs, $\dot{\mathbf w} = C\mathbf w - (\mathbf w^\top C\mathbf w)\mathbf w$, show that $\frac{d}{dt}|\mathbf w|^2 = 2(\mathbf w^\top C\mathbf w)(1 - |\mathbf w|^2)$ and conclude that the length tends to 1.">

$\frac{d}{dt}|\mathbf w|^2 = 2\mathbf w^\top\dot{\mathbf w} = 2\mathbf w^\top C\mathbf w - 2(\mathbf w^\top C\mathbf w)|\mathbf w|^2 = 2(\mathbf w^\top C\mathbf w)(1 - |\mathbf w|^2)$.

For positive definite $C$, $\mathbf w^\top C\mathbf w \gt 0$ whenever $\mathbf w \neq 0$. So $|\mathbf w|^2$ increases when it is below 1 and decreases when above 1: the unit sphere is attracting.

On the sphere the rule is $\dot{\mathbf w} = C\mathbf w - (\mathbf w^\top C\mathbf w)\mathbf w$, which is zero only at eigenvectors; a linear stability analysis shows only the eigenvector with the largest eigenvalue is stable. The neuron's output variance $\mathbf w^\top C\mathbf w$ is then maximised: Hebbian learning with this normalisation is PCA.

</Prove>

<Prove title="2. The fixed points of one-pattern BCM" task="For a single input pattern presented repeatedly, with $y = \mathbf w\cdot\mathbf x$ and $\theta = y^2$ at equilibrium (taking $y_0 = 1$), find the non-zero fixed point of $\dot{\mathbf w} = \eta\mathbf x y(y - \theta)$ and show it is stable.">

A fixed point needs $y(y - \theta) = 0$. With $\theta = y^2$, this is $y(y - y^2) = y^2(1 - y) = 0$, so $y = 0$ or $y = 1$.

Near $y = 1$, with the threshold at equilibrium, $\dot y = \mathbf x\cdot\dot{\mathbf w} = \eta|\mathbf x|^2 y^2(1 - y)$. If $y$ is slightly above 1, $\dot y \lt 0$; slightly below, $\dot y \gt 0$. So $y = 1$ is stable.

The response settles at a level set by $y_0$, not by the input's size, which is the sense in which the sliding threshold normalises without any explicit constraint on the weights. With several patterns the same argument, applied pattern by pattern, gives the selective fixed points of Day 3.

</Prove>
