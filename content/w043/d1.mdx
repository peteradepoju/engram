<Step n={1} title="A map in the hippocampus" minutes={15} />

O'Keefe and Dostrovsky's 1971 report was short: of 76 units recorded in dorsal CA1, eight responded only when the rat was in a particular part of the testing platform, facing in a particular way. O'Keefe and Nadel's 1978 book, <em>The Hippocampus as a Cognitive Map</em>, built a theory on it: the hippocampus holds an allocentric map, organised by places in the world rather than by the animal's body, in the spirit of Tolman's cognitive maps.

Fifty years on, a few facts are settled. In an open arena most CA1 and CA3 pyramidal cells have zero, one or a few place fields, each about 20 to 50 cm across in a rat. Fields form within minutes of entering a new environment and are stable for days. The spikes within a field are timed against the theta rhythm (phase precession, which you analysed with Fourier methods in Week 11), and a population of a few dozen cells is enough to decode position to within a few centimetres (Week 36).

<Step n={2} title="Rate maps" minutes={30} />

A rate map is spikes per bin divided by time per bin. Dividing by occupancy matters: a rat spends far more time in corners and along walls than in the middle, and a cell that fires at a constant rate would otherwise look like a corner cell.

The rat visits all but a bin or two in 20 minutes, and the estimated map correlates about 0.93 with the true field. The remaining error is Poisson noise, which is worst in rarely visited bins, where a single spike in a fraction of a second becomes a very high rate. Published analyses usually smooth the map with a Gaussian of one or two bins and drop bins with less than a fraction of a second of occupancy, trading resolution for noise.

<Step n={3} title="Spatial information and its bias" minutes={35} />

How much does a spike tell you about where the rat is? Skaggs, McNaughton, Gothard and Markus (1993) proposed

$$
I = \sum_i p_i\,\frac{\lambda_i}{\bar\lambda}\log_2\frac{\lambda_i}{\bar\lambda}\quad\text{bits per spike},
$$

where $p_i$ is the fraction of time in bin $i$, $\lambda_i$ the rate there and $\bar\lambda = \sum_i p_i\lambda_i$ the mean rate. It is the mutual information between spike and position (Week 35), per spike, in the limit of short time bins. A cell that fires in one bin out of $K$ equally visited bins carries $\log_2 K$ bits per spike.

Estimated from finite data it is biased upwards: noise makes the measured map uneven even when the true map is flat. The standard remedy is a shuffle. Shift the whole spike train in time by a random amount, wrapping round the end, so that its relation to position is broken while its own temporal structure is kept; recompute the information many times; and call the cell spatial only if its real value beats, say, 99% of the shuffles.

  ok = occ > 0

  def skaggs(m):
      p = occ[ok] / occ[ok].sum(); lam_i = m[ok]
      lam_bar = (p * lam_i).sum()
      with np.errstate(divide="ignore", invalid="ignore"):

The place cell carries about 4 bits per spike, far above its shuffles, which reach about 2.2: the shuffled place cell still looks spatial because the rat moves slowly and the shifted spike bursts still land in clusters. The flat cell, whose true information is zero, measures about 0.12 bits per spike. That is the bias, and it is the same size as its shuffles, so the test correctly refuses to call it spatial. Without the shuffle you could not tell 0.12 bits of real tuning from 0.12 bits of noise.

<Callout kind="warning">

The shuffle answers "is this cell spatial at all?", not "how spatial is it?". With long sessions almost any cell with a weak spatial modulation passes, and the proportion of "place cells" reported in a study depends heavily on the threshold, the bin size, the speed filter (most studies drop periods when the rat is still) and the session length. When you compare place-cell proportions across papers, check these choices first.

</Callout>

<Step n={4} title="Remapping" minutes={25} />

Put the rat in a different room and the map changes completely. Cells that had fields lose them, silent cells gain fields, and the fields that remain move to unrelated positions. This global remapping (Muller and Kubie, 1987) means the hippocampus gives each environment its own nearly independent code. Smaller changes, such as the colour of the walls, can instead change firing rates while leaving fields in place, which is called rate remapping (Leutgeb and colleagues, 2005).
