<Step n={1} title="A lattice in the entorhinal cortex" minutes={15} />

Hafting and colleagues found that cells in layer II of dorsocaudal medial entorhinal cortex fired at the vertices of an equilateral triangular grid. Each cell's grid is described by three numbers: its **spacing** (distance between neighbouring fields, from about 30 cm dorsally to several metres ventrally), its **orientation**, and its **phase** (the offset of the lattice). Neighbouring cells share spacing and orientation but have different phases, so together they tile the floor. Later work (Stensola and colleagues, 2012) showed that spacings are organised in a few discrete **modules**, with each module's spacing about 1.4 times the previous one.

Grid patterns survive darkness and persist when the arena is changed, suggesting the cells compute position from the animal's own movement, a process called path integration, with landmarks used to correct accumulated error. Head-direction cells and speed cells in the same region supply the inputs that such a computation would need.

<Step n={2} title="Building and scoring a grid" minutes={35} />

The sum of three cosine gratings whose wave vectors are 60 degrees apart has hexagonal symmetry. With wave number $k = 4\pi/(\sqrt3\,\lambda)$ the peaks are spaced $\lambda$ apart:

$$
g(\mathbf x) = \sum_{j=0}^{2}\cos\!\big(\mathbf k_j\cdot(\mathbf x - \mathbf c)\big),\qquad \mathbf k_j = k\,(\cos(\theta + j\pi/3),\ \sin(\theta + j\pi/3)).
$$

To score real data, the convention (Sargolini and colleagues, 2006) is to compute the map's spatial autocorrelogram, rotate it, and compare: a hexagonal pattern matches itself at 60 and 120 degrees and mismatches at 30, 90 and 150 degrees. **Gridness** is the smaller of the 60 and 120 degree correlations minus the largest of the other three.

The hexagonal map scores about 1.26 and barely changes with noise of 30% of its peak, because the autocorrelogram averages over many fields. A single place field scores zero: its autocorrelogram is a central blob that looks the same at every rotation. A square lattice scores about -1.2, since it matches itself at 90 degrees and not at 60. In real recordings, a cell is usually called a grid cell if its gridness exceeds the 95th or 99th percentile of shuffled data, the same logic as Day 1's spatial information test.

<Step n={3} title="A modular code for position" minutes={30} />

A single grid module is ambiguous: positions one spacing apart look identical. But the phases of several modules with different spacings, taken together, are unique over a range far larger than any one spacing, much as the remainders of a number divided by 3, 5 and 7 identify it uniquely up to 105. Fiete, Burak and Brookings (2008) argued that this gives grid cells an enormous coding range. The price is fragility: a small error in one module's phase can make the decoder jump to a completely different position.

Without noise, three modules with spacings under 60 cm locate the rat to within a fraction of a millimetre anywhere on a 10 m track. With phase noise of 2% of a cycle the median error is a few millimetres and almost no decode goes badly wrong; at 5% the typical error is still about a centimetre, but over a third of decodes land on the wrong position entirely, often metres away. The range of a modular code comes at the cost of this all-or-nothing failure, and Sreenivasan and Fiete (2011) argued that a suitable readout, possibly in the hippocampus, can correct such errors by allowing only consistent combinations of phases.

<Step n={4} title="Path integration" minutes={25} />

If the grid is driven by velocity, its estimate of position is the integral of noisy velocity measurements. Independent errors add, so their variance grows linearly in time and the typical error like $\sqrt t$, which is the signature of a random walk (Week 24).

  dt, T = 0.02, 3000                                    # 60 s
  v = 0.2 * r.normal(size=(T, 2)); x_true = np.cumsum(v * dt, 0)

  def integrate(reset_every=None, noise=0.05):
      est = np.zeros(2); out = np.zeros((T, 2))
      vm = v + noise * r.normal(size=v.shape)
      for t in range(T):
          est = est + vm[t] * dt
          if reset_every and (t + 1) % reset_every == 0:
              est = x_true[t].copy()
          out[t] = est
      return out

The error grows from about 2 cm at 5 s to 4.4 cm at 20 s and 7.8 cm at 60 s: quadrupling the time doubles the error. Correcting against a landmark every 10 s caps it at the error reached in 10 s, about 3 cm. Real animals show this drift: in darkness, grid patterns slowly lose their alignment, and Hardcastle, Ganguli and Giocomo (2015) found that grid errors grew with time since the mouse last touched a wall and were corrected at boundaries.

<Callout kind="research">

Grid-like units emerge in recurrent networks trained to path integrate (Banino and colleagues, 2018; Cueva and Wei, 2018), but Schaeffer, Khona and Fiete (2022) showed that whether they appear depends sharply on choices such as the readout and the activation function. Whether trained networks explain why the brain has grid cells, or only show that grids are one of several solutions, is an active debate.
