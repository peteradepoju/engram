<Step n={1} title="Head-direction cells" minutes={15} />

Ranck discovered head-direction cells in the rat's dorsal presubiculum in 1984, and Taube, Muller and Ranck described them in detail in 1990. Each cell has a preferred direction and a tuning curve about 90 degrees wide; together their preferred directions cover the circle. Their activity follows head turns with a small anticipatory lead, persists in darkness (with slow drift), and rotates as a whole when a landmark is rotated: the cells keep their relationships with each other while their common reference changes.

These properties suggested a network whose activity can sit at any angle and stay there, a **continuous attractor**. In the fruit fly central complex, Seelig and Jayaraman (2015) imaged the ellipsoid body, a physical ring of neurons, and saw a single bump of activity that rotated as the fly turned, even in darkness. Kim and colleagues (2017) then showed by stimulating it that the bump could be moved and that only one bump could exist at a time, as attractor models predict.

<Step n={2} title="A bump that stays" minutes={35} />

Take $N$ rate neurons with preferred angles $\theta_i$ around a ring and connections that depend only on the difference of angles:

$$
\tau\dot u_i = -u_i + \sum_j W_{ij}\,f(u_j) + I_i, \qquad W_{ij} = \frac{1}{N}\big(J_0 + J_1\cos(\theta_i - \theta_j)\big),
$$

with $f$ a rectified, saturating function. Nearby neurons excite each other ($J_1 \gt 0$) and global inhibition ($J_0 \lt 0$) keeps activity local. If $J_1$ is large enough, a bump of activity can sustain itself without input. Because $W$ depends only on angle differences, if a bump centred at $\phi$ is a steady state, so is the same bump rotated to any other angle: the steady states form a ring, a continuum of fixed points. This is the two-dimensional cousin of the line attractor you met in Week 6.

The cue creates a bump centred on 1 rad, and 1700 steps after the cue is removed it is still there, within 0.005 rad of where it started, with 41 of the 100 neurons active. Nothing in the network marks 1 rad as special; cue it at any other angle and the bump stays there instead. Lower $J_1$ below about 2 and the bump decays once the cue is gone: recurrent excitation must be strong enough to replace the input, the same condition as the line attractor's eigenvalue of exactly one.

<Step n={3} title="Turning the bump" minutes={25} />

To follow head turns, the bump must move at a speed proportional to angular velocity. Zhang's solution was to add an odd component to the connections, $\gamma\sin(\theta_i - \theta_j)$, whose size is set by the angular velocity signal. Each neuron then excites its neighbours on one side more than on the other, and the bump slides towards that side. In the fly, a separate set of neurons, the P-EN neurons, carry angular velocity and project to the ring with a shift in one direction or the other, which is an anatomical version of the same idea (Turner-Evans and colleagues, 2017; Green and colleagues, 2017).

Without asymmetry the bump stays put; with $\gamma$ of 0.5, 1 and 2 it turns at about 0.083, 0.166 and 0.331 radians per unit time, proportional to $\gamma$ to within a fraction of a percent. A linear map from angular velocity to $\gamma$ therefore gives a bump that tracks heading, which is integration of angular velocity. Any error in that gain makes the internal compass turn too fast or too slow, and in flies and rodents the gain is recalibrated by visual landmarks.

<Step n={4} title="Drift and imperfection" minutes={30} />

A continuous attractor needs perfect symmetry. Any heterogeneity, in connection strengths or in neurons' excitability, makes some bump positions slightly more stable than others, and the bump slowly slides into these few preferred positions. Noise, meanwhile, makes it diffuse. Both effects limit how long a ring can remember a heading without input, and both have been measured: head-direction estimates in the dark drift, and working-memory reports of remembered angles show both diffusion and attraction towards particular angles (Week 46).

Ten starting angles end at only five places, and five of the ten bumps end within a hundredth of a radian of 0.56 rad, having travelled up to 1.3 rad to get there. Connection errors of a few percent have turned the ring of fixed points into a handful of discrete attractors joined by slow channels. The bumps that started near one of the stable points barely moved, which is why drift can look small in short experiments and large in long ones. In real circuits, this kind of drift is one reason head-direction estimates need constant correction by landmarks.

<Callout kind="research">

Ring attractors are the best-supported continuous attractors in any brain, but how they are wired is still being worked out. In the fly, the connectome (Hulse and colleagues, 2021) lets models be built from measured synapses rather than assumed cosines. In mammals, Chaudhuri, Gerçek, Pandey, Peyrache and Fiete (2019) found that the joint activity of head-direction neurons lies on a one-dimensional ring even during sleep, when there is no heading to track, which is strong evidence that the ring is a property of the network itself.

</Callout>
