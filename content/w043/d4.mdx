<Step n={1} title="A circuit with two jobs" minutes={20} />

Information enters the hippocampus from the entorhinal cortex. It reaches the dentate gyrus, whose roughly one million granule cells in a rat outnumber their entorhinal inputs about five to one and are very sparsely active, with perhaps 2 to 5% firing in any environment. The granule cells project to CA3 through the mossy fibres, few but powerful synapses that can act as "detonators". CA3 pyramidal cells are richly connected to each other by recurrent collaterals, and also receive direct entorhinal input. CA3 projects to CA1, which projects back to the entorhinal cortex.

Marr (1971) proposed that a recurrent network like CA3 can store patterns in its connections and retrieve a whole pattern from part of it, which is the Hopfield idea of Weeks 6 and 22 applied to a real circuit. McNaughton and Morris (1987) and Treves and Rolls (1994) added that, for this to work with many similar memories, the patterns must first be made dissimilar, and that the dentate gyrus with its expansion and sparse firing is suited to that. Leutgeb and colleagues (2007) recorded both regions as rats explored arenas morphed in small steps between a square and a circle. Even the smallest change of shape produced clear changes in the activity of dentate granule cells, as separation predicts, while CA3 ensembles changed less for small differences and more as the shapes became distinct.

Every input overlap comes out lower: 0.9 becomes about 0.55, 0.7 becomes 0.33 and 0.3 becomes 0.09. Two things do the work. The random expansion spreads each input over many granule cells, and the competition is a threshold: a granule cell that was just inside the top 40 for one input can fall just outside for a similar one. Because only 2% of cells survive, these small shifts change a large fraction of the winners. Make the output denser in the figure and the separation weakens.

<Step n={3} title="Completion in CA3" minutes={30} />

Now store $M$ random sparse patterns $\boldsymbol\xi^\mu$ in a recurrent network of 1,000 CA3 cells, each pattern with a fraction $a$ of cells active. With binary patterns that are mostly zeros, the plain Hebbian outer product is dominated by the mean; the covariance rule subtracts it:

$$
W_{ij} = \sum_{\mu}(\xi^\mu_i - a)(\xi^\mu_j - a), \qquad W_{ii} = 0.
$$

Recall iterates $\mathbf s \leftarrow \mathrm{kWTA}(W\mathbf s)$, keeping the activity level fixed at $aN$ as inhibition would. Cue the network with a pattern from which 70% of the active cells have been removed.

  N = 1000

  def store(P, a):
      W = (P - a).T @ (P - a)
      np.fill_diagonal(W, 0)
      return W

  def recall(W, cue, k, steps=10):
      s = cue.copy()
      for _ in range(steps):
          s = kwta(W @ s, k)
      return s

The cue keeps only 30% of the pattern's active cells, an overlap of about 0.55, and the network restores the full pattern exactly. Each correct cell in the cue votes, through the stored weights, for the other cells of its pattern; after kWTA the most supported cells are exactly the missing ones, and a few iterations clean up the rest. This is the behaviour Nakazawa and colleagues (2002) tested in mice lacking NMDA receptors in CA3: the mice learned a water maze normally but, unlike controls, could not find the platform when most of the room's cues were removed.

<Step n={4} title="Why sparse codes store more" minutes={25} />

The classic Hopfield network with dense patterns ($a = 0.5$) stores about $0.14N$ patterns. Theory for sparse patterns with the covariance rule gives a capacity that grows as the code gets sparser, roughly as $N/(a\,|\ln a|)$ (Tsodyks and Feigel'man, 1988; Treves and Rolls, 1991), because sparse patterns overlap less and so interfere less. Test it.

With 5% of cells active, 400 patterns in 1,000 neurons are all recalled perfectly, and the theoretical capacity is far higher. With half the cells active, 50 patterns are fine but 200, which is $0.2N$, pushes the network past its capacity of about $0.14N$ and recall falls to an overlap of about 0.7. Sparseness is not free: each sparse pattern carries less information, and very sparse codes need precise inhibition to hold the activity level. But for a structure that must store many episodes, the trade is a good one.

<Callout kind="think">

Separation and completion conflict. A network that completes strongly will merge a genuinely new but similar experience into an old memory; one that separates strongly will fail to recognise an old place seen from a new angle. Where should a memory system set the balance, and should the balance depend on how familiar or how important the situation is? Some theories give acetylcholine this role, switching the hippocampus between encoding and retrieval (Hasselmo, 2006).

</Callout>
