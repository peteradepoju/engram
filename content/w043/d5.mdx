<Step n={1} title="The puzzle of consolidation" minutes={20} />

Scoville and Milner (1957) described H.M.'s profound anterograde amnesia after bilateral medial temporal lobe removal. Later studies of patients and animals with hippocampal damage found that retrograde amnesia is often temporally graded: recent memories are lost and remote ones spared. In rats, Kim and Fanselow (1992) found that hippocampal lesions one day after fear conditioning abolished the context fear memory, while lesions four weeks later left it intact. This pattern defines **systems consolidation**: a memory is initially supported by the hippocampus and over time comes to depend on the neocortex.

The **complementary learning systems** theory of McClelland, McNaughton and O'Reilly (1995) explains why two systems exist. Neocortex extracts the shared structure of many experiences, which requires distributed, overlapping representations and slow learning. Overlapping representations make a network vulnerable to interference: learning new items quickly disrupts old ones. The hippocampus, with its sparse, separated codes (Day 4), can learn an episode in one shot without interference. If it then replays recent episodes, interleaved with the neocortex's existing knowledge, the neocortex can absorb new information slowly without destroying what it already knows.

Wilson and McNaughton (1994) found the replay. Place cells that fired together while a rat explored also fired together during subsequent sleep, and later work showed replay of whole trajectories in compressed time during sharp-wave ripples (Week 47).

After training on A the network is perfect on A. After training on B it is perfect on B, and its error on A is 0.83, almost the 0.89 of a network that never learned A at all. Every B update changes the weights in directions that A's inputs also use, and nothing pushes back. Trained on all twenty items together from the start, the same network reaches errors of about 0.05 and 0.03 on the two sets in the same number of epochs. The capacity was there; the order of training destroyed it. McCloskey and Cohen (1989) and Ratcliff (1990) first described this in connectionist networks.

Replaying one A item after one in ten B presentations cuts the error on A from 0.83 to about 0.23, while B is still learned almost perfectly; replaying after every B item brings the error on A down to 0.05, as good as full interleaving. A modest amount of replay from a store that holds the old items goes a long way. In the theory, that store is the hippocampus and the replay happens during sleep and quiet rest, when sharp-wave ripples carry compressed sequences of recent experience.

<Step n={4} title="The gradient of amnesia" minutes={25} />

A minimal two-store model captures the temporal gradient. A new memory is laid down at strength 1 in the hippocampus, which decays with time constant $\tau_h$. While the hippocampal trace lasts, replay strengthens a neocortical trace $c$ towards 1:

$$
\dot h = -h/\tau_h, \qquad \dot c = k\,h\,(1 - c).
$$

Retrieval succeeds with strength $1 - (1 - h)(1 - c)$ when both stores are available. A hippocampal lesion at time $t$ leaves only $c(t)$.

  days = np.arange(0, 100 + dt_d / 2, dt_d)

A lesion one day after learning leaves a memory of strength 0.25; at ten days 0.85; at fifty days 0.95, which is as strong as it will get. This is the temporal gradient seen after hippocampal damage. The intact memory stays strong throughout, because the hippocampal trace fades only as the neocortical one takes over. The model has only two parameters, and fitting them to lesion data from different species gives very different time scales, from days in rodents to years in humans, which the theory attributes to different rates of neocortical learning.

<Callout kind="history">

The standard model of consolidation is not the only view. Nadel and Moscovitch (1997) proposed multiple trace theory: detailed episodic memories always depend on the hippocampus, however old, while only gist and semantic knowledge become independent of it. Evidence that some remote autobiographical memories are lost after hippocampal damage supports them. Later versions of complementary learning systems theory (Kumaran, Hassabis and McClelland, 2016) also allow fast neocortical learning when new information fits an existing schema, as Tse and colleagues (2007) showed in rats.

</Callout>
