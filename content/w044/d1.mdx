<Step n={1} title="Conditioning" minutes={15} />

In classical (Pavlovian) conditioning a neutral **conditioned stimulus** (CS), such as a tone, is followed by an **unconditioned stimulus** (US), such as food, that produces a response by itself. After pairings, the CS alone produces a conditioned response. In instrumental conditioning the animal's own action is followed by the outcome; that is the subject of Days 3 and 4.

Kamin's blocking experiment (1968) is the key result. Rats first learned that a noise predicted a shock. Then noise and light together were followed by the same shock. Tested alone, the light produced almost no fear. Pairing was not enough: the light added no new information, and so it was not learned. Rescorla (1968) showed a related point from the other side: a tone paired with shock is not learned if shocks are just as likely without the tone. Animals learn about **contingency** and **surprise**, not co-occurrence.

<Step n={2} title="The Rescorla-Wagner rule" minutes={30} />

Let each stimulus $i$ have an associative strength $V_i$. On a trial with stimuli present ($x_i = 1$) and outcome $r$, the animal's prediction is the sum of the strengths of the stimuli present, and every present stimulus changes in proportion to the shared error:

$$
\delta = r - \sum_i V_i x_i, \qquad \Delta V_i = \alpha\, \delta\, x_i.
$$

Because the error is shared, stimuli compete for the available prediction. This is the delta rule of Widrow and Hoff (1960), and it is gradient descent on $\tfrac12\delta^2$, the same learning rule as the linear network of Week 43 and, with nonlinear units, the root of backpropagation.

The first rewarded trial raises $V$ by exactly $\alpha = 0.2$, the next by $0.2 \times 0.8 = 0.16$, and so on: learning is fast when the outcome is surprising and slows as it becomes expected, giving the negatively accelerated curve seen in conditioning experiments. Extinction is the same process in reverse, driven by negative errors when the food fails to arrive.

After pretraining, the light predicts food perfectly, the error on compound trials is zero, and the tone learns nothing: blocking. Without pretraining, the two cues split the prediction between them. Overexpectation is the most striking prediction because it is counter-intuitive: two cues that each predict food perfectly, rewarded together on every trial, both get weaker, because together they predict two units of food and only one arrives. Rescorla confirmed this in rats, a success for a model that was built before the experiment was done.

<Step n={4} title="Limits of the model" minutes={25} />

Rescorla-Wagner explains a great deal with one line, which is why it is still the starting point. But several findings fall outside it.

- **Latent inhibition.** Pre-exposing a CS without any outcome slows later learning about it. In the model nothing happens on those trials ($\delta = 0$ when $V = 0$ and $r = 0$), so pre-exposure should have no effect. Models in which attention to a cue changes with experience (Mackintosh, 1975; Pearce and Hall, 1980) explain it.
- **Extinction is not unlearning.** After extinction, the response comes back with time (spontaneous recovery), in a different context (renewal) or after an unsignalled US (reinstatement). In the model, extinction drives $V$ back to zero and the memory is gone. Bouton's work showed that extinction is new learning, specific to context, laid over the old association. The two-rate model of Week 41 is one way to get spontaneous recovery.
- **Timing within a trial.** The model treats a trial as a single event. It cannot say when within a trial the US is expected, or explain second-order conditioning, in which a cue paired with another predictive cue becomes predictive itself. Tomorrow's temporal difference model fixes both.

<li>Learning is driven by prediction error, not by pairing.</li>
