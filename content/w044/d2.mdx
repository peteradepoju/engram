<Step n={1} title="Schultz's recordings" minutes={15} />

Dopamine neurons in the ventral tegmental area and substantia nigra pars compacta fire tonically at a few spikes per second and respond to rewards with short bursts. Schultz and colleagues trained monkeys in simple tasks and found three properties. The neurons respond to unpredicted rewards; after learning, they respond to the earliest reliable predictor of reward instead; and when a predicted reward is omitted, their firing drops below baseline at the expected time. The response depends on the difference between what was received and what was expected, and on when it was expected.

Later work made the case quantitative. Fiorillo, Tobler and Schultz (2003) varied reward probability: the response to the cue grew with probability while the response to the reward shrank. Bayer and Glimcher (2005) showed that firing tracks a graded positive prediction error, and Eshel and colleagues (2015) found that dopamine neurons in mice subtract expected reward from actual reward in a remarkably uniform way.

<Step n={2} title="Value and the TD error" minutes={30} />

Divide a trial into time steps. The **value** of the state at time $t$ is the expected sum of future rewards, discounted by $\gamma$ per step:

$$
V(t) = \mathbb E\big[r_{t+1} + \gamma r_{t+2} + \gamma^2 r_{t+3} + \cdots\big] = \mathbb E\big[r_{t+1} + \gamma V(t+1)\big].
$$

The second form, the Bellman equation, says that a good prediction now must agree with the reward that arrives next plus the prediction made one step later. The TD error measures the disagreement:

$$
\delta_t = r_{t+1} + \gamma V(t+1) - V(t),
$$

and learning moves the prediction towards removing it: $V(t) \leftarrow V(t) + \alpha\,\delta_t$. Unlike Rescorla-Wagner, the target includes a prediction, $V(t+1)$, so errors can arise from changes in expectation as well as from rewards. That is what lets a cue become rewarding.

To represent time since the cue, use the **complete serial compound**: after the cue, a different feature is active at each time step, so each step has its own weight. Before the cue nothing is active and the value is zero, because the cue's timing is unpredictable.

On the first trial the only error is at the reward, of size 1, exactly as in Rescorla-Wagner. Over training, the error creeps backwards one step per trial or so, because each step's value is learned from the next step's, until it reaches the cue. There it stops: the step before the cue has no feature and so no value, and the cue's arrival is a jump in expectation from 0 to 1. After training the error at the cue is about 1 and at the reward about 0. Recordings have since found this gradual backward shift in some conditions (Amo and colleagues, 2022), though in many data sets the response seems to move to the cue without passing through the intermediate times, which is one argument for richer timing representations.

Omitting the reward leaves the cue burst unchanged and produces an error of -1 at the time the reward was due: the dip in Schultz's third panel. The model knows when the reward should have come because each time step has its own learned value. Delivering the reward 5 steps early gives a burst at the early time, which is what Hollerman and Schultz (1998) found. But the model also predicts a dip at the usual time, and the neurons showed none: once the reward has arrived, real animals do not keep expecting it. A state representation that knows the reward has already happened, such as a belief state over hidden task states (Daw, Courville and Touretzky, 2006; Starkweather and colleagues, 2017), removes the false dip.

<Step n={4} title="Discounting and probability" minutes={25} />

Two parameters shape the model's predictions. The discount factor $\gamma$ makes the value ramp up towards the reward: a cue far from the reward is worth less. And if the reward arrives with probability $p$, the learned value at the cue becomes $p$, so the cue response grows with $p$ while the reward response, $1 - p$, shrinks, as Fiorillo and colleagues found.

The cue response comes out near $p$ (0.20, 0.47 and 0.74 for $p$ of 0.25, 0.5 and 0.75) and the response to a delivered reward near $1 - p$, the pattern Fiorillo and colleagues reported. The small shortfalls are the cost of a constant learning rate, which keeps the value fluctuating trial by trial around $p$. Fiorillo and colleagues also found a slow ramp of activity between cue and reward that was largest at $p = 0.5$, where uncertainty is greatest; whether that ramp reflects uncertainty or is an artefact of averaging TD errors has been debated (Niv, Duff and Dayan, 2005).

<Callout kind="research">

The TD account is powerful but contested in its details. Dopamine concentration in the striatum ramps up as rats approach a goal (Howe and colleagues, 2013), which a simple TD error does not predict but which may reflect value or a TD error with particular state representations (Kim and colleagues, 2020). Dopamine neurons also respond to novel and salient non-rewarding events, and some subpopulations encode movement or threat. And causal manipulations now test the theory directly: optogenetically stimulating dopamine neurons at the time of an expected reward can unblock learning, as a prediction error should (Steinberg and colleagues, 2013).

</Callout>

<li>Value is expected discounted future reward, defined recursively by the Bellman equation.</li>

<li>The fit is close but not complete: ramps, novelty and heterogeneity remain.</li>
