<Step n={1} title="The exploration-exploitation dilemma" minutes={15} />

An agent faces $K$ actions with unknown mean rewards $\mu_k$. It keeps an estimate $Q_k$ for each, updated after each pull by the incremental mean or by a Rescorla-Wagner rule with learning rate $\alpha$:

$$
Q_a \leftarrow Q_a + \alpha\,(r - Q_a).
$$

How should it choose? Performance is measured by **regret**, the reward lost by not always pulling the best arm: $\sum_t (\mu^* - \mu_{a_t})$. A good strategy has regret that grows more and more slowly. Lai and Robbins (1985) proved that no strategy can do better than regret growing like $\log t$.

Humans and animals explore in at least two ways (Wilson and colleagues, 2014): randomly, by adding noise to choices, and in a directed way, by preferring options they know less about. Today's strategies cover both.

<Step n={2} title="Four strategies" minutes={35} />

- **Greedy**: always choose $\arg\max_k Q_k$.
- **Epsilon-greedy**: with probability $\varepsilon$ choose at random, otherwise greedily.
- **Softmax**: choose $k$ with probability $\propto e^{\beta Q_k}$, where the inverse temperature $\beta$ sets how deterministic choice is.
- **Upper confidence bound (UCB)**: choose $\arg\max_k \big(Q_k + c\sqrt{\ln t / N_k}\big)$, an optimistic estimate that adds a bonus for arms tried few times ($N_k$).

Greedy choice loses about 0.55 per step even after 1,000 steps: it settles on whichever arm first looked better than zero and never checks the others. Epsilon-greedy keeps losing a little forever because it explores at a constant rate even when it knows the answer; softmax does slightly better because its exploration favours arms that are nearly as good. UCB's regret per step falls to about 0.01, and its total regret is a quarter of softmax's: it explores where exploring is informative and stops once the bonus shrinks. Optimism in the face of uncertainty is the simplest form of directed exploration.

<Step n={3} title="The likelihood of choices" minutes={30} />

To use these models as theories of a person or an animal, turn them around. Given a sequence of choices $a_1, \dots, a_T$ and rewards $r_1, \dots, r_T$, and a model with parameters $\theta = (\alpha, \beta)$, run the model along the observed sequence, updating $Q$ with the rewards that were actually received, and add up the log probabilities it assigned to the choices that were actually made:

$$
\log p(a_{1:T} \mid r_{1:T}, \theta) = \sum_t \log \frac{e^{\beta Q_{a_t}(t)}}{\sum_k e^{\beta Q_k(t)}}.
$$

The best-fitting parameters maximise this. This is the workhorse of model-based analysis of behaviour (Week 50) and of model-based fMRI, in which the fitted model's prediction errors are used as regressors, as Daw and colleagues did.

Here a simulated participant plays a two-armed bandit whose arms pay off with probability 0.7 and 0.3, swapped halfway through (a reversal), learning with $\alpha = 0.3$ and choosing by softmax with $\beta = 5$.

The true parameters give a negative log-likelihood of about 103, half that of a random chooser, and the grid minimum lies at $\alpha = 0.25$, $\beta = 5.5$, close to the truth. The contour plot shows the shape that makes fitting hard: a diagonal valley along which a higher learning rate can be traded for a lower inverse temperature with little change in likelihood. Choices that look noisy can be explained either by noisy choice or by fast, jumpy learning, and 300 trials only partly separate the two.
