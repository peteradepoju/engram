<Step n={1} title="Selecting actions in the basal ganglia" minutes={20} />

The striatum receives input from almost the whole cortex and projects through two routes to the output nuclei, the internal globus pallidus and substantia nigra pars reticulata, which tonically inhibit the thalamus. The **direct pathway** (D1 striatal neurons) inhibits the output nuclei and so releases the thalamus: it promotes the selected action. The **indirect pathway** (D2 neurons, via the external globus pallidus and subthalamic nucleus) has the opposite net effect: it suppresses actions. Dopamine excites D1 neurons and inhibits D2 neurons, and it gates plasticity in both, in opposite directions (Shen and colleagues, 2008).

Frank's model (2004, 2005) turned this into a learning rule. A dopamine burst after a good outcome strengthens the Go connections for the chosen action and weakens its NoGo connections; a dip after a bad outcome does the reverse. In the probabilistic selection task (Frank, Seeberger and O'Reilly, 2004), participants learn which symbol in each of three pairs is rewarded more often, then face new pairings. Patients with Parkinson's disease tested off their medication were better at avoiding the worst symbol than choosing the best; on medication, the pattern reversed. Learning from rewards and learning from punishments depend on dopamine in opposite ways.

<Step n={2} title="Q-learning" minutes={30} />

In a **Markov decision process**, an agent in state $s$ takes action $a$, receives reward $r$ and moves to state $s'$. Q-learning learns the value of each action in each state, using a TD error whose target assumes the best action will be taken next:

$$
\delta = r + \gamma\max_{a'}Q(s', a') - Q(s, a), \qquad Q(s, a) \leftarrow Q(s, a) + \alpha\,\delta.
$$

The world here is a 5 by 5 grid. The agent starts in one corner, moves up, down, left or right, and receives 1 on reaching the opposite corner, which ends the episode.

<Step n={3} title="Actor and critic" minutes={30} />

Q-learning stores a value for every action. An **actor-critic** splits the job. A critic learns state values $V(s)$ with the ordinary TD error $\delta = r + \gamma V(s') - V(s)$. An actor keeps action preferences $H(s, a)$, chooses by softmax over them, and nudges the preference for the action it took by the same $\delta$: if things turned out better than expected, do that again.

Barto (1995) and Houk, Adams and Barto (1995) proposed that the ventral striatum is the critic and the dorsal striatum the actor, with dopamine broadcasting the shared TD error to both. O'Doherty and colleagues (2004) found support in humans: ventral striatum responded to prediction errors whether or not a choice was required, while dorsal striatum responded only when the participant's actions mattered.

The actor-critic starts worse, with some episodes hitting the 200-step limit, because its initial policy is uniform and it has no separate value per action to guide it. But it ends at about 9 steps, as good as Q-learning. The two algorithms make different commitments: Q-learning needs a value for every state-action pair and can learn about the best action while doing something else (off-policy); actor-critic learns only from what it does (on-policy), but its policy can be anything the actor can represent, which is what makes it the basis of most methods for continuous actions.

<Step n={4} title="Learning from gains and losses" minutes={25} />

If positive and negative prediction errors drive learning through different pathways, their effective learning rates can differ, and dopamine levels or drugs can shift the balance. With rate $\alpha^+$ after positive errors and $\alpha^-$ after negative ones, a value learned for an option that pays 1 with probability $p$ settles where the expected upward and downward steps balance:

$$
p\,\alpha^+(1 - V) = (1 - p)\,\alpha^- V \quad\Rightarrow\quad V = \frac{p\,\alpha^+}{p\,\alpha^+ + (1 - p)\,\alpha^-}.
$$

When $\alpha^+ \gt \alpha^-$ the learner is optimistic; when $\alpha^- \gt \alpha^+$, pessimistic. Tomorrow you will see that a population of learners with different ratios represents a whole distribution of outcomes.

The simulations land on the formula: an optimistic learner values an 80% option at 0.96, a pessimistic one at about 0.39, and only the balanced learner recovers the true 0.8. Optimism can make an agent persist with a risky option; pessimism can make it avoid one that is objectively good. In Frank's terms, a learner whose Go pathway learns faster than its NoGo pathway behaves like the optimist.

<Callout kind="warning">

Fitting separate learning rates for gains and losses has become common, and differences between groups are often interpreted as differences in dopamine function. Be careful. With few trials the two rates trade off against each other and against the inverse temperature, and choice perseveration (a tendency to repeat the last choice regardless of outcome) can masquerade as asymmetric learning (Katahira, 2018). Fit a model with a perseveration term and check parameter recovery before drawing conclusions.

</Callout>
