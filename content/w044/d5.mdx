<Step n={1} title="Learning a distribution" minutes={15} />

Yesterday's optimistic and pessimistic learners settled above and below the mean. That bias is useful. A learner whose positive and negative learning rates are in the ratio $\tau : (1 - \tau)$ converges to the value $V_\tau$ at which

$$
\tau\,\mathbb E\big[(r - V_\tau)_+\big] = (1 - \tau)\,\mathbb E\big[(V_\tau - r)_+\big],
$$

the $\tau$-**expectile** of the reward distribution. The 0.5-expectile is the mean; low $\tau$ gives values near the bottom of the distribution and high $\tau$ near the top. A population of learners with $\tau$ spread from 0 to 1 jointly encodes the whole distribution, and the distribution can be decoded from their values. Bellemare, Dabney and Munos (2017) showed that deep reinforcement learning agents that learn return distributions outperform those that learn only means, partly because predicting a richer target shapes better internal representations.

Each learner lands within a few hundredths of the true expectile for its $\tau$, and together they span from about 1.6 to 6.4. The middle learner ($\tau = 0.5$) sits at the mean, 4.5, a value the reward almost never takes. Notice how the learners spread: they are denser where the expectile curve is flat, between the two modes, and the gap between the modes shows up as a steep stretch. Imputing a distribution that matches all nineteen expectiles at once recovers the two humps, which is how Dabney and colleagues decoded distributions from neurons.

<Step n={3} title="Evidence from dopamine neurons" minutes={20} />

Dabney and colleagues reanalysed recordings by Eshel and colleagues of optogenetically identified dopamine neurons in mice receiving juice of seven different, unpredictable sizes. If all neurons reported the same TD error, each would switch from dip to burst at the same reward size, the mean. Instead, the **reversal points** differed reliably from neuron to neuron. Neurons whose responses to better-than-expected rewards were steep relative to their responses to worse-than-expected ones (high $\tau$) had high reversal points, as the theory requires, and decoding the population's reversal points recovered the shape of the reward distribution, including its bimodality in a second experiment.

The finding is a neat case of an AI idea predicting a neural fact. It also invites caution: the reversal points were estimated from relatively few trials per neuron, the relation between asymmetry and reversal point is noisy, and alternative accounts (for example, neurons differing in their baseline expectations for other reasons) have been debated. Later work has looked for distributional codes in striatum and in human behaviour.

<Step n={4} title="Model-free and model-based" minutes={35} />

Everything so far is **model-free**: values are cached from experience, and change only when experienced outcomes produce errors. A **model-based** agent instead learns how the world works, which actions lead to which states and what each state yields, and computes values by planning. The two respond differently to the same events. Daw, Gershman, Seymour, Dayan and Dolan (2011) designed the two-step task to tell them apart.

At the first step the agent chooses between two options. Each leads to one of two second-stage states, mostly (70%) to one and rarely (30%) to the other. At the second stage it chooses again and is rewarded with a probability that drifts slowly. The key is what happens after a reward reached through a rare transition. A model-free learner credits the first-stage choice it made and repeats it. A model-based learner knows that the rewarded second-stage state is more reliably reached by the other first-stage option, and switches.

The model-free agent ($w = 0$) stays about 90% of the time after any reward and about 55% after no reward, whatever the transition: a main effect of reward. The model-based agent ($w = 1$) stays after a reward only if the transition was common (0.63 against 0.45) and, after no reward, stays more if the transition was rare (0.64 against 0.45): an interaction, because it reasons about which first-stage choice most reliably reaches the good state. The mixture shows both. Human participants typically look like the mixture, with weights that vary between people and shift with stress, cognitive load and training.

<Callout kind="research">

The two-step task became one of the most used paradigms in computational psychiatry, with reduced model-based weight reported in compulsive disorders (Gillan and colleagues, 2016). But Akam, Costa and Dayan (2015) showed that model-free strategies with richer state representations can mimic the model-based signature, and that the simple stay analysis can mislead. Fitting full models, checking recovery and designing tasks that force the distinction are now standard practice, and the distinction itself may be better seen as a continuum of ways to use a world model.

</Callout>
