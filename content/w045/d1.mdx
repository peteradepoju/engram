<Step n={1} title="Three parameters with meanings" minutes={15} />

In the form used by Ratcliff, evidence starts at $z$ between two bounds at $0$ and $a$ and drifts at rate $v$ with noise of standard deviation $s$ (conventionally $s = 1$ or 0.1, which only sets the scale). A response is made when either bound is reached, and the observed response time adds a **non-decision time** $T_{er}$ for encoding the stimulus and executing the movement. Each parameter has a psychological reading:

- **drift rate** $v$: the quality of evidence, set by the stimulus and the observer's sensitivity;
- **boundary separation** $a$: caution, set by the observer's strategy;
- **non-decision time** $T_{er}$: sensory and motor delays;
- **starting point** $z$: bias, set by prior expectations or payoffs (Day 4).

The power of the model is that these components, confounded in raw accuracy and mean response time, can be separated by fitting. A slower group might have lower drift (worse information), higher bounds (more caution) or longer non-decision time (slower movement), and each would have different implications.

Accuracy is about 0.79, and the response times have a sharp rise just after the 0.3 s floor and a long right tail, with a skewness of about 2. This shape, rather than a symmetric bell, is what real response times look like, and it falls out of the geometry of a random walk between bounds: there are many ways to wander for a long time and few ways to arrive quickly. Mean correct and error times are almost the same, 0.64 and 0.65 s; that equality is a prediction of the simple model with an unbiased start, and the callout below explains why real data usually break it.

<Step n={3} title="Recovering parameters" minutes={35} />

Fitting the full model needs numerical methods, but Wagenmakers, van der Maas and Grasman (2007) showed that, for an unbiased starting point, three summary statistics determine the three main parameters in closed form. From the proportion correct $P_c$, the variance of correct response times $V_{RT}$ and their mean $M_{RT}$, with $L = \ln\frac{P_c}{1 - P_c}$ and $s = 1$:

$$
v = \operatorname{sign}(P_c - \tfrac12)\left[\frac{L\,(L P_c^2 - L P_c + P_c - \tfrac12)}{V_{RT}}\right]^{1/4}, \qquad a = \frac{L}{v},
$$

and $T_{er} = M_{RT} - \text{MDT}$, where the mean decision time is $\text{MDT} = \frac{a}{2v}\cdot\frac{1 - e^{-va}}{1 + e^{-va}}$. This is the EZ-diffusion model.

solution={String.raw`
  def ez(pc, vrt, mrt):
      L = np.log(pc / (1 - pc))
      v = np.sign(pc - 0.5) * (L * (L * pc ** 2 - L * pc + pc - 0.5) / vrt) ** 0.25
      a = L / v
      mdt = (a / (2 * v)) * (1 - np.exp(-v * a)) / (1 + np.exp(-v * a))
      return v, a, mrt - mdt

  v_hat, a_hat, t_hat = ez(ch.mean(), rt[ch == 1].var(), rt[ch == 1].mean())
  print(f"EZ estimates: v = {v_hat:.3f} (true 1.0), a = {a_hat:.3f} (true 1.2), Ter = {t_hat:.3f} s (true 0.3)")
`} />

From three numbers the EZ method recovers drift 1.04, boundary separation 1.25 and non-decision time 0.296 s, all close to the truth. Its simplicity is its appeal and its limit: it assumes an unbiased start and no trial-to-trial variability, it ignores error response times, and it can fail when accuracy is near ceiling (then $L$ is undefined). For such data, or to test assumptions, fit the full model.

Both manipulations cost about 9 percentage points of accuracy, so accuracy alone cannot tell them apart. Response times can: the speed instruction makes responses 170 ms faster, while the weaker stimulus makes them slightly slower. The fits place the change correctly, the bound in one case and the drift in the other, with the other parameters unchanged. In real experiments speed instructions often lower non-decision time a little as well, and the separation is less clean, but the logic is the same.

<Callout kind="note">

The full Ratcliff diffusion model adds trial-to-trial variability in drift (standard deviation $\eta$), starting point and non-decision time. Drift variability makes errors slower than correct responses, as is typical in difficult, accuracy-emphasised tasks; starting-point variability makes errors faster, as is typical under speed pressure. The simple model you simulated predicts equal mean times for correct and error responses, which real data rarely show. Fitting the full model needs numerical likelihoods or quantile-based methods, implemented in packages such as HDDM and PyDDM.

</Callout>
