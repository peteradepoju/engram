<Step n={1} title="From motion to decision" minutes={20} />

The random-dot task separates the evidence from the decision. Direction-selective neurons in area MT respond to the motion itself, with firing rates that grow roughly linearly with coherence in their preferred direction; Britten, Shadlen, Newsome and Movshon (1992) showed that single MT neurons can be about as sensitive as the monkey, and microstimulation of MT biases choices (Salzman, Britten and Newsome, 1990). But MT responses are steady during the stimulus; they do not accumulate. Something downstream must turn a stream of momentary evidence into a decision.

Shadlen and Newsome (2001) recorded from LIP while monkeys viewed motion for a fixed duration. When the motion favoured the target in a neuron's response field, the firing rate rose; when it favoured the other target, it fell; and the slope of these changes grew with coherence. In the reaction-time version (Roitman and Shadlen, 2002), activity rose until about 70 ms before the saccade, reaching a similar level on every trial in which the monkey chose the response-field target. Both properties are what an accumulator with a bound would show, and the comparison with the diffusion model is the subject of Gold and Shadlen's 2007 review that you read in Week 24.

The average rate is flat for the first 200 ms and then rises with a slope of about 29, 58 and 100 spikes per second per second at 12.8, 25.6 and 51.2% coherence, close to proportional to coherence; at 0% it stays flat on average, because upward and downward drifts cancel across trials. This proportionality between coherence and build-up rate is the central observation of Shadlen and Newsome. Note that the average at high coherence bends over as more trials reach the bound and stop rising: averages aligned to stimulus onset mix trials at different stages of the decision.

<Step n={4} title="Ramps or steps?" minutes={30} />

Latimer, Yates, Meister, Huk and Pillow (2015) proposed a different account of the same averages. Suppose each neuron, on each trial, sits at a baseline and then jumps once to a high or low state at a random time, earlier and more often upwards for stronger motion. Averaged over trials, the jumps at different times blend into a smooth ramp. Their model fitted many LIP neurons better than a diffusion-to-bound model, and Shadlen and colleagues (2016) and Zoltowski and colleagues (2019) have since argued about how to tell the two apart. The key is that the two models differ in how the activity varies across trials, not in its average.

At half a second the two models' average rates differ by only a few spikes per second, and both rise smoothly when averaged. Their variances do not: across trials the stepping model's rate varies five times as much, because each trial is either at 5, 20 or 50 spikes per second, while the ramping trials are spread continuously around the mean. Churchland and colleagues (2011) used a related measure, the variance of the latent rate after removing Poisson noise, and found that it grew over time in a way consistent with diffusion. Latimer and colleagues fitted both models to spike trains directly and favoured steps for many neurons. Both analyses depend on assumptions about spiking noise, which is why the question is still open.

<Callout kind="research">

Two later lines of work have reshaped the debate. Inactivating LIP, unexpectedly, had little effect on monkeys' choices in the motion task (Katz and colleagues, 2016), suggesting that LIP reflects a decision computed in a wider network rather than being its sole site. And recording many neurons at once with Neuropixels now lets the decision variable be read out on single trials from the population, and such studies have reported drift-like single-trial dynamics. Population recordings answer questions that trial averages of single neurons could not.
