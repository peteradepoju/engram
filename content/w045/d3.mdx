<Step n={1} title="Competition between two populations" minutes={20} />

Wang's 2002 network contains 2,000 integrate-and-fire neurons: 1,600 excitatory and 400 inhibitory. Two small excitatory populations are selective for the two choices; each receives input that grows with motion coherence in its direction, as MT would provide. Within each population, recurrent excitation is strong and mediated largely by NMDA receptors, whose time constant of about 100 ms is long compared with a neuron's membrane time constant. The populations compete through the shared inhibitory neurons.

Without input the network rests at low spontaneous rates. With input, both populations' activity rises slowly as the slow synapses integrate the evidence; small differences are amplified by mutual inhibition until one population wins and its rate climbs to a high state while the other is suppressed. A response is triggered when the winner crosses a threshold, as LIP activity does before a saccade. Because the high state is an attractor of the dynamics, it can persist without input.

<Step n={2} title="The reduced model" minutes={35} />

Wong and Wang (2006) reduced the spiking network to two variables, the NMDA gating variables $S_1$ and $S_2$ of the two populations:

$$
\frac{dS_i}{dt} = -\frac{S_i}{\tau_S} + (1 - S_i)\,\gamma\,H(x_i), \qquad x_1 = J_{11}S_1 - J_{12}S_2 + I_0 + I_1 + I_{\text{noise},1},
$$

and symmetrically for population 2, where $H(x) = \frac{ax - b}{1 - e^{-d(ax - b)}}$ is a population's firing rate as a function of its input current, $I_i = J_A\,\mu_0\,(1 \pm c/100)$ is the stimulus input for coherence $c$ per cent, and the noise is an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process (Week 24) with a 2 ms time constant. With the published parameters ($\tau_S = 100$ ms, $J_{11} = 0.2609$ nA, $J_{12} = 0.0497$ nA, $I_0 = 0.3255$ nA, $J_A = 5.2\times10^{-4}$ nA/Hz, $\mu_0 = 30$ Hz, noise amplitude 0.02 nA) the model reproduces the spiking network's behaviour. A decision is made when either rate exceeds 15 Hz.

solution={String.raw`
  import numpy as np
  import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
  a_, b_, d_ = 270.0, 108.0, 0.154
  gamma, J11, J12, I0, JA, mu0, tauA, sigma = 0.641, 0.2609, 0.0497, 0.3255, 5.2e-4, 30.0, 0.002, 0.02

  def H(x):
      return (a_ * x - b_) / (1 - np.exp(-d_ * (a_ * x - b_)))

  def sim(c, n=300, T=1.5, dt=0.0005, thr=15.0, tauS=0.1, stim=(0.0, None), seed=0):
      g = np.random.default_rng(seed)
      S1 = np.full(n, 0.1); S2 = np.full(n, 0.1); N1 = np.zeros(n); N2 = np.zeros(n)
      ch = np.full(n, -1); rt = np.full(n, np.nan)
      for k in range(int(T / dt)):
          t = k * dt
          on = float(t >= stim[0] and (stim[1] is None or t < stim[1]))
          I1, I2 = JA * mu0 * (1 + c / 100) * on, JA * mu0 * (1 - c / 100) * on
          x1 = J11 * S1 - J12 * S2 + I0 + I1 + N1
          x2 = J11 * S2 - J12 * S1 + I0 + I2 + N2
          r1, r2 = H(x1), H(x2)
          S1 += dt * (-S1 / tauS + (1 - S1) * gamma * r1)
          S2 += dt * (-S2 / tauS + (1 - S2) * gamma * r2)
          N1 += -N1 * dt / tauA + np.sqrt(dt / tauA) * sigma * g.normal(size=n)
          N2 += -N2 * dt / tauA + np.sqrt(dt / tauA) * sigma * g.normal(size=n)
          new = (ch < 0) & ((r1 > thr) | (r2 > thr))
          ch[new] = np.where(r1[new] > r2[new], 1, 0); rt[new] = t
      return ch, rt, r1, r2

  cohs = [0.0, 3.2, 6.4, 12.8, 25.6, 51.2]

Accuracy climbs from chance at 0% to about 70% at 3.2%, 85% at 6.4% and essentially perfect above 12.8%, and decision times fall from about 0.42 to 0.17 s. These are the shapes of the monkeys' psychometric and chronometric functions, produced by a circuit with no explicit integrator and no explicit bound. The model is more accurate than monkeys at middle coherences with these parameters; more noise or a weaker stimulus gain would bring it into line. Like the data, and unlike the simple diffusion model, it also makes slower errors at low coherence, because errors tend to happen on trials where neither population pulls ahead for a long time.

Two seconds after the stimulus has gone, the winning population still fires at about 20 Hz and the loser at under 1 Hz: the network remembers its choice. Without any stimulus, both stay at their spontaneous 2 Hz and no decision is made, so the high state is reached only when input pushes the network out of the low one. The network is bistable, and in this the decision circuit is the same kind of object as the working-memory circuits of Week 46.

<Step n={4} title="Why NMDA?" minutes={25} />

Wang argued that the long NMDA time constant is essential. Replace it with a fast, AMPA-like 20 ms and see what is left.

With 100 ms synapses every trial reaches a decision; with 20 ms synapses none do. A fast synapse lets the gating variable track the rate almost instantly, so the recurrent drive is too small to lift a population into the high state: the decision attractors disappear along with the long integration window. Wang's prediction that NMDA receptors matter for both slow integration and persistent activity has been supported by studies in which blocking NMDA receptors in monkey prefrontal cortex reduced persistent activity (Wang and colleagues, 2013).
