<Step n={1} title="Two ways to be biased" minutes={20} />

A prior expectation or an asymmetric payoff can enter the diffusion model in two places. A **starting-point bias** moves $z$ towards the favoured bound: the decision maker begins partway there. A **drift bias** adds a constant to the drift: every sample of evidence is read as slightly favouring one side. Both shift the proportion of choices. But they differ in when the bias acts. A starting-point shift matters most for decisions made after little accumulation, which are the fast ones; a drift bias accumulates with time and so acts on fast and slow decisions alike.

The normative answer depends on the situation. For a known prior and a fixed stimulus strength, the optimal policy shifts the starting point by the log prior odds (Bogacz and colleagues, 2006). When stimulus strength varies from trial to trial, the optimal adjustment becomes partly time-dependent, and it can look like a drift bias (Hanks, Mazurek, Kiani, Hopp and Shadlen, 2011, who found such a dynamic bias signal in LIP).

The two biases give almost the same overall accuracy, about 0.81 to 0.82, but they distribute the bias very differently. With a starting-point shift, 84% of the fastest responses are 'right', while the slowest are slightly biased the other way (44%): fast responses come mostly from walks that started near the favoured bound, and slow ones from walks that had to overcome the head start. With a drift bias, the proportion falls only from 67% to 54%. Plotting choice proportions against response time is therefore a cheap first test of how a prior is used, and in Hanks and colleagues' monkeys the bias grew with time, pointing to a dynamic, drift-like component.

<Step n={3} title="Collapsing bounds" minutes={30} />

If the bound falls over the course of a trial, $B(t)$ decreasing towards zero, decisions that have dragged on are made on less evidence. An equivalent formulation multiplies the accumulated evidence by a growing **urgency signal** $u(t)$ and compares it with a fixed bound (Cisek, Puskas and El-Murr, 2009; Churchland, Kiani and Shadlen, 2008, who found an evidence-independent rise in LIP activity). Either way, the model makes two predictions the fixed-bound model does not: accuracy falls for slower responses within a condition, and the long right tail of the response time distribution is cut short.

With a fixed bound, accuracy is the same, about 0.84, in fast, middle and slow responses: in this simple model the time taken carries no information about whether the answer is right. With a collapsing bound, accuracy falls from 0.83 in the fastest third to 0.71 in the slowest, because late decisions are made on less evidence. Mean decision time falls from 0.86 to 0.54 s and the skewness from 1.7 to 0.6, as the long tail is cut away. Be warned, though: falling accuracy with time is also produced by trial-to-trial variability in drift, because the weakest-drift trials are both the slowest and the least accurate, which is why this signature alone is not decisive.

<Step n={4} title="Do bounds collapse?" minutes={25} />

The normative argument is straightforward. When stimulus strength varies unpredictably and some trials carry almost no evidence, a decision maker who waits for a fixed amount of evidence spends a long time on exactly the trials where waiting helps least. If time has a cost, because reward rate matters or because each moment of sampling is effortful, the optimal policy lowers the bound as time passes. Drugowitsch and colleagues derived the optimal bounds for a cost of evidence that could vary over time, fitted the model to humans and monkeys in the random-dot task, and found that the inferred cost rose during the trial in some participants, implying collapsing bounds, but that bounds in many participants were close to fixed.

Hawkins, Forstmann, Wagenmakers, Ratcliff and Brown (2015) compared fixed-bound and collapsing-bound models across many data sets and found that fixed bounds usually fitted human data as well or better, while monkeys, trained for months with strong pressure to be fast, more often showed collapse. The answer seems to depend on species, training and the reward structure. The lesson for an analyst is the one from Day 1: the signatures are real, but several mechanisms (collapsing bounds, drift variability, urgency, leak) can produce similar patterns, and only careful model comparison on full distributions can separate them.
