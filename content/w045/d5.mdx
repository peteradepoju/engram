<Step n={1} title="Asking a monkey how sure it is" minutes={15} />

In Kiani and Shadlen's task the motion was shown for a duration chosen by the experimenter, from 100 to 900 ms. On half the trials, during the delay after the motion, a third target appeared. Choosing it gave a reward smaller than a correct direction choice but certain. The design separates two questions: whether the monkey knows the answer, measured on trials without the sure target, and whether it knows that it knows, measured by when it takes the sure target and how well it does when it does not.

<Step n={2} title="Confidence as a probability" minutes={35} />

The natural definition (Kepecs and Mainen, 2012; Pouget, Drugowitsch and Kepecs, 2016) is that confidence is the probability that the choice just made is correct, given everything the decision maker knows: here, the accumulated evidence $x$ and the elapsed time $T$. If the motion strength $c$ is drawn from a known set and the evidence is $x \sim \mathcal N(k c s T, T)$ for direction $s = \pm 1$, then the choice is $\operatorname{sign}(x)$ and

$$
P(\text{correct} \mid x, T) = \frac{\sum_c p(c)\,\phi\big(x;\ \operatorname{sign}(x)\,kcT,\ T\big)}{\sum_c p(c)\big[\phi(x;\ kcT,\ T) + \phi(x;\ -kcT,\ T)\big]},
$$

where $\phi(x; \mu, \sigma^2)$ is the normal density. The time matters: after a long stimulus, a given $x$ is weaker evidence, because noise has had longer to accumulate.

In every bin, accuracy matches mean confidence to within a percentage point: trials given a confidence of 0.75 are right three times in four. That is what it means for a confidence signal to be calibrated, and it holds here because the decision maker knows the true generative model. A real observer who misjudges the mix of difficulties, or the noise, will be over- or underconfident. And at a fixed evidence of 1, confidence is lower after 0.8 s than after 0.2 s: elapsed time is itself evidence about difficulty, which is one reason confidence in reaction-time tasks falls for slow responses.

The model opts out on about three quarters of offered trials at 0 to 6.4% coherence and on a third at 51.2%, the same ordering as the monkeys. The key result also appears: at 6.4% coherence, accuracy is 0.69 on trials where the sure target was offered and declined, against 0.59 when it was not offered. Declining is informative because it happens only when confidence is high, so the declined trials are a selected, easier subset. Kiani and Shadlen found the same, and found that LIP activity at the end of the motion predicted both the choice and whether the sure target would be taken, as a single decision variable carrying confidence should.

<Step n={4} title="Confidence on correct and error trials" minutes={25} />

How should confidence vary with stimulus strength? For correct choices, stronger evidence should bring higher confidence. For errors, the opposite: an error on an easy trial must have come from evidence that was weak or misleading, so as the stimulus gets stronger, the errors that remain are made with less confidence. Plotted against stimulus strength, the two curves diverge in a "folded X". Kepecs, Uchida, Zariwala and Mainen (2008) found this pattern in the time rats were willing to wait for an uncertain reward, and in neurons of orbitofrontal cortex; Sanders, Hangya and Kepecs (2016) found it in human confidence reports.

Confidence on correct trials rises from 0.64 at 3.2% to 0.80 at 51.2%; on error trials it falls from 0.63 to 0.58. The divergence is slight for weak stimuli, where correct and error trials come from similar evidence, and grows for strong ones, where errors require evidence pushed far from the truth by noise and so tend to be the least convincing. The pattern makes a useful check that a measured signal behaves like confidence, but read today's warning before treating it as proof.

<Callout kind="warning">

The folded X is often treated as proof that a confidence signal is a Bayesian posterior. It is not: many simple measures of evidence strength, computed from the same noisy samples, produce it too (Adler and Ma, 2018). The more distinctive predictions are calibration, the effect of elapsed time at fixed evidence, and how confidence changes with new information after the choice. Testing those needs designs like Kiani and Shadlen's, in which the stimulus duration is controlled independently of the evidence.

</Callout>
