**Part A: one model for accuracy and time.** Palmer, Huk and Shadlen (2005) proposed that the drift is proportional to coherence, $v = kc$, so that a single sensitivity $k$, a bound $a$ and a non-decision time $T_{nd}$ must explain both the psychometric function (accuracy against coherence) and the chronometric function (mean response time against coherence). With bounds at $\pm a$:

$$
P_c(c) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-2kca}}, \qquad \overline{RT}(c) = \frac{a}{kc}\tanh(kca) + T_{nd}, \qquad \overline{RT}(0) = a^2 + T_{nd}.
$$

Simulate 600 trials at each of six coherences from a model with $k = 10$, $a = 0.8$ and $T_{nd} = 0.35$ s, then recover the parameters by minimising the squared errors of both curves together.

The joint fit recovers $k \approx 10.5$, $a \approx 0.82$ and $T_{nd} \approx 0.36$ s, and both fitted curves pass through the data. Three parameters explain twelve numbers. The constraint is what makes the model testable: the same $k$ and $a$ that set how accuracy rises with coherence also fix how response time falls, so a model that fitted accuracy but missed the response times would be refuted. Palmer, Huk and Shadlen found that a single $k$ described both curves well for human observers across a range of motion durations and speed instructions.

Without drift variability, errors and correct responses take the same time, within simulation noise of a few milliseconds, as the second derivation below proves. With $\eta = 1.5$, errors are 90 to 100 ms slower. Errors come disproportionately from trials whose drift happened to be weak, and weak-drift trials are slow; correct responses come disproportionately from strong-drift trials, which are fast. This is why Ratcliff's full model includes $\eta$, and why mean accuracy and RT alone cannot tell you whether the drift varies.

<Prove title="1. Accuracy with symmetric bounds" task="For drift $v$, unit noise and bounds at $\pm a$ starting from 0, use the result of Week 24 for bounds at $0$ and $2a$ starting at $a$ to show that $P(\text{upper}) = 1/(1 + e^{-2va})$.">

Shift the axis by $a$: bounds at $\pm a$ from 0 are bounds at $0$ and $2a$ from $a$. Week 24 gave, for bounds $0$ and $A$ starting at $z$, $P(\text{upper}) = \frac{1 - e^{-2vz}}{1 - e^{-2vA}}$ with unit noise.

With $z = a$ and $A = 2a$: $P = \frac{1 - e^{-2va}}{1 - e^{-4va}} = \frac{1 - e^{-2va}}{(1 - e^{-2va})(1 + e^{-2va})} = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-2va}}$.

The product $va$ alone sets accuracy, so doubling the bound has the same effect on accuracy as doubling the drift, but a very different effect on time.

</Prove>

<Prove title="2. Equal mean times for correct and error responses" task="In the simple diffusion model with an unbiased start, explain why correct and error responses have the same distribution of decision times.">

Take any path that ends at $-a$ at time $t$. Reflect it through zero: it becomes a path ending at $+a$ at the same time $t$, and it has the same noise increments with the sign reversed.

The likelihood of a path under drift $v$ is proportional to $\exp(v x_t - v^2 t/2)$ times a term that does not depend on $v$. For the original path $x_t = -a$, for the reflected one $x_t = +a$; the ratio of their likelihoods is $e^{2va}$, the same at every $t$.

So the time distributions of upper and lower crossings differ only by a constant factor, which is absorbed when each is normalised: they are identical. Breaking the symmetry, by drift variability across trials or a biased start, is what makes errors slower or faster.

</Prove>
