<Step n={1} title="Delay activity in prefrontal cortex" minutes={15} />

In the oculomotor delayed response task a monkey fixates, a cue flashes briefly at one of several locations, and after a delay of a few seconds the fixation point goes out and the monkey must look where the cue was. Funahashi, Bruce and Goldman-Rakic (1989) found that many dorsolateral prefrontal neurons fired persistently during the delay, with tuning curves over remembered location, a "memory field". The activity predicted performance: on error trials it was weaker or wrong. Similar delay activity has since been found in parietal cortex, in the frontal eye fields and elsewhere.

The bump attractor model of Compte and colleagues explains this activity with the ring architecture you met for head direction in Week 43: pyramidal neurons with similar preferred locations excite one another, through slow NMDA synapses, and broad inhibition keeps the activity local. A brief cue starts a bump at the cued location; the bump persists without input. Because nothing pins it in place, noise moves it, and the remembered location drifts.

The variance of the remembered location grows steadily with delay, from about 0.016 to 0.12 rad² over six recorded delays, close to linear (a sixfold increase in time gives roughly a sevenfold increase here, within sampling noise). So the typical error grows like the square root of the delay: four times the delay, twice the error. Human and monkey errors in delayed-response tasks grow with delay in this way, and the bump model gives a mechanism for it: there is nothing to pull the bump back, because every location is equally stable.

<Step n={3} title="Distractors" minutes={30} />

During the delay, a brief, weaker stimulus appears somewhere else. What happens to the memory depends on where.

A distractor 0.3 rad from the memory pulls it almost all the way there, and one at 0.6 or 1.2 rad does much the same: these distractors overlap the existing bump, so the network merges them into it and the bump settles in between or on the distractor. A distractor 2.5 rad away leaves the memory where it was. There, the global inhibition generated by the existing bump suppresses the new input before it can build a second bump. Compte and colleagues described this distance dependence, and it is one reason people are worse at ignoring distractors that resemble what they are trying to remember.

With strong recurrent excitation ($J_1 = 6$) the bump is still at full height at the end of the delay; with $J_1 = 2.5$ it has decayed to the background level. Below a critical strength the recurrent input cannot replace the cue, and the network has only one stable state, the uniform one. Persistent activity therefore depends on the strength of excitatory connections, which dopamine and NMDA receptor function modulate, and it is fragile in exactly the conditions, such as ageing and schizophrenia, in which working memory is impaired (Arnsten and colleagues have studied these links in prefrontal cortex).

<Callout kind="research">

Bump attractor models make a testable prediction about errors: the remembered location should drift over the delay, and the drift should be visible in the population activity. Wimmer, Nykamp, Constantinidis and Compte (2014) found exactly this in prefrontal recordings: when the population's bump was displaced late in the delay, the monkey's saccade erred in the same direction. Kilpatrick, Ermentrout and others have studied how short-term plasticity or heterogeneity can slow the drift. And serial dependence, the bias of a memory towards the previous trial's stimulus, may arise from the same attracting dynamics carried across trials.

</Callout>
