<Step n={1} title="Three challenges to persistent activity" minutes={15} />

Stokes (2015) summarised three kinds of evidence. **Dynamics**: in prefrontal recordings, the population pattern that distinguishes remembered items often evolves through the delay (Meyers, Freedman, Kreiman, Miller and Poggio, 2008; Stokes and colleagues, 2013). **Sparseness**: in single trials, delay activity can be bursty rather than sustained, with brief bouts of gamma and beta activity (Lundqvist and colleagues, 2016). **Silence**: information about an unattended memory item can disappear from measured activity and then reappear when it becomes relevant, or when the brain is pinged with a neutral stimulus (Wolff, Jochim, Akyürek and Stokes, 2017).

Mongillo, Barak and Tsodyks (2008), whose model you met in Week 33, had proposed a mechanism: short-term synaptic facilitation, lasting about a second, can hold a memory in the strengthened synapses of the neurons that were recently active, with occasional reactivations refreshing it. Persistent activity and synaptic storage are not exclusive; the brain may use both, for different purposes.

<Step n={2} title="Cross-temporal decoding" minutes={35} />

Train a decoder on the population activity at one time, $t_1$, and test it at another, $t_2$. Repeat for all pairs and you get a matrix of accuracies. A stable code gives a square block: any decoder works at any time. A dynamic code gives a narrow diagonal: a decoder works only near the time it was trained, even if each time is decodable.

All three populations are decodable at every moment, with diagonal accuracy near 0.98. Off the diagonal they differ completely. The stable code's decoders work across the whole delay. The dynamic code's decoders work only near their training time; for gaps of 8 bins or more they are barely above chance, at 0.57, because the coding direction has rotated away. The mixed code sits in between at about 0.69. This is why single-time decoding cannot tell you whether a memory is held in a fixed pattern: you need the off-diagonal.

<Step n={3} title="A stable subspace" minutes={25} />

Murray, Bernacchia, Roy, Constantinidis, Romo and Wang (2017) found that prefrontal codes in working-memory tasks are mixtures like the third population: much of the activity changes through the delay, but a subspace of the population carries the memory stably. A decoder restricted to that subspace generalises across the whole delay. The simplest estimate of it is the class difference averaged over all delay times, which cancels the parts that rotate.

Averaging the class difference over time keeps the stable component and partly cancels the rotating one, and the resulting decoder works at every time bin, never below about 0.76 and 0.88 on average. A decoder trained at the first bin averages only 0.65, because most of what it learned rotates away. The same population can thus look dynamic or stable depending on which directions you read: a downstream area needs only the stable subspace to read the memory at any moment, while the dynamic part may carry time or task information.

<Step n={4} title="Memory in the synapses" minutes={30} />

In the Mongillo model, presenting an item drives its neurons and raises the utilisation $u$ of their outgoing synapses, which then decays back to baseline $U$ with a facilitation time constant $\tau_F$ of about 1.5 s. Spiking can stop entirely while $u$ stays raised. A brief, non-specific input, a ping, then drives every neuron equally, but the neurons with facilitated synapses respond more, through recurrent connections, and the memory reappears in the activity. Wolff and colleagues used exactly this logic with EEG in humans: a task-irrelevant visual impulse during the delay evoked a response from which an unattended item could be decoded.

  delays = np.array([0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8])

Half a second after the cue, the ping response identifies the item on 92% of trials; after 2 s, 68%; by 8 s, chance. The memory fades with the facilitation time constant unless something refreshes it, which in the Mongillo model is a brief population spike that resets facilitation, and in the brain may be attention returning to the item. Two features distinguish this from persistent activity: nothing in the firing rates carries the memory between pings, and the readout is driven by a probe that itself contains no information about the item.

<Callout kind="warning">

"Activity-silent" means silent to the measurement, not necessarily to the brain. EEG and fMRI average over millions of neurons, and a code carried by a small or sparse population can be invisible to them while still being present in spikes. Barbosa and colleagues (2020) found that prefrontal activity in monkeys carried weak but real information through delays where human EEG had suggested silence, and that its reactivation predicted serial biases. Treat claims of silence as claims about a particular signal.

</Callout>
