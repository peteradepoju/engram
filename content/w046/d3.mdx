<Step n={1} title="The capacity debate" minutes={15} />

In a **continuous report** task, a participant sees several coloured squares, then after a delay is cued to one location and picks its colour on a colour wheel. The error, reported minus true colour, is an angle. Zhang and Luck (2008) found that as the number of items grew beyond about three, errors became a mixture of two kinds: reports close to the true colour with roughly constant precision, plus a growing fraction spread uniformly around the wheel, as if those items were not stored and the participant guessed. They argued for a small number of discrete **slots**.

Bays and Husain (2008) and van den Berg, Shin, Chou, George and Ma (2012) argued for a continuous **resource**: every item is stored, but with precision that falls as the resource is shared among more items, and with variability in precision from item to item, which can mimic guesses. The two accounts make different predictions about how precision and guessing change with set size, and testing them is an exercise in model comparison.

<Step n={2} title="Slots or resources?" minutes={35} />

The standard analysis fits each set size's errors with a mixture of a normal distribution centred on zero (remembered items) and a uniform distribution (guesses), giving a guess rate $g$ and a precision $\sigma$. Expectation maximisation alternates between assigning each error a probability of being remembered and re-estimating $g$ and $\sigma$.

For the slot data, the fitted guess rate is zero for one and two items, 0.26 for four and 0.50 for six, matching $1 - 3/N$, while the precision stays at about 15 degrees throughout: items are either stored well or not at all. For the resource data there is no guessing at any set size, but the spread grows from 15 to 37 degrees, as $\sqrt N$. Real data have looked like a mixture of both patterns, and van den Berg and colleagues showed that a resource model with variable precision from item to item fits as well or better than slots, because a few very imprecise memories look like guesses. The lesson is that the guess rate of a mixture fit is a description, not a mechanism.

<Step n={3} title="Mixed selectivity" minutes={20} />

In Rigotti and colleagues' task, monkeys saw two visual cues in sequence and, depending on a rule, had to recognise the sequence or recall it. Recording from prefrontal neurons, they found that many responded to particular combinations: a neuron might fire for one cue, but only under one rule and only in one task epoch. This **nonlinear mixed selectivity** cannot be described as a sum of separate responses to each variable. Rigotti and colleagues measured the dimensionality of the population's responses across task conditions and found it near the maximum possible, and found that it collapsed on error trials.

Why would that matter? A downstream neuron is, to a first approximation, a linear readout followed by a threshold. If the population's responses to $C$ conditions span only a few dimensions, many ways of grouping the conditions cannot be read out linearly. If they span $C - 1$ dimensions, every grouping can be.

The purely selective population spans only 3 dimensions across the 8 conditions, however many neurons it has, and a linear readout can implement only about 37% of the random dichotomies tried (the others include XOR-like groupings, such as "rule A with cue 1, or rule B with cue 2"). The mixed population, the same weighted sums passed through a threshold, spans the full 7 dimensions and every dichotomy is separable. The threshold is what does it: it turns a sum of variables into a response to their conjunction. Rigotti and colleagues argued that this is why prefrontal neurons look so idiosyncratic, and that a random, high-dimensional code is a cheap way to make any rule learnable downstream.
