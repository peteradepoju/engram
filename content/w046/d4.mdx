<Step n={1} title="Two kinds of attentional modulation" minutes={15} />

Measured across contrasts, a visual neuron's response rises sigmoidally and saturates. Attention can change this curve in two ways. **Contrast gain**: the curve shifts left, so attention acts like an increase in contrast and has little effect at saturation (Reynolds, Pasternak and Desimone, 2000; Martínez-Trujillo and Treue, 2002). **Response gain**: the curve is multiplied, with the largest effect at high contrast (Williford and Maunsell, 2006). The two patterns were taken as evidence for different mechanisms until Reynolds and Heeger noticed that the experiments differed in a detail: the size of the stimulus relative to the region the monkey was attending.

Desimone and Duncan's (1995) **biased competition** account came from a different observation. When two stimuli fall in one receptive field, the response is roughly their average; attending to one of them drives the response towards what that stimulus would evoke alone (Reynolds, Chelazzi and Desimone, 1999). The stimuli compete, and attention biases the competition.

<Step n={2} title="The model" minutes={30} />

For a population of neurons indexed by receptive field position $x$, the **stimulus drive** $E(x)$ is the input each would receive without normalisation. The **attention field** $A(x)$ equals 1 outside the attended region and is larger inside it. Attention multiplies the drive, and the result is divided by a **suppressive drive**, the attended drive pooled over a wider neighbourhood:

$$
R(x) = \frac{A(x)E(x)}{S(x) + \sigma}, \qquad S(x) = \big[k * (A E)\big](x),
$$

where $k$ is a broad pooling kernel and $\sigma$ sets the contrast at which responses saturate. The key is that attention enters both numerator and denominator. If the attention field is large and covers the whole pool, it scales numerator and denominator alike, which acts like multiplying the contrast. If it is small, it boosts the numerator for the attended neuron but barely changes the pooled denominator, which acts like multiplying the response.

  import numpy as np
  import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
  x = np.linspace(-10, 10, 201)
  k = np.exp(-x ** 2 / (2 * 5.0 ** 2)); k /= k.sum()

  def model(c, stim_w, att_w, attend=True, sigma=0.05, att_gain=3.0):
      E = c * np.exp(-x ** 2 / (2 * stim_w ** 2))
      A = 1 + (att_gain - 1) * np.exp(-x ** 2 / (2 * att_w ** 2)) if attend else np.ones_like(x)
      EA = E * A
      S = np.convolve(EA, k, mode="same")
      R = EA / (S + sigma)
      return R[100]                                        # the neuron at the centre

  cs = np.logspace(-3, 0, 31)
  c50 = lambda R: np.interp(R.max() / 2, R, cs)

With a small stimulus inside a large attention field, attention more than halves the contrast needed for a half-maximal response (c50 ratio 0.43) and raises the maximum by only 16%: contrast gain. With a large stimulus and a small attention field, the maximum response doubles: response gain. One model, two regimes, depending only on geometry. Herrmann, Montaser-Kouhsari, Carrasco and Heeger (2010) then tested the prediction in human psychophysics by manipulating the size of the attended region and the stimulus, and found the switch between the two kinds of effect on contrast sensitivity that the model predicts.

<Step n={4} title="Biased competition and transformers" minutes={30} />

For two stimuli in one receptive field, write the response as normalised, attention-weighted drive. If the neuron's input weights for the preferred and non-preferred stimulus are $w_1$ and $w_2$, each stimulus also contributes to the suppressive pool, and attention multiplies a stimulus's contribution to both:

$$
R = \frac{a_1 w_1 c_1 + a_2 w_2 c_2}{a_1 c_1 + a_2 c_2 + \sigma}.
$$

Compare a transformer's attention head: the output at one position is $\sum_j \frac{e^{q\cdot k_j}}{\sum_l e^{q\cdot k_l}}\,v_j$, a weighted average of values in which each weight is divided by the sum over all inputs. Both are divisive normalisations; in the brain, the attention gain $a$ comes from top-down signals, and in a transformer, the weights $e^{q \cdot k}$ come from the match between a query and each input.

Alone, the preferred stimulus evokes about 55 spikes per second and the non-preferred about 9; together, about 33, close to their average, because each adds to the suppressive pool as well as to the drive. Attending to the preferred stimulus brings the pair response to 49, most of the way to its single response; attending to the non-preferred brings it down to 20. This is biased competition, with no separate competition mechanism: attention gives one stimulus a larger share of a normalised total. The softmax in the last lines does the same thing to its inputs, giving 88% of the weight to the matching key.

<Callout kind="think">

Normalisation explains how attention changes responses, not where the attention signal comes from or why attending improves perception. Candidate sources include the frontal eye fields, whose microstimulation produces attention-like effects in visual cortex (Moore and Armstrong, 2003), and the pulvinar. Improved perception may come less from larger responses than from reduced variability and reduced noise correlations between neurons (Cohen and Maunsell, 2009), which make the population code more informative (Week 35).
