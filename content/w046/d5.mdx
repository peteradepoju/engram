<Step n={1} title="Context-dependent decisions" minutes={15} />

In Mante and colleagues' task, each stimulus had a motion coherence (left or right, of varying strength) and a colour coherence (more red or more green, of varying strength). A cue told the monkey which to report. The monkeys ignored the irrelevant feature almost completely. A natural expectation was that the irrelevant input would be filtered out before reaching prefrontal cortex, but recordings showed that both motion and colour were represented in prefrontal activity in both contexts, with similar strength. The selection occurred later: a recurrent network trained on the same task did the same, integrating along a line attractor in a direction that depended on the context, with the irrelevant input pushing activity along directions that the readout ignored.

You will not train a full recurrent network by backpropagation through time here. Instead you will use a **reservoir**: a fixed random recurrent network, driven by the inputs, whose final state is read out by a trained linear decoder (Jaeger, 2001; Maass, Natschläger and Markram, 2002). Training only the readout is enough to solve the task and to ask what the network represents.

  r = np.random.default_rng(2013)

With a trained readout the random network gets 73% of trials right, and the same on conflict trials, where answering by the wrong feature would give 0%. Without the context input, conflict trials fall to chance: the readout cannot know which feature to follow. Nothing in the recurrent weights was trained; the random network's nonlinear mixing of evidence and context, the mixed selectivity of Day 3, was enough to make the right answer linearly readable. Accuracy is limited by the noise in the evidence, not by the network: it rises to over 95% for strong stimuli.

Both features can be decoded in both contexts, at roughly 67 to 75%: the network does not filter out the irrelevant feature, as Mante and colleagues found in prefrontal cortex. Yet behaviour ignores it. In the motion context, accuracy rises from 0.59 to 0.96 as motion gets stronger, but stays at about 0.70 to 0.75 whatever the colour strength. Selection is done by the readout acting on a state in which context has changed how the evidence is laid out, not by blocking the input. In Mante's trained network, the equivalent was a context-dependent direction of integration along a line attractor.

<Step n={4} title="Many tasks in one network" minutes={30} />

Yang and colleagues trained a single recurrent network on twenty tasks drawn from the neuroscience literature: versions of delayed response, decision making with one or two modalities, context-dependent decisions, delayed match-to-sample and anti-saccades. A rule input told the network which task to perform. After training they measured each unit's task variance, how much its activity varied across stimuli within each task, and clustered the units. The network had organised itself into clusters, some active in many tasks and some in a few, which looked like functionally specialised subregions. Lesioning a cluster impaired the tasks it served. Task representations were compositional: the rule input for "anti-saccade with delay" was close to "anti-saccade" plus "delay" minus "no delay", and a network could perform a new combination by adding rule vectors. And training the tasks one after another caused catastrophic forgetting (Week 43) unless a continual learning method protected important weights.

<Callout kind="warning">

Trained networks are hypotheses, not explanations. A network that matches neural data may do so for reasons unrelated to the brain's: the architecture, the loss, the regularisation and even the optimiser shape the solution, and several different solutions can match the same data equally well (Schaeffer and colleagues, 2022, made this point for grid cells, Week 43). Use trained networks to generate predictions that a specific experiment can test, not as proof of mechanism.

</Callout>
