<Step n={1} title="Pyramidal-interneuron gamma" minutes={20} />

The best-supported mechanism for cortical gamma is **PING** (pyramidal-interneuron network gamma). Excitatory cells fire and excite fast-spiking inhibitory interneurons, mainly parvalbumin-expressing basket cells; the interneurons fire a volley a few milliseconds later that silences the excitatory cells; as the inhibition decays, the most excitable pyramidal cells fire again, and the cycle repeats. The period is roughly the time for inhibition to decay enough to let excitation through, a few times the GABA-A decay constant, plus the short delays of the loop. A related mechanism, **ING**, needs only mutually inhibiting interneurons driven by tonic excitation. Optogenetically driving parvalbumin interneurons at gamma frequencies amplifies gamma in mouse cortex, while driving them at other frequencies does not (Cardin and colleagues, 2009; Sohal and colleagues, 2009).

Brunel and Wang (2003) analysed both regimes with mean-field theory and simulations. In the sparse, noisy networks that resemble cortex, the population rhythm is fast and regular while individual neurons fire irregularly, at rates well below the oscillation frequency: each cell joins only a fraction of the cycles.

      W_EI = (g.random((NI, NE)) < p) * 0.15         # E -> I
      W_IE = (g.random((NE, NI)) < p) * 1.2          # I -> E
      W_II = (g.random((NI, NI)) < p) * 0.4          # I -> I
      vE, vI = g.random(NE), g.random(NI)

      steps = int(T / dt); rateE = np.zeros(steps); spikes = []
      for k in range(steps):
          vE += dt / 10 * (-vE + drive - xIE) + noise * np.sqrt(dt) * g.normal(size=NE)

With $\tau_I = 8$ ms the excitatory population rate oscillates at about 61 Hz, in the gamma band. Shorten inhibition to 4 ms and the rhythm speeds up to about 84 Hz; lengthen it to 16 ms and it slows to about 51 Hz. The frequency falls as inhibition lengthens, but by less than a factor of two per doubling, because the period also includes the time for excitatory cells to charge back to threshold and the loop delays, which do not scale with $\tau_I$. This is the behaviour Brunel and Wang predicted, and it is why drugs that prolong GABA-A currents, such as some anaesthetics and benzodiazepines, slow gamma.

Each excitatory cell fires at about 29 Hz, so it joins fewer than half the cycles of a 61 Hz rhythm, and its interspike intervals have a coefficient of variation near 0.9, close to that of a Poisson process (CV of 1). A recording from one cell would show irregular firing and no obvious rhythm; the oscillation is a property of the population, visible in the summed activity and in the local field potential. This is the regime Brunel and Wang called sparsely synchronised, and it matches cortical recordings, where cells fire irregularly at low rates while the field shows clear gamma.

<Step n={4} title="What gamma might do" minutes={20} />

Gamma power rises with attention in visual cortex, with stimulus contrast, and during memory encoding in hippocampus. Proposed functions include binding the features of an object by synchrony (Singer and Gray), selective routing of information between areas (Day 3's communication-through-coherence hypothesis), and a clock for temporal codes. Each proposal faces a difficulty: gamma frequency in visual cortex changes with stimulus contrast and differs between neighbouring sites (Ray and Maunsell, 2010), which complicates the idea of a shared clock, and some argue that gamma is a by-product of the E-I balance that any active circuit has, rather than a mechanism the brain uses.
