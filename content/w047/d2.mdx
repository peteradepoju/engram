<Step n={1} title="Locking to a rhythm" minutes={15} />

Given the phases $\phi_1, \dots, \phi_n$ of the field oscillation at each of a neuron's spikes, the phase-locking value (PLV) is the length of their mean resultant vector:

$$
\text{PLV} = \left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n} e^{i\phi_k}\right|.
$$

It is 1 if every spike falls at the same phase and close to 0 if phases are uniform. But "close to 0" depends on $n$: the sum of $n$ random unit vectors has length about $\sqrt n$, so for unlocked spikes the PLV is about $1/\sqrt n$. With 10 spikes that is 0.3, which would look like strong locking.

Vinck and colleagues' fix is to compare spikes in pairs. For any two spikes with phases $\phi_j$ and $\phi_k$, $\cos(\phi_j - \phi_k)$ measures whether they agree. Averaging over all distinct pairs gives the **pairwise phase consistency**, which has an expectation of zero for unlocked spikes whatever $n$ is, and of $\text{PLV}_\infty^2$ (the square of the population value) for locked spikes:

$$
\text{PPC} = \frac{2}{n(n - 1)}\sum_{j \lt k}\cos(\phi_j - \phi_k) = \frac{\left|\sum_k e^{i\phi_k}\right|^2 - n}{n(n - 1)}.
$$

  import numpy as np
  import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
  from scipy.signal import butter, filtfilt, hilbert
  r = np.random.default_rng(2010)
  fs, T = 1000, 60
  t = np.arange(T * fs) / fs
  theta = 2 * np.pi * 8 * t + np.cumsum(0.05 * r.normal(size=len(t)))      # a slightly wandering rhythm
  lfp = np.cos(theta) + 0.8 * r.normal(size=len(t))

  def spikes(rate, kappa, pref=0.0):
      lam = rate * np.exp(kappa * np.cos(theta - pref)) / np.i0(kappa) / fs
      return np.where(r.random(len(t)) < lam)[0]

  b, a = butter(3, [6 / (fs / 2), 10 / (fs / 2)], "band")
  phase = np.angle(hilbert(filtfilt(b, a, lfp)))

  def plv(ph):
      return np.abs(np.mean(np.exp(1j * ph)))

  def ppc(ph):
      n = len(ph)
      return (np.abs(np.exp(1j * ph).sum()) ** 2 - n) / (n * (n - 1))

  s = spikes(5, 1.0); p = phase[s]
  print(f"{len(s)} spikes: PLV {plv(p):.3f}, PPC {ppc(p):.3f}, PLV squared {plv(p) ** 2:.3f}")
  plt.hist(p, 24); plt.xlabel("theta phase at spike (rad)"); plt.show()

For random phases the PLV is 0.30 with 10 spikes and still 0.09 with 100, tracking $1/\sqrt n$. A study comparing two conditions with 10 and 100 spikes per neuron would find "stronger locking" in the first, from the bias alone. The PPC averages essentially zero at every $n$. Its estimates are noisier with few spikes, and can be negative, but they are centred on the truth, which is what matters when averaging across neurons or comparing conditions.

The two neurons are locked equally, yet the PLV says the 0.5 Hz neuron, with about ten spikes in 20 seconds, is more than twice as locked as the 20 Hz neuron (0.32 against 0.14). The PPC gives both about 0.02, the square of their true locking. Studies of interneurons against pyramidal cells, or of attended against unattended conditions with different rates, have exactly this problem, and the PPC, or a PLV computed on equal numbers of spikes subsampled from each group, is the remedy.

<Callout kind="warning">

The PPC removes the bias from spike count, but not every problem. Spikes within the same trial share that trial's rhythm and may share its phase drift; the PPC variants of Vinck and colleagues (2012) compare only spikes from different trials to avoid this. And if the spike itself contaminates the field recorded on the same electrode, the spike's own waveform creates artefactual locking at high frequencies. Use fields from a neighbouring electrode or remove the spike waveform first.

</Callout>

<Key title="Before you go">
<ul>
<li>Spike phases come from the Hilbert transform of a band-passed field.</li>
<li>The PLV is biased upwards when spikes are few.</li>
<li>The PPC removes this bias by excluding self-pairs.</li>
<li>Comparing neurons or conditions with different spike counts needs the PPC.</li>
</ul>
