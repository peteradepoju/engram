<Step n={1} title="The hypothesis" minutes={20} />

Fries's 2015 statement of communication through coherence (CTC) has three parts. A receiving population that oscillates in the gamma band alternates between moments of high and low excitability, because each cycle includes a volley of inhibition (Day 1). Input spikes that arrive during high excitability have more effect than those arriving during inhibition. And attention, or task relevance, establishes coherence between the receiver and the sender carrying the relevant input, with a phase relation that makes the sender's spikes arrive at the receiver's excitable phase, after allowing for conduction delay. Inputs from other senders, not coherent with the receiver, arrive at random phases and are on average attenuated.

The evidence cited includes stronger gamma coherence between V1 and V4 sites representing an attended stimulus than an unattended one (Bosman and colleagues, 2012; Grothe and colleagues, 2012), and the finding that the effect of a stimulus on V4 depended on the gamma phase at which it arrived. Fries also linked gamma to feedforward signalling and alpha and beta rhythms to feedback (Bastos and colleagues, 2015).

solution={String.raw`
  import numpy as np
  import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
  r = np.random.default_rng(2015)
  f, T, delay = 40.0, 2.0, 0.004
  excitability = lambda t: 0.5 * (1 + np.cos(2 * np.pi * f * t))

  def gain(dphi, jitter=0.002, n=2000):
      k = r.integers(0, int(T * f), n)
      t_spike = k / f - dphi / (2 * np.pi * f) + jitter * r.normal(size=n)   # sender fires at its phase 0, leading the receiver by dphi
      return excitability(t_spike + delay).mean()

  phis = np.linspace(0, 2 * np.pi, 24, endpoint=False)
  gains = np.array([gain(p) for p in phis])
  print(f"gain ranges from {gains.min():.2f} to {gains.max():.2f}; best phase relation {phis[np.argmax(gains)]:.2f} rad (2 pi f delay = {2 * np.pi * f * delay:.2f})")
  plt.plot(phis, gains, "o-"); plt.xlabel("sender lead (rad)"); plt.ylabel("effective gain"); plt.show()

Gain ranges from 0.06 to 0.94 depending only on the phase relation: the same spikes, at the same rate, can be almost ignored or almost fully effective. The best relation, about 1.05 rad, is the phase the conduction delay takes up, $2\pi f \times 4\,\text{ms} \approx 1.0$ rad: the sender must lead by just enough for its spikes to arrive at the receiver's excitable moment. Real measurements of gamma phase relations between V1 and V4 are consistent with such delay-compensating leads.

The coherent sender's gain is 0.94 and the drifting sender's 0.49, the average excitability: the receiver effectively listens to one input nearly twice as well as the other, without any change in connection strength or firing rate. The advantage depends on precise timing. With 5 ms of jitter, a fifth of a 25 ms cycle, the coherent gain falls to 0.72; with 10 ms it is 0.52, no better than the incoherent sender. Routing by coherence needs senders whose spikes are tightly synchronised within each cycle, which is one reason why it is proposed for gamma generated by PING rather than for slower or looser rhythms.

<Step n={4} title="Evidence and doubts" minutes={25} />

The hypothesis is elegant, and it has strong critics. Ray and Maunsell (2010, 2015) showed that gamma frequency in V1 varies with stimulus contrast and size, so sites viewing different stimuli oscillate at different frequencies and cannot maintain a fixed phase relation. Schneider, Dann, Sheshadri, Scherberger and Vinck (2021) argued that the coherence observed between areas can arise passively from the sender driving the receiver, with no need for the receiver's rhythm to be a gating mechanism: coherence would be a consequence of communication rather than its cause. And many studies report gamma only for particular stimuli, such as large gratings, raising the question of whether it is present during natural vision. Supporters respond that sender and receiver can lock despite frequency differences through entrainment, and that causal manipulations are beginning to test the idea.

<Callout kind="research">

The decisive experiments are causal: change the phase relation between two areas without changing their rates, and measure whether communication changes. Optogenetic and electrical stimulation timed to ongoing rhythms make this possible in rodents and monkeys. If you designed such an experiment, which phase relation would you impose, what would you measure in the receiver, and what result would falsify the hypothesis?
