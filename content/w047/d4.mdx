<Step n={1} title="Cortical states and arousal" minutes={20} />

In quiet wakefulness or light anaesthesia, cortical neurons alternate between depolarised **up states** and hyperpolarised **down states** every second or so, and the field potential shows large slow waves: a synchronised state. With arousal, the slow fluctuations disappear and the cortex becomes desynchronised. Locomotion adds further depolarisation and changes sensory responses again (Niell and Stryker, 2010, found larger visual responses during running in mouse V1, while auditory responses were often suppressed).

McGinley and colleagues recorded the membrane potential of auditory cortical neurons together with pupil diameter. At small pupil, the potential fluctuated slowly and widely; at large pupil and during locomotion, it was depolarised with fast fluctuations and reduced evoked responses; at intermediate pupil it was hyperpolarised and quiet, and tone-evoked responses were largest and most reliable. In a tone-in-noise detection task, mice performed best at the same intermediate pupil size. Pupil diameter tracks locus coeruleus activity (Joshi, Li, Kalwani and Gold, 2016) and also cholinergic signalling (Reimer and colleagues, 2016).

<Step n={2} title="A model of the inverted U" minutes={30} />

Two observations are enough for an inverted U. The background noise against which a stimulus must be detected, the slow membrane fluctuations, is large at low arousal and falls quickly as arousal rises. The evoked response is large until arousal is high and then shrinks. If detection depends on their ratio, $d' = A(a)/\sigma(a)$, performance peaks in between:

$$
A(a) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{(a - 0.7)/0.08}}, \qquad \sigma(a) = 0.3 + 1.2\,e^{-a/0.15}.
$$

This is a description of McGinley and colleagues' measurements, not a mechanism; the mechanisms behind each curve, from thalamic bursting to neuromodulatory effects on inhibition, are research topics in their own right.

Sensitivity peaks at an arousal of about 0.49 with $d' \approx 2.7$, and falls to 0.67 at the lowest arousal and 0.08 at the highest. The two ends fail for different reasons: at low arousal the stimulus is drowned by slow fluctuations, while at high arousal the background is quiet but the response itself has shrunk. That distinction matters, because it predicts different effects of interventions: reducing slow fluctuations should help drowsy animals, and nothing that reduces noise will help an over-aroused one.

<Step n={3} title="Testing for an inverted U" minutes={35} />

In an experiment you do not see $d'$; you see trials, each with a pupil size and a correct or incorrect response. Simulate 3,000 trials of a detection task under the model, with pupil a noisy readout of arousal, and test for the inverted U the way a careful analyst would: compare logistic regressions with and without a squared pupil term.

The linear model finds a negative slope, which would suggest that larger pupils are simply bad for performance, missing the rise at small pupil sizes. Adding a squared term improves the fit enormously (a likelihood ratio of 155 for one extra parameter), the squared coefficient is negative, and the fitted peak is at a pupil size of 0.43, close to the model's optimum. A binned plot shows the same shape. For a published claim you would add the cautions below, and check that the peak is not produced by a few sessions with unusual pupil ranges.

<Step n={4} title="Neuromodulators set the gain" minutes={20} />

Aston-Jones and Cohen's (2005) **adaptive gain theory** proposes that the locus coeruleus, the brain's main source of noradrenaline, operates in two modes. In the **phasic** mode, at moderate tonic activity, it fires brief bursts after task-relevant events, increasing the gain of target circuits at the right moments and supporting focused performance (exploitation). In the **tonic** mode, at high baseline activity, the bursts disappear, gain is raised indiscriminately, and behaviour becomes distractible and exploratory. The inverted U of performance against arousal follows naturally. Acetylcholine, from the basal forebrain, has related effects on cortical state and on the balance between bottom-up sensory input and top-down expectations (Yu and Dayan, 2005, proposed that acetylcholine signals expected uncertainty and noradrenaline unexpected uncertainty).

<Callout kind="warning">

Pupil diameter is a convenient readout but not a pure one. It is driven by both noradrenergic and cholinergic systems, responds to luminance and accommodation, and lags neural events by about a second. An inverted U against pupil size can also arise from mixing trials from different behavioural states, such as engaged and disengaged. Control luminance, measure the pupil's derivative as well as its size, and check that the effect survives within sessions.

</Callout>
