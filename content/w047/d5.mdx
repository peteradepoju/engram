<Step n={1} title="Up states and down states" minutes={15} />

During the slow oscillation, intracellular recordings show each neuron's membrane potential jumping between a **down state**, hyperpolarised and silent, and an **up state**, depolarised by about 10 to 15 mV, in which the neuron fires irregularly and receives intense, balanced excitation and inhibition. Neighbouring neurons switch nearly together, and the transitions propagate across cortex as travelling waves, mostly from front to back in humans (Massimini, Huber, Ferrarelli, Hill and Tononi, 2004). In the scalp EEG of deep sleep, the down states appear as large slow waves.

Two ingredients explain the alternation (Sanchez-Vives and McCormick, 2000; Compte, Sanchez-Vives, McCormick and Wang, 2003). **Recurrent excitation** makes the cortex bistable: once enough neurons are active, they keep each other active; once silent, they stay silent. **Slow activity-dependent adaptation**, through calcium- and sodium-activated potassium currents and synaptic depression, builds up during an up state until it can no longer be sustained, and recovers during the down state until noise or a small input restarts activity.

<Step n={2} title="Bistability and adaptation" minutes={35} />

One population with activity $x$, recurrent weight $w$, input $I$ and adaptation $a$:

$$
\tau\,\dot x = -x + f(wx + I - a) + \text{noise}, \qquad \tau_a\,\dot a = -a + b\,x,
$$

with $f(u) = 1/(1 + e^{-(u - 3)/0.5})$, $w = 6$, $\tau = 10$ ms and $\tau_a = 500$ ms. With $b = 0$ there is no adaptation.

Without adaptation the population is bistable and noise alone moves it between states, rarely: about one transition every ten seconds, with dwell times of several seconds that vary widely. With adaptation and a modest drive it cycles about 0.7 times per second, with up and down states each lasting around 0.7 s, the timescale of the slow oscillation. Adaptation supplies the clock: each up state builds the current that ends it, and each down state lets it decay until the next up state can start. Up-state durations still vary (a coefficient of variation of about 0.6), because noise decides the exact moment of each transition, as in real recordings.

With no drive the population is almost always down; at a drive of 1 it alternates, up about half the time; at 2 it is up 98% of the time and down states have essentially vanished. A single parameter takes the circuit from a sleep-like to a waking-like regime, which is roughly what cholinergic and noradrenergic projections do on waking. In between, at a drive of 1.5, up states dominate but down states still intrude: a description, perhaps, of drowsiness.

The sleep-like state has a spectral peak at about 0.5 Hz, the slow oscillation, and nearly twenty times more power below 2 Hz than the waking-like state, whose spectrum is flat. This is how sleep is scored from the EEG: large power at slow frequencies marks deep non-REM sleep. Note the connection to Day 4: the large slow fluctuations of a drowsy animal are the same kind of activity, and they are the background noise that made detection poor at low arousal.

<Callout kind="research">

The slow oscillation coordinates other rhythms. Thalamocortical spindles (11 to 16 Hz) tend to occur on the rising phase of up states, and hippocampal sharp-wave ripples tend to nest within spindles; this nesting is thought to time the transfer of recent memories from hippocampus to cortex (Week 43). Ngo, Martinetz, Born and Mölle (2013) played sounds in phase with the up states of sleeping people and enhanced both slow oscillations and next-day memory. Whether such closed-loop stimulation helps memory reliably, and for whom, is an active question.

</Callout>
