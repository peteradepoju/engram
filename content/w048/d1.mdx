<Step n={1} title="Two distributions and a criterion" minutes={20} />

On each trial the observer receives an internal response $x$. On noise trials, $x \sim \mathcal N(0, 1)$; on signal trials, $x \sim \mathcal N(d', 1)$. The observer says "yes" if $x$ exceeds a criterion $k$. Then the hit rate and false-alarm rate are

$$
H = 1 - \Phi(k - d'), \qquad F = 1 - \Phi(k),
$$

where $\Phi$ is the standard normal cumulative distribution. Inverting them gives the two quantities separately:

$$
d' = z(H) - z(F), \qquad c = -\tfrac12\big(z(H) + z(F)\big),
$$

where $z = \Phi^{-1}$ and $c$ measures the criterion relative to the midpoint between the distributions (0 is unbiased; positive is conservative). Tanner and Swets showed that human observers detecting tones behave this way: as payoffs or instructions change, hit and false-alarm rates move together along a curve of constant $d'$.

Both conditions give $d' = 1.49$; only the criterion moved, from $+0.25$ (slightly conservative) to $-0.25$ (slightly liberal). The observer's hearing did not change at all; the payoff made them say yes more readily, which raised hits and false alarms together. Comparing hit rates alone would have suggested better detection. This is why attention, emotion and drug effects on perception must be analysed with $d'$ and $c$, not with hit rates.

<Step n={3} title="ROC curves from ratings" minutes={35} />

Running a separate block for each payoff is slow. Instead, ask the observer to rate confidence on, say, a six-point scale, from "sure no" to "sure yes". Each boundary between ratings acts as a criterion, and cumulating the ratings from the top gives a whole receiver operating characteristic (ROC) in one experiment. Plotted as $z(H)$ against $z(F)$, an equal-variance observer gives a straight line of slope 1; if the signal distribution is wider than the noise distribution, as is common in memory and some perception experiments, the slope is $\sigma_n/\sigma_s$, less than 1.

The equal-variance observer's z-ROC has a slope of 0.97, close to 1, and the unequal-variance observer's 0.63, close to the predicted $1/1.5 = 0.67$. The area under the equal-variance ROC is 0.84, a little below $\Phi(1.5/\sqrt2) = 0.86$ because joining five points with straight lines cuts the corners of a smooth curve. Recognition memory experiments typically give z-ROC slopes near 0.8, which is one piece of evidence that remembered items produce more variable memory strength than new ones (Wixted, 2007).

<Step n={4} title="Two-alternative forced choice" minutes={20} />

In two-alternative forced choice (2AFC), each trial contains one signal interval and one noise interval, and the observer picks the interval with the larger response. The difference between the two responses has mean $d'$ and standard deviation $\sqrt2$, so

$$
P_c = \Phi\!\left(\frac{d'}{\sqrt 2}\right),
$$

which is also the area under the yes-no ROC (Green and Swets, 1966). 2AFC has the advantage of being free of criterion bias, which is why most psychophysics uses it.

<Key title="Before you go">
<ul>
<li>Sensitivity and criterion are computed from z-transformed hit and false-alarm rates.</li>
<li>Payoffs move the criterion; only perceptual change moves d'.</li>
<li>Confidence ratings give a whole ROC; its z-slope tests equal variance.</li>
<li>2AFC accuracy is Φ(d'/√2), the area under the ROC.</li>
