<Step n={1} title="The aperture problem" minutes={20} />

A neuron with a small receptive field sees only part of a moving edge. It can measure the motion perpendicular to the edge but not along it, because sliding the edge along itself changes nothing in the aperture. Each local measurement constrains the velocity to a line in velocity space: all velocities $\mathbf v$ with $\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf v = s$, where $\mathbf n$ is the edge's normal and $s$ the measured normal speed. With two edges of different orientations, the two lines intersect at a single velocity, the **intersection of constraints** (IOC; Adelson and Movshon, 1982). An alternative, cheaper rule, the **vector average** (VA) of the two normal velocities, gives the wrong answer for some stimuli. People perceive the IOC at high contrast and something closer to the vector average at low contrast, which looked like evidence for two different mechanisms.

Weiss and colleagues showed that a single Bayesian observer does both. The measurements are noisy, with noise that grows as contrast falls, and the prior favours slow speeds:

$$
p(\mathbf v \mid \text{image}) \propto \exp\!\Big(-\sum_i \frac{(\mathbf n_i\cdot\mathbf v - s_i)^2}{2\sigma^2}\Big)\,\exp\!\Big(-\frac{|\mathbf v|^2}{2\sigma_p^2}\Big).
$$

<Step n={2} title="Speed with a slow prior" minutes={25} />

In one dimension, with likelihood $\mathcal N(v_\text{true}, \sigma_l^2)$ and prior $\mathcal N(0, \sigma_p^2)$, the posterior mean is

$$
\hat v = \frac{\sigma_p^2}{\sigma_p^2 + \sigma_l^2}\,v_\text{true}.
$$

If the measurement noise scales inversely with contrast, $\sigma_l = \sigma_0 / c$, lower contrast means stronger shrinkage towards zero.

At full contrast the observer sees 99% of the true speed; at 10% contrast, half; at 5%, a fifth. Nothing is wrong with the observer: given noisy measurements and a world in which slow motion is common, shrinking towards zero minimises error on average. Stone and Thompson (1992) found that a low-contrast grating needs to move substantially faster than a high-contrast one to appear equally fast, and Thompson's observation that drivers in fog underestimate speed is the same effect outside the laboratory.

<Step n={3} title="Direction from two constraints" minutes={40} />

Now the plaid. It moves right at speed 1; its gratings have normals at 50 and 75 degrees, so each measures a normal speed $s_i = \mathbf n_i\cdot\mathbf v$. With Gaussian likelihood and prior, the posterior is Gaussian and its peak solves a linear system:

$$
\Big(\frac{N^\top N}{\sigma^2} + \frac{I}{\sigma_p^2}\Big)\hat{\mathbf v} = \frac{N^\top\mathbf s}{\sigma^2},
$$

where the rows of $N$ are the normals.

With precise measurements the percept is the intersection of constraints, moving right at full speed. As noise grows the percept slows and its direction swings upward: 17 degrees at moderate noise, 48 at high noise, and 56 at very high noise, close to the vector average direction of 57 degrees. The prior does not treat all directions equally because the two constraint lines are nearly parallel: the data pin down the velocity well along one axis and poorly along the other, so the prior shrinks it mainly along the poorly constrained axis, which rotates it. One observer, with no switch between rules, produces both IOC-like and VA-like percepts, as Yo and Wilson (1992) and others had found psychophysically.

<Step n={4} title="What the model explains and misses" minutes={20} />

Weiss and colleagues showed that the same observer, with one prior and contrast-dependent noise, accounts for the perceived slowing at low contrast (Thompson, 1982), direction biases in plaids and rhombuses, the barber pole illusion, and the effect of edge orientation on perceived motion of lines. Its success made "perception as Bayesian inference" a mainstream view.

The model also has limits. The prior was assumed Gaussian and centred on zero, and its width was chosen, not measured; tomorrow's paper shows how to measure it. At high speeds, some studies find that low contrast makes things appear faster, not slower (Thompson, Brooks and Hammett, 2006), which a single slow prior cannot produce. And the model does not say how neurons compute the posterior, a question for Day 4.
