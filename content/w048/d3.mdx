<Step n={1} title="What the data constrain" minutes={20} />

Work in log speed, $u = \log v$, where Weber's law makes measurement noise roughly constant. The observer's measurement of a stimulus with contrast $c$ is $m \sim \mathcal N(u, \sigma_c^2)$. Near any speed the log prior can be approximated by a straight line with slope $a$; for a prior favouring slow speeds, $a \lt 0$. Then the posterior mean is shifted from the measurement by $a\sigma_c^2$:

$$
\hat u = m + a\,\sigma_c^2.
$$

Noisier measurements are shifted more. When the observer compares a test (contrast $c_t$) with a reference ($c_r$), the probability of calling the test faster is

$$
P(\text{test faster}) = \Phi\!\left(\frac{u_t - u_r + a(\sigma_t^2 - \sigma_r^2)}{\sqrt{\sigma_t^2 + \sigma_r^2}}\right).
$$

Two things can be read from a psychometric function measured against $u_t$: its **slope** gives $\sqrt{\sigma_t^2 + \sigma_r^2}$, and its **point of subjective equality** (PSE), where $P = \frac12$, is shifted by $-a(\sigma_t^2 - \sigma_r^2)$. Comparing same-contrast and different-contrast conditions separates the noises from the prior.

The binomial likelihood weights each point by how much information it carries, which matters near 0 and 1 where proportions are most precise.

The recovered noise levels, 0.156 and 0.354, are within a few per cent of the truth, and the prior slope, $-1.28$, is close to the true $-1.2$. Each estimate rests on 720 trials per condition; the prior slope is the most fragile because it divides a small shift by a difference of variances. Stocker and Simoncelli fitted all conditions at once with a full model, which uses the data more efficiently and gives a prior slope at each reference speed, tracing out the whole prior.

<Callout kind="research">

Stocker and Simoncelli repeated this at several reference speeds and contrasts and reconstructed each observer's speed prior; it fell off with speed more slowly than a Gaussian, closer to a power law. Later work inferred priors for orientation, which match the over-representation of cardinal orientations in natural scenes (Girshick, Landy and Simoncelli, 2011), and showed that the same efficient-coding principle that shapes neural tuning (Week 39) predicts both perceptual biases and discrimination thresholds (Wei and Stocker, 2015), including biases away from the prior that a simple Bayesian observer cannot produce.
