<Step n={1} title="The theory" minutes={20} />

Assume an image $\mathbf x$ is generated from causes $\mathbf r$ through weights $U$, $\mathbf x = U\mathbf r + \text{noise}$, and that causes are sparse. The best causes minimise

$$
E(\mathbf r, U) = \tfrac12\,|\mathbf x - U\mathbf r|^2 + \lambda\,|\mathbf r|_1.
$$

Gradient descent on $\mathbf r$ gives the inference dynamics

$$
\mathbf e = \mathbf x - U\mathbf r, \qquad \dot{\mathbf r} \propto U^\top\mathbf e - \lambda\,\operatorname{sign}(\mathbf r),
$$

which have a natural neural reading: **error units** compute $\mathbf e$, the input minus the top-down prediction $U\mathbf r$, and send it up through weights $U^\top$; **representation units** $\mathbf r$ change to reduce the error. Learning slowly adjusts the generative weights by $\Delta U \propto \mathbf e\,\mathbf r^\top$, a Hebbian product of error and cause. Rao and Ballard stacked such modules, so that each level predicts the representation of the level below. Similar sparse-coding ideas from Olshausen and Field (Week 13) learned the same oriented filters.

Inference finds the two bars that made the patch: the two largest causes are exactly the true ones, and the prediction error falls from about 90% of the input to about 30%. The error does not reach the noise level because the sparsity penalty shrinks every cause slightly, a bias that trades accuracy for sparse, interpretable causes. Notice what the network never had to do: compare the input with each bar separately. The error signal, fed back through the same weights, did the search.

From random weights, 400 batches of learning recover all 16 bars: every true bar has a learned column matching it essentially perfectly, and the relative error on new patches is about 0.27, the same as with the true dictionary. The learning rule is local: each weight changes by the product of an error unit's activity and a cause unit's activity. Rao and Ballard ran the same procedure on natural image patches and obtained oriented, localised receptive fields like V1 simple cells.

<Step n={4} title="Surprise" minutes={25} />

Predictive coding predicts that responses to an expected stimulus should be small and responses to an unexpected one large, because error units report only what was not predicted. Apply the idea over time: a higher level predicts the next input from recent inputs, $\mathbf p_t = \mathbf p_{t-1} + \alpha(\mathbf x_{t-1} - \mathbf p_{t-1})$, and the error is $|\mathbf x_t - \mathbf p_t|$. Present a standard stimulus repeatedly with occasional deviants, as in the oddball paradigm used to measure the mismatch negativity.

The first standard produces a large error, 1.5; by its eleventh repetition the error has fallen to 0.45, close to the noise floor: repetition suppression. Deviants, arriving after many standards, produce errors of about 1.75, more than three times those of late standards: a mismatch response. Notice, though, that the same pattern would come from simple adaptation of the units that respond to the standard. Telling prediction from adaptation needs designs in which a stimulus is rare but not novel, or expected but not repeated, which is the subject of today's warning.

<Callout kind="warning">

Predictive coding is a family of theories, not one, and its core prediction, dedicated error neurons that report the difference between input and prediction, has been hard to pin down. Many apparent prediction-error responses can also be explained by adaptation of feedforward responses, and some studies that control for adaptation find genuine expectation effects while others do not (Walsh, McGovern, Clark and O'Connell, 2020, review the evidence). Stimulus-specific adaptation, the deceptively simple explanation of today's oddball result, must be ruled out before claiming prediction.

</Callout>
