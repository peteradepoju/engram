<Step n={1} title="The connectome" minutes={15} />

Network neuroscience works at several scales: synapses between neurons (the full wiring of C. elegans or the fly), axonal projections between areas traced in animals, and white-matter tracts estimated with diffusion MRI in humans. **Structural connectivity** is the wiring. **Functional connectivity** is statistical dependence between regions' activity, usually correlation of fMRI time series at rest. **Effective connectivity** is the causal influence of one region on another, which requires a model or an intervention.

Bullmore and Sporns highlighted several properties shared across scales and species. Brain networks are **modular**, with modules corresponding to functional systems such as visual, motor and default-mode networks. They have **hubs**, often in association cortex, which are densely interconnected with each other in a **rich club** (van den Heuvel and Sporns, 2011). And they are **economical**: most connections are short, which saves wiring cost, but a few expensive long-range links shorten paths. Damage to hubs has disproportionate effects, and hubs are often implicated in brain disorders.

<Step n={2} title="Finding modules" minutes={35} />

Newman's **modularity** of a division of nodes into communities is the fraction of links that fall within communities minus the fraction expected if links were placed at random with the same degrees:

$$
Q = \frac{1}{2m}\sum_{ij}\Big(A_{ij} - \frac{k_ik_j}{2m}\Big)\delta(c_i, c_j),
$$

where $m$ is the number of links. The matrix $B_{ij} = A_{ij} - k_ik_j/2m$ is the **modularity matrix**. Newman (2006) showed that splitting the network by the signs of $B$'s leading eigenvector approximately maximises $Q$; repeating the split within each part gives more communities.

  import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
  from itertools import permutations
  from scipy.sparse.csgraph import shortest_path
  r = np.random.default_rng(2009)
  M, n = 4, 30; N = M * n; lab = np.repeat(np.arange(M), n)
  P = np.where(lab[:, None] == lab[None], 0.3, 0.02)
  A = np.triu(r.random((N, N)) < P, 1); A = (A | A.T).astype(float)

  def modQ(A, c):
      k = A.sum(1); m2 = k.sum(); B = A - np.outer(k, k) / m2
      return (B * (c[:, None] == c[None])).sum() / m2

  def split(A, idx):
      k = A.sum(1); B = A - np.outer(k, k) / k.sum()
      Bg = B[np.ix_(idx, idx)]; Bg = Bg - np.diag(Bg.sum(1))
      w, v = np.linalg.eigh(Bg)
      return idx[v[:, -1] >= 0], idx[v[:, -1] < 0]

  c = np.zeros(N, int); g = 0
  for part in split(A, np.arange(N)):
      p1, p2 = split(A, part); c[p1] = g; c[p2] = g + 1; g += 2

Two rounds of spectral splitting assign 92% of nodes to their planted community and give a modularity of 0.47, a little below the planted partition's 0.55. Repeated bisection cannot undo an early mistake, so practical methods refine the partition afterwards, for example by moving single nodes when that raises $Q$ (the Louvain algorithm of Blondel and colleagues, 2008, is the most used). Modularity also has a resolution limit: it can merge small genuine modules, so the communities found depend on the method as well as the network.

The grown network has hubs: the best-connected node has 33 links against a mean of 4. Removing 20 random nodes leaves 99% of the global efficiency, because most nodes are peripheral and few paths ran through them. Removing the 20 biggest hubs leaves 14%: the network falls apart. Albert, Jeong and Barabási (2000) described this combination of robustness and fragility for the internet; for brains, it suggests why lesions or degeneration that hit hub regions can have outsized effects.

A and C correlate at 0.46 although nothing connects them: B carries A's influence to C. Their partial correlation is zero, because once B is accounted for, A tells you nothing more about C; the real links, A to B and B to C, keep strong partial correlations, and the independent region D is unrelated to everything. Resting-state functional connectivity is mostly computed as plain correlation, so many of its 'connections' are indirect. Partial correlation and related sparse inverse covariance methods (Smith and colleagues, 2011, compared many such methods on simulated fMRI) are closer to the structural network.

<Callout kind="warning">

Partial correlation removes indirect paths only through measured regions. An unmeasured common input still produces a spurious link, and partial correlation is symmetric, so it cannot tell A to B from B to A. Interventions (stimulation, lesions) or careful causal models are needed for direction, as Neuromatch's causality tutorials develop. Functional connectivity measured with fMRI also depends on head motion, physiological noise and preprocessing choices such as global signal regression, which can change results substantially.

</Callout>
