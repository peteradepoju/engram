<Step n={1} title="Surprisal" minutes={20} />

The **surprisal** of word $w_t$ in its context is

$$
s(w_t) = -\log_2 P(w_t \mid w_1, \dots, w_{t-1}) \quad \text{bits}.
$$

A word that was certain has surprisal 0; one with probability 1/8 has 3 bits. Levy (2008) showed that surprisal equals the amount by which a comprehender's probability distribution over complete interpretations of the sentence must change when the word arrives (the relative entropy between the distributions before and after), which gives it a principled interpretation as the work of updating beliefs. Hale (2001) had proposed surprisal as a difficulty measure; Levy derived it and showed that it accounts for effects of syntactic expectation, such as why some relative clauses are harder than others.

The theory needs a probability model of language. Classic work used probabilistic grammars; today the best estimates come from neural language models, and their surprisal predicts reading times and the N400, an EEG response to unexpected words (Kutas and Hillyard, 1980), across many languages.

<Step n={2} title="A bigram model" minutes={25} />

The corpus below is a few dozen short sentences written for this lesson. A **bigram** model estimates $P(w_t \mid w_{t-1})$ from counts, smoothed so that unseen pairs are not impossible:

$$
P(w \mid v) = \frac{\text{count}(v, w) + \alpha}{\text{count}(v) + \alpha|V|}.
$$

The bigram model sees 'socks' after 'the' as almost impossible, 9 bits, against 3.5 for 'bread', and the full stop after 'socks' is surprising too, because 'socks' never appeared anywhere. But it is blind to the verb: after 'milked the', it finds 'cow' (4.8 bits) no more expected than 'bread' (3.5 bits), because it only sees 'the', and in this corpus 'the bread' is more common than 'the cow'. Any model that predicts from a fixed short window will miss expectations set further back in the sentence.

<Step n={3} title="More context" minutes={30} />

A trigram model conditions on two previous words. With little data most trigrams are unseen, so the estimate is **interpolated** with bigram and unigram estimates:

$$
P(w \mid u, v) = \lambda_3\,\hat P(w \mid u, v) + \lambda_2\,\hat P(w \mid v) + \lambda_1\,\hat P(w),
$$

with $\lambda_3 = 0.6$, $\lambda_2 = 0.3$, $\lambda_1 = 0.1$ here.

With two words of context, 'cow' after 'milked the' costs 0.7 bits and 'bread' 5.0: the verb now shapes the expectation for its object, as it does for human readers. The interpolation matters: almost every trigram in a real sentence is unseen in a small corpus, and without falling back on bigrams and unigrams most words would get probability zero. Neural language models solve the same problem by sharing statistical strength between similar contexts instead of counting exact word sequences.

The regression recovers a surprisal effect of about 21 ms per bit and a length effect of about 7 ms per letter, close to the 20 and 8 used to simulate the data. The two predictors are correlated (unexpected words in this set are often longer), which is why both must be included: leaving length out would inflate the surprisal coefficient. Real studies add word frequency, position in the sentence and spill-over from the previous word, and use mixed models with random effects for readers and items.

<Callout kind="research">

Two open questions keep this area active. First, the shape: Smith and Levy (2013) found reading time linear in surprisal over a wide range, but some studies find superlinear effects at high surprisal, which would favour different accounts of processing. Second, which model: surprisal from larger language models predicts reading times better up to a point, after which even better language models predict human reading worse, perhaps because they know rare facts that people do not (Oh and Schuler, 2023). What a 'human-like' predictor should be is not settled.
