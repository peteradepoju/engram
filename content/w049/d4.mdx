<Step n={1} title="The method" minutes={20} />

An **encoding model** predicts each brain measurement (a voxel, an electrode) as a weighted sum of features of the stimulus, here the activations of a language model's layer for each sentence. Because features are many and stimuli few, the weights are fitted by ridge regression (Week 36) and evaluated on held-out sentences by the correlation between predicted and measured responses. Two refinements make scores interpretable. The **noise ceiling** estimates how well any model could do, given that the measurements themselves are noisy: if the same sentence measured twice gives correlated but different responses, no model can exceed that reliability. **Normalised predictivity** divides the model's held-out correlation by the ceiling.

Schrimpf and colleagues found that transformer models such as GPT-2 reached normalised predictivity close to 1 in language-selective cortex, that untrained models did surprisingly well, and that across models the brain score correlated with next-word prediction performance but not with performance on other language benchmarks. They took this as evidence that the language network is shaped by predictive processing.

  r = np.random.default_rng(2021)
  n_sent, n_lat, n_vox = 200, 20, 50
  Z = r.normal(size=(n_sent, n_lat))                       # latent sentence properties
  B = r.normal(size=(n_lat, n_vox))
  signal = Z @ B / np.sqrt(n_lat)
  Y1 = signal + 1.0 * r.normal(size=signal.shape)          # repetition 1
  Y2 = signal + 1.0 * r.normal(size=signal.shape)          # repetition 2
  Y = (Y1 + Y2) / 2
  F_info = Z @ r.normal(size=(n_lat, 100)) + 0.5 * r.normal(size=(n_sent, 100))
  F_rand = r.normal(size=(n_sent, 100))

  def cv_score(F, Y, lam=10.0, k=5):
      folds = np.array_split(r.permutation(len(F)), k); pred = np.zeros_like(Y)
      for te in folds:
          tr = np.setdiff1d(np.arange(len(F)), te)
          Fm, Ym = F[tr].mean(0), Y[tr].mean(0)
          Ft, Yt = F[tr] - Fm, Y[tr] - Ym
          W = np.linalg.solve(Ft.T @ Ft + lam * np.eye(F.shape[1]), Ft.T @ Yt)
          pred[te] = (F[te] - Fm) @ W + Ym
      return np.array([np.corrcoef(pred[:, v], Y[:, v])[0, 1] for v in range(Y.shape[1])])

  for k_, s in score.items():
      print(f"{k_:11s}: median held-out r = {np.median(s):.3f}")

The informative model predicts held-out voxel responses with a median correlation of 0.69; the random model scores about zero, as it should, since cross-validation gives no credit for fitting noise. Without held-out testing the random model would look impressive: 100 features fitted to 200 sentences can match much of any response by chance. The ridge penalty and the train-test split are not optional details; they are what make the score mean anything.

<Step n={3} title="The noise ceiling" minutes={25} />

With two repetitions, the split-half correlation $r_{12}$ between them measures reliability. The reliability of their average is given by the Spearman-Brown formula, $\frac{2r_{12}}{1 + r_{12}}$, and the best correlation any model could reach with the averaged response is its square root.

The two repetitions of each voxel correlate at about 0.47, so the averaged response has a ceiling of about 0.80: no model can do better than that, because a fifth of the variance in the measured average is noise. The informative model's 0.69 is therefore 89% of what is achievable. A raw correlation of 0.3 could be excellent in noisy data with a ceiling of 0.35 and poor in clean data with a ceiling of 0.9, which is why a score without its ceiling is uninterpretable.

Across the eight simulated models, brain score rises with task score and the two correlate at 0.98. Here that relation is built in: both depend on the same quality parameter. That is exactly the interpretive problem of the real finding. A correlation across models between next-word prediction and brain predictivity is compatible with prediction shaping the brain's representations, but also with any property, such as size or training data, that improves both. Schrimpf and colleagues addressed some of these confounds; others remain open.

<Callout kind="warning">

A high brain score shows that a model's features contain the information that linearly predicts brain responses, not that the model computes as the brain does. Features that encode simple properties, such as word position or sentence length, can earn substantial scores (Kauf and colleagues, 2024, found that much of the predictivity of language models for fMRI depends on lexical semantic content rather than syntax), and the correlation with next-word prediction across models could arise from shared confounds such as model size. Controls, such as scrambling word order or matching models on size, are essential.

</Callout>
