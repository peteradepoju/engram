<Step n={1} title="Prediction as representation" minutes={20} />

For a policy that moves between states with transition matrix $T$, the **successor representation** (SR) is the expected discounted number of future visits to state $s'$ starting from $s$:

$$
M(s, s') = \mathbb E\Big[\sum_{t=0}^\infty \gamma^t\,\mathbb 1(s_t = s') \,\Big|\, s_0 = s\Big] = \big[(I - \gamma T)^{-1}\big]_{s s'}.
$$

Given rewards $\mathbf R$ at each state, values follow immediately as $\mathbf V = M\mathbf R$. The SR sits between model-free and model-based learning (Week 44): like model-free values it is cached and quick to use, and like a model it can re-plan instantly when rewards change, because only $\mathbf R$ must be relearned (Momennejad and colleagues, 2017, found such partial flexibility in human behaviour).

Stachenfeld and colleagues proposed that place cells encode columns of $M$: the cell for location $s'$ fires at $s$ in proportion to how much $s$ predicts future visits to $s'$. The prediction depends on the animal's behaviour, which explains why place fields change with experience, skew along routes and avoid barriers.

With an unbiased random walk the field is symmetric about state 25. With a rightward habit it still peaks at 25 but its mass shifts to 18.75: positions to the left strongly predict reaching 25 soon, while positions to the right, which the animal will rarely revisit, do not. Mehta, Quirk and Wilson (2000) found this backward expansion and skew of place fields as rats ran repeatedly in one direction, which they explained with STDP (Week 42); the SR explains it as learned prediction.

<Step n={3} title="Learning by temporal differences" minutes={25} />

The SR obeys a Bellman equation, $M(s, \cdot) = \mathbb 1_s + \gamma\,\mathbb E[M(s', \cdot)]$, so it can be learned from experience with a TD rule (Week 44) applied to whole rows:

$$
M(s, \cdot) \leftarrow M(s, \cdot) + \alpha\big(\mathbb 1_s + \gamma M(s', \cdot) - M(s, \cdot)\big).
$$

Replay of sequences during rest (Week 43) could speed it up by supplying extra transitions.

<Step n={4} title="Eigenvectors and grids" minutes={30} />

Stachenfeld and colleagues noted that the eigenvectors of the SR in an open environment are periodic patterns of increasing spatial frequency, like the Fourier modes of the room, and proposed that grid cells (Week 43) provide such a low-dimensional basis for the predictive map, a compressed representation from which the SR can be reconstructed. In a square room they look like stripes and checkerboards; Stachenfeld and colleagues found hexagonal patterns under some conditions, and their irregular eigenvectors in rooms with barriers predicted how grids distort in such rooms.

The leading non-constant eigenvectors change sign about 22 to 24 times across the rows and columns of the room: one or two broad waves. By eigenvector 10 there are 54 sign changes, and by eigenvector 20, 72. They form a ladder of periodic patterns, coarse to fine, like the modules of grid cells with their increasing frequencies. In a square box the patterns are stripes and checkerboards rather than hexagons; Stachenfeld and colleagues obtained hexagonal patterns only with further assumptions, such as non-negativity, which is one of the open points of the theory.

<Callout kind="research">

The SR has been extended well beyond space. Garvert, Dolan and Behrens (2017) found that the entorhinal cortex represents relations between objects learned from sequences, as an SR over a graph of objects would; Behrens and colleagues (2018) argued that the hippocampal-entorhinal system builds cognitive maps of any structured knowledge. The Tolman-Eichenbaum machine (Whittington and colleagues, 2020) learns grid-like and place-like codes by separating the structure of a task from its sensory details, and has been linked to transformers. Whether the brain's maps are SRs, models, or something else is an active question.

</Callout>
