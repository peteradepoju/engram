<Step n={2} title="Weekly challenge" minutes={60} />

**Part A: connector and provincial hubs.** Guimerà and Amaral (2005) classified nodes by their degree and their **participation coefficient**,

$$
P_i = 1 - \sum_{m}\left(\frac{k_{im}}{k_i}\right)^2,
$$

where $k_{im}$ is the number of node $i$'s links into module $m$. $P_i$ is near 0 if all links stay in one module and approaches $1 - 1/M$ if they spread evenly over $M$ modules. Build four modules of 30 nodes; add four extra high-degree nodes, two that link mostly inside one module (provincial hubs) and two that link evenly to all modules (connector hubs). Classify them.

All four added nodes have the same degree, 24, three times the typical node's, so degree alone cannot tell them apart. The participation coefficient can: the provincial hubs score 0, all their links inside their home module, and the connector hubs score 0.75, the maximum for four equal modules. In human connectomes, connector hubs sit mainly in association cortex and their damage has been linked to widespread cognitive deficits (Warren and colleagues, 2014, found that lesions to connector hubs disrupted the modular structure of the whole network).

After a single run, the SR agent's values match the new true values perfectly: it needed only the new reward vector, and its predictive map did the rest. The model-free agent's values still point to the old reward and are negatively correlated with the new ones: one pass of TD updates moved them only slightly, and its values near state 45 remain high. This rapid revaluation after a reward change, together with slow adjustment after a change in transitions, is the behavioural signature by which the SR was distinguished from both model-free and fully model-based learning.

<Prove title="1. The successor representation as a geometric series" task="Show that $\sum_{t=0}^\infty \gamma^t T^t = (I - \gamma T)^{-1}$ for a stochastic matrix $T$ and $0 \le \gamma \lt 1$, and hence that $M(s, s') = \mathbb E[\sum_t \gamma^t \mathbb 1(s_t = s')]$.">

$(T^t)_{ss'}$ is the probability of being in $s'$ after $t$ steps from $s$, so $\mathbb E[\sum_t \gamma^t\mathbb 1(s_t = s')] = \sum_t \gamma^t (T^t)_{ss'}$.

The eigenvalues of a stochastic matrix have modulus at most 1, so those of $\gamma T$ have modulus less than 1 and the series $\sum_t (\gamma T)^t$ converges.

Multiplying $S = \sum_t (\gamma T)^t$ by $I - \gamma T$ telescopes: $(I - \gamma T)S = I$, so $S = (I - \gamma T)^{-1}$.

</Prove>

<Prove title="2. Surprisal as belief change" task="A comprehender holds a distribution $P$ over complete sentences. When word $w$ arrives, sentences inconsistent with it are ruled out and the rest renormalised. Show that the relative entropy from the old to the new distribution equals $-\log P(w \mid \text{context})$.">

Let $A$ be the set of sentences consistent with the context and $w$, and $Z = P(A) = P(w \mid \text{context})$ (taking $P$ already conditioned on the context). The new distribution is $Q(x) = P(x)/Z$ for $x \in A$ and 0 otherwise.

$D(Q\,\|\,P) = \sum_{x \in A} Q(x)\log\frac{Q(x)}{P(x)} = \sum_{x\in A} Q(x)\log\frac1Z = -\log Z$.

So the amount the comprehender's beliefs must change is exactly the surprisal of the word, which is Levy's (2008) justification for surprisal as processing cost.

</Prove>
