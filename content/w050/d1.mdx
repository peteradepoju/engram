<Step n={1} title="The ten rules" minutes={20} />

Wilson and Collins's ten rules, in brief:

1. Design a good experiment: one whose behaviour depends on the processes you want to measure.
2. Design good models: candidate models that embody distinct hypotheses, including simple baselines.
3. Simulate, simulate, simulate: run the models on the task before collecting data, to see whether they make different predictions.
4. Fit the parameters, with care about local optima and bounds.
5. Check that parameters can be recovered (Week 44, Day 3).
6. Check that models can be recovered: data simulated from one model should be identified as coming from that model.
7. Collect data.
8. Validate the model: compare its simulated behaviour with the real data, not just its likelihood (tomorrow).
9. Analyse the winning model's latent variables, such as prediction errors.
10. Report everything, including the models that lost.

The rules are not new individually, but the paper made them a checklist, and journals and reviewers now expect them.

<Step n={2} title="Three models of choice" minutes={25} />

On a two-armed bandit with reward probabilities 0.2 and 0.8, swapped halfway through the 200 trials, consider three models:

- **Q-learning with softmax** (parameters $\alpha$, $\beta$): learns values and chooses the better one more often.
- **Win-stay lose-shift** with noise $\varepsilon$: repeats a rewarded choice and switches after no reward, each with probability $1 - \varepsilon/2$.
- **Biased random** (parameter $p$): chooses option 1 with fixed probability, ignoring outcomes.

The three models behave very differently on the same task: the learner tracks the better arm 94% of the time, switching soon after the reversal; win-stay lose-shift manages 72%, because it abandons the good arm after every unlucky trial; and the biased chooser stays near chance. The reversal matters. Without it, a learner that has settled on the good arm looks just like a chooser with a strong fixed bias, and the two models would be hard to separate; designing the task so that models predict different behaviour is Wilson and Collins's first rule.

Fitting each model from one starting point, as here, risks local optima; for real data, restart from several points and keep the best fit, Wilson and Collins's fourth rule.

With a reversal and 200 trials, every learner and every win-stay lose-shift chooser is recovered correctly, and 93% of biased choosers; the remaining few are mistaken for win-stay lose-shift with high noise, which behaves almost randomly. Two lessons follow. First, recovery depends on the design: run the same analysis without the reversal and learners with fast learning rates are often classified as biased choosers. Second, recovery depends on the parameters: models become confusable at the parameter values where they make similar predictions, so the ranges you simulate should match those you expect in real participants.

<Callout kind="note">

The confusion matrix answers "given that the data came from model A, how often do I select A?". The question you care about after an experiment is the reverse: "given that I selected A, how likely is it that A generated the data?". Wilson and Collins recommend computing this **inversion matrix** too, by normalising the confusion matrix down its columns, which requires assuming how common each model is among real participants.

</Callout>

<Key title="Before you go">
<ul>
<li>Simulate candidate models before collecting data.</li>
<li>Fit every model to every simulated dataset.</li>
<li>A confusion matrix shows which models your design can distinguish.</li>
<li>Parameters that make a model behave like another make them hard to separate.</li>
