<Step n={1} title="Comparison is not validation" minutes={20} />

Palminteri, Wyart and Koechlin made three points. **Relative model comparison**, by likelihood, AIC or BIC, picks the best of the models you thought of; if all are wrong, the winner is merely the least wrong, and its parameters can be distorted to compensate. **Absolute model falsification** asks whether the model can generate the data's key features: simulate it with its fitted parameters, analyse the simulated data exactly as you analysed the real data, and compare. And the features to check should be chosen in advance, as the qualitative signatures that distinguish the hypotheses, not just overall accuracy.

This is the same logic as the posterior predictive check of Bayesian statistics (Gelman and colleagues): if replicated data from the fitted model do not look like the observed data, the model is missing something.

<Step n={2} title="A behavioural signature" minutes={25} />

The task is a two-armed bandit with reward probabilities 0.3 and 0.7 that swap every 100 trials, for 400 trials. The simulated participant learns with $\alpha = 0.3$, chooses with $\beta = 3$, and has a perseveration bonus $\kappa = 1.5$ for the option chosen last. Two signatures summarise choice behaviour: the probability of repeating a choice after a win and after a loss.

The participant repeats 92% of choices after a win and, strikingly, 82% after a loss. A pure learner with these settings would switch more after losses, because each loss lowers the chosen option's value. The high stay-after-loss rate is the fingerprint of perseveration, and it is visible in the raw data before any model is fitted.

The perseveration model fits much better, by 38 units of negative log-likelihood, and recovers parameters close to the truth. The plain model compensates as best it can: its inverse temperature rises from 3 to 5, so it explains runs of repeated choices as unusually consistent value-based choice. If you had only the plain model, you would report this participant as an unusually decisive learner, and a group difference in such inflated values could be entirely spurious.

Simulated with its fitted parameters, the plain model stays after losses only 64% of the time, far below the participant's 82%: it cannot produce the key effect, and so it is falsified, whatever its likelihood. The perseveration model reproduces both signatures within a couple of percentage points. Note that the plain model gets the stay-after-win rate roughly right; a check based only on overall accuracy or win-stay would have missed its failure.

<Callout kind="think">

Which signatures you check determines which failures you can find. Stay probabilities caught missing perseveration; they would not catch a model with the wrong learning rate after reversals. Before fitting, write down the two or three behavioural effects that your theory exists to explain, and treat reproducing them as a requirement, not a bonus.

<Key title="Before you go">
<ul>
<li>Model comparison is relative; falsification is absolute.</li>
<li>Simulate fitted models and analyse the simulations like the data.</li>
<li>Check signatures chosen from the theory, such as stay after wins and losses.</li>
<li>Missing processes distort the parameters of the models that lack them.</li>
