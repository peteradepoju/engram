<Step n={1} title="Model evidence" minutes={20} />

For each participant $n$ and model $k$, the **log model evidence** $\ell_{nk} = \log p(\text{data}_n \mid \text{model}_k)$ measures how well the model, averaged over its parameters, predicts that participant's data. In practice it is approximated, for example by $-\text{BIC}/2$ or by a variational free energy. Differences in log evidence are log Bayes factors: a difference of 3 means the data are about 20 times more probable under one model.

<Step n={2} title="Fixed effects" minutes={20} />

If every participant used the same model, the group evidence would be the product of individual evidences, so log evidences would add:

$$
\ell_k^\text{group} = \sum_n \ell_{nk}.
$$

Summed across the group, model B wins by about 50 units of log evidence, a Bayes factor so large it would usually be called decisive. Yet 14 of the 20 participants favour model A. The fixed-effects comparison has answered the question "which single model best explains everyone's data together?", which is the wrong question when people differ: six participants with very strong evidence outvote fourteen with moderate evidence.

<Step n={3} title="Random effects" minutes={40} />

Stephan and colleagues assume each participant's model is drawn from a multinomial with frequencies $\mathbf r$, which have a Dirichlet prior with parameters $\boldsymbol\alpha_0$ (here all 1). Variational Bayes then alternates two steps until convergence:

$$
u_{nk} = \exp\big(\ell_{nk} + \psi(\alpha_k) - \psi(\textstyle\sum_j \alpha_j)\big), \qquad g_{nk} = \frac{u_{nk}}{\sum_j u_{nj}}, \qquad \alpha_k = \alpha_{0k} + \sum_n g_{nk},
$$

where $\psi$ is the digamma function and $g_{nk}$ is the posterior probability that participant $n$ used model $k$. The posterior over frequencies is Dirichlet($\boldsymbol\alpha$), so the expected frequency of model $k$ is $\alpha_k/\sum_j\alpha_j$.

solution={String.raw`
  def rfx_bms(lme, iters=100):
      a0 = np.ones(lme.shape[1]); a = a0.copy()
      for _ in range(iters):
          lu = lme + digamma(a) - digamma(a.sum())
          lu -= lu.max(1, keepdims=True)
          u = np.exp(lu); g = u / u.sum(1, keepdims=True)
          a = a0 + g.sum(0)
      return a, g

  alpha, g = rfx_bms(lme)
  freq = alpha / alpha.sum()
  print(f"Dirichlet parameters {np.round(alpha, 2)}; expected frequencies A {freq[0]:.3f}, B {freq[1]:.3f}")

Random-effects selection estimates that about two thirds of the population use model A (expected frequency 0.66) and a third model B, with each participant confidently assigned to the model it favours. The strength of each participant's evidence now matters only for how sure we are about that participant's assignment, not for how much that participant counts. The Dirichlet prior with $\alpha_0 = 1$ pulls the frequencies slightly towards equality, which is why the estimate is 0.66 rather than 14/20 = 0.70.

<Step n={4} title="Exceedance probabilities" minutes={25} />

The **exceedance probability** of model $k$ is the posterior probability that it is more frequent in the population than every other model, $P(r_k \gt r_j\ \forall j \neq k)$. With a Dirichlet posterior it is easily estimated by sampling.

With all 20 participants, the posterior probability that model A is more common than B is 0.94; with five participants, four favouring A, it is 0.88, lower because the frequencies are less certain. Exceedance probabilities answer a comparative question about the population, not "how many use A", which is why they should be reported with the expected frequencies, and preferably in their protected form (the warning below).

<Callout kind="warning">

Exceedance probabilities can be overconfident: they never reach 0.5 for all models even when there is no evidence of any difference, because some model is always more frequent in each sample. Rigoux, Stephan, Friston and Daunizeau (2014) introduced the **protected exceedance probability**, which accounts for the possibility that all models are equally frequent; report it, along with expected frequencies, rather than exceedance probabilities alone.

</Callout>
