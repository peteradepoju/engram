<Step n={1} title="The first-passage time density" minutes={20} />

For a Wiener process with drift $v$, unit noise, bounds at 0 and $a$, and starting point $z = wa$, the density of first reaching the lower bound at time $t$ is

$$
f(t \mid v, a, w) = \frac{1}{a^2}\exp\!\Big(-vaw - \frac{v^2t}{2}\Big)\,\pi\sum_{k=1}^\infty k\,\exp\!\Big(-\frac{k^2\pi^2 t}{2a^2}\Big)\sin(k\pi w).
$$

The density at the upper bound is obtained by replacing $v$ with $-v$ and $w$ with $1 - w$. The series converges quickly for large $t$ and slowly for small $t$; Navarro and Fuss derived how many terms guarantee a given accuracy and combined it with a second series that converges fast for small times. Using about 60 terms of this large-time series is accurate enough for the response times in this lesson. Observed response times add a non-decision time $t_0$: the density of an observed time $T$ is $f(T - t_0)$.

  import numpy as np
  import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
  from scipy.optimize import minimize
  r = np.random.default_rng(2009)

  def fpt_lower(t, v, a, w, K=60):
      u = t / a ** 2
      k = np.arange(1, K + 1)[:, None]
      s = np.pi * (k * np.exp(-k ** 2 * np.pi ** 2 * u / 2) * np.sin(k * np.pi * w)).sum(0)
      return np.exp(-v * a * w - v ** 2 * t / 2) / a ** 2 * s

  def simulate(v, a, t0, n=1500, dt=0.001):
      x = np.full(n, a / 2); done = np.zeros(n, bool); ch = np.zeros(n, int); rt = np.zeros(n); k = 0
      while not done.all() and k < 20000:
          idx = ~done; x[idx] += v * dt + np.sqrt(dt) * r.normal(size=idx.sum()); k += 1
          up, lo = idx & (x >= a), idx & (x <= 0)
          ch[up] = 1; rt[up | lo] = k * dt + t0; done |= up | lo
      return ch, rt

  ch, rt = simulate(1.2, 1.5, 0.3)
  tt = np.arange(0.0005, 6, 0.001)

  print(f"integrated lower density {area_lower:.4f}; analytic {p_lower_theory:.4f}; simulated error rate {1 - ch.mean():.4f}")
  plt.hist(rt[ch == 1] - 0.3, 60, density=True, alpha=0.4, label="simulated correct")

The lower-bound density integrates to 0.1415, against the analytic 0.1419 and a simulated error rate of 0.142: the series is right, and the small shortfall comes from the grid starting just above zero and stopping at 6 s. Normalised, the upper-bound density traces the histogram of simulated correct response times, including its sharp rise and long tail. A density that integrates to less than 1 at each bound, with the two adding to 1, is called defective; the defect at each bound is the probability of that choice.

Starting from a deliberately poor guess, Nelder-Mead recovers drift 1.205, boundary 1.499 and non-decision time 0.306, all within a few per cent of the truth from 1,500 trials. The non-decision time is constrained to lie below the fastest response, since no response can be faster than the non-decision time; this hard edge is one reason fast outliers are so damaging to diffusion model fits.

The model's quantiles for correct responses lie within about 15 ms of the data's at every level from the 10th to the 90th percentile. A good likelihood does not guarantee this: if the data had slow errors, or a contaminant distribution, the simple model could fit the bulk well and miss a tail. Quantile plots for correct and error responses, side by side, are the standard visual check, and they correspond to the posterior predictive checks of Day 2.

<Callout kind="note">

In practice, use a tested implementation, such as the Navarro and Fuss approximation in PyDDM, HDDM or the ddm functions of R packages, and fit the full model with across-trial variabilities when the data demand it (Week 45). Response time outliers, such as very fast guesses or very slow lapses, can distort a likelihood fit badly; a mixture with a uniform contaminant distribution, or trimming fixed in advance, protects against them.

</Callout>
