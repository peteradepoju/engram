<Step n={1} title="The model" minutes={15} />

In each trial $t$ the mouse is in one of $K$ hidden states $z_t$. In state $k$ it chooses right with probability

$$
P(y_t = 1 \mid z_t = k) = \sigma(\mathbf w_k^\top\mathbf x_t),
$$

a logistic regression on inputs $\mathbf x_t$ (here the signed stimulus contrast and a constant for bias). States follow a Markov chain with transition matrix $A$, $A_{jk} = P(z_{t+1} = k \mid z_t = j)$. Ashwood and colleagues fitted this model to mice from the International Brain Laboratory (Weeks 2 and 3) and to mice in other tasks, and found that three states described most animals: one engaged, and two biased towards left or right. The states were persistent, with expected dwell times of many trials, and differed in response times as well as in choice, supporting the interpretation that they reflect internal states rather than fitting artefacts.

<Step n={3} title="Forward-backward" minutes={35} />

Given the parameters, the posterior probability of each state at each trial combines what came before and what came after. The **forward** pass computes $\alpha_t(k) \propto P(z_t = k, y_{1:t})$ recursively, normalising at each step; the **backward** pass computes $\beta_t(k) \propto P(y_{t+1:T} \mid z_t = k)$; their product, normalised, is $\gamma_t(k) = P(z_t = k \mid y_{1:T})$.

  def forward_backward(X, y, W, A, pi):
      P = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-X @ W.T))
      like = np.where(y[:, None] == 1, P, 1 - P)
      T_, K = like.shape
      al = np.zeros((T_, K)); c = np.zeros(T_)
      al[0] = pi * like[0]; c[0] = al[0].sum(); al[0] /= c[0]
      for t in range(1, T_):
          al[t] = (al[t - 1] @ A) * like[t]; c[t] = al[t].sum(); al[t] /= c[t]
      be = np.ones((T_, K))
      for t in range(T_ - 2, -1, -1):
          be[t] = (A @ (like[t + 1] * be[t + 1])) / c[t + 1]
      g = al * be; g /= g.sum(1, keepdims=True)
      return g, c, al, be, like

With the true parameters, the most probable state matches the true state on 94% of trials. The posterior is confident during long runs and uncertain near switches, where a few ambiguous choices could belong to either state; trials with strong stimuli are the most informative, because only there do the two states predict different choices. On zero-contrast trials, choices say little about the state, and the posterior relies on the states' persistence.

<Step n={4} title="Learning by EM" minutes={30} />

Expectation maximisation alternates the forward-backward pass (E-step) with updates of the parameters (M-step): the transition matrix from expected transition counts, $\xi$, and each state's weights by a weighted logistic regression in which trial $t$ counts with weight $\gamma_t(k)$.

      g0 = np.random.default_rng(seed)
      W = g0.normal(0, 1, (K, X.shape[1])); A = np.full((K, K), 0.05 / (K - 1)); np.fill_diagonal(A, 0.95); pi = np.ones(K) / K
      for it in range(iters):
          g, c, al, be, like = forward_backward(X, y, W, A, pi)
          xi = np.zeros((K, K))
          for t in range(len(y) - 1):
              xi += al[t][:, None] * A * (like[t + 1] * be[t + 1])[None] / c[t + 1]
          A = xi / xi.sum(1, keepdims=True)
          pi = g[0]
          for k in range(K):
              for _ in range(5):

From random starting weights, 40 iterations recover the engaged state's stimulus weight (4.07 against 4) and the disengaged state's weak stimulus weight and right bias (0.41 and 1.57 against 0.5 and 1.5), and the transition matrix is close to the truth. The fitted model decodes states as well as the true one, and its log-likelihood is slightly higher, as a fitted model's should be. Ordering states by stimulus weight after fitting resolves the labelling ambiguity: EM does not know which state is 'engaged'.

<Callout kind="warning">

EM finds a local maximum of the likelihood, so fit from several random starts and keep the best. The number of states must be chosen, by cross-validated likelihood on held-out sessions, as Ashwood and colleagues did; more states always fit training data better. And states are defined by choice policies alone: a "disengaged" state may be lapses of attention, exploration, a strategy based on previous trials, or a mixture, and other measures (response times, pupil, neural activity) are needed to interpret it.

</Callout>
