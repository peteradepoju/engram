On the 1,000 test trials, one state reaches a log-likelihood of about -0.567 per trial and two states about -0.497, a gain of 0.07 nats per trial, or roughly 70 nats over the test block: overwhelming evidence for a second state. The third state brings the test likelihood to the same value to four decimal places. Its extra weights and transitions fit the training trials slightly better, but they describe noise rather than structure, so nothing carries over to new trials. Two states it is, which is the true number. With real mice Ashwood and colleagues made the same choice by the same method, and found that three or four states were usually justified because real animals have more than one way of disengaging.

All twenty participants are assigned to the model that generated their data, which is the payoff for Day 1's reversal design: without it, Q-learners with low learning rates look like biased choosers. Random-effects selection estimates the population frequencies at about 0.65 for Q-learning, 0.30 for win-stay lose-shift and 0.04 for the bias model, against true proportions of 0.70, 0.30 and 0. The estimates are shrunk towards equal frequencies by the Dirichlet prior, which is why they never reach zero for a model nobody uses. This is the shape of a result you should report: frequencies with their uncertainty, not a single winner.

<Prove title="1. The forward recursion" task="Show that $P(z_t = k, y_{1:t}) = \text{like}_t(k)\sum_j P(z_{t-1} = j, y_{1:t-1})A_{jk}$, and explain why normalising at each step gives the log-likelihood as $\sum_t \log c_t$.">

By the Markov property, $P(z_t = k, y_{1:t}) = P(y_t \mid z_t = k)\sum_j P(z_t = k \mid z_{t-1} = j)\,P(z_{t-1} = j, y_{1:t-1})$, which is the stated recursion with $\text{like}_t(k) = P(y_t \mid z_t = k)$.

If at each step the vector is divided by its sum $c_t$, then $c_t = P(y_t \mid y_{1:t-1})$, because the unnormalised sum is $P(y_t, \cdot \mid y_{1:t-1})$ summed over states.

So $\log P(y_{1:T}) = \sum_t \log P(y_t \mid y_{1:t-1}) = \sum_t \log c_t$, which avoids the numerical underflow of multiplying thousands of probabilities.

</Prove>

<Prove title="2. Why fixed effects can be dominated" task="With two models and log evidence differences $d_n = \ell_{nA} - \ell_{nB}$, show that fixed effects favours A if and only if $\sum_n d_n \gt 0$, and construct a group in which most $d_n$ are positive but A loses.">

Fixed effects compares $\sum_n \ell_{nA}$ with $\sum_n \ell_{nB}$, which is the sign of $\sum_n d_n$.

Take 14 participants with $d_n = 3$ and 6 with $d_n = -15$. Then 14 of 20 favour A but $\sum_n d_n = 42 - 90 = -48 \lt 0$, so fixed effects favours B with a Bayes factor of $e^{48}$.

The sum weights each participant by the strength of their evidence, so a minority with strong evidence outweighs a majority with moderate evidence. Random-effects selection instead weights each participant equally in estimating frequencies.

</Prove>
