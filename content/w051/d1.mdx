<Step n={1} title="The project and the task" minutes={20} />

A mouse is head-fixed in front of three screens, its front paws on a small wheel. A grating appears on the left or the right screen, and the mouse turns the wheel to move it to the centre. A correct turn earns a drop of water; a wrong one earns a burst of noise and a longer wait. The grating's contrast varies from trial to trial: 100%, 25%, 12.5%, 6.25% or 0%, where nothing is shown and the mouse is rewarded at random for one side. Burgess and colleagues (2017) developed this task at University College London, and the International Brain Laboratory (IBL) standardised it so that it could be run identically in laboratories on three continents.

Once trained, the mice move on to a **biased** version. Each session starts with 90 trials in which both sides are equally likely. After that, trials come in blocks in which the stimulus is on the left 80% of the time, alternating with blocks in which it is on the right 80% of the time. Blocks last between 20 and 100 trials, and nothing signals a switch. A mouse that tracks which block it is in can do better on hard trials, and at zero contrast it can do better than chance only by using that knowledge.

The data are from the IBL's 2019 public release, which accompanied their 2021 paper on reproducibility (Week 3's deep read). Engram's file holds the first five biased sessions (with at least 400 trials each) of two mice from each of the nine laboratories: 18 mice, 90 sessions and 70,402 trials. For every trial it records the signed contrast (negative on the left, positive on the right), the choice (1 for right, 0 for left, −1 when the mouse made no choice within the time limit), whether it was rewarded, the block's probability of a left stimulus, and the time from stimulus onset to the response.

Accuracy climbs from 73% at 6.25% contrast to 94.5% at full contrast, and the median response time falls from 415 to 284 ms: harder trials are slower, as the diffusion models of Week 45 predict. Two numbers deserve a second look. Even at full contrast the mice are wrong on more than 5% of trials, the lapses you will meet again on Day 5. <Step n={3} title="Psychometric curves by block" minutes={35} />

In Week 37 you fitted a psychometric function with lapses to simulated IBL choices. Now fit it to the real ones. The probability of choosing right at signed contrast $c$ is

$$
P(\text{right}\mid c) = \gamma + (1 - \gamma - \lambda)\,\Phi\!\left(\frac{c - \mu}{\sigma}\right),
$$

where $\Phi$ is the standard normal cumulative distribution, $\mu$ is the bias (the contrast at which the mouse is indifferent, apart from lapses), $\sigma$ the threshold, and $\gamma$ and $\lambda$ the lapse rates on the left and right. If a mouse uses the block, its curve should move: in left blocks it needs more evidence to choose right, so $\mu$ should be larger.

Every one of the 18 mice moves its curve towards the likely side, by a median of 0.066 in contrast units: roughly the size of the weakest visible stimulus, 0.0625. The shifts vary more than tenfold, from 0.009 for SWC_013 to 0.228 for KS003, so the mice differ a great deal in how much they rely on the prior. That variation is something the models of Days 3 and 4 will need to explain. The answer to the prediction is in the figure: at zero contrast, mice choose right on 61% of trials in right blocks and 37% in left blocks, far less than the 80% that the block would justify if the mice knew it perfectly. A mouse that always chose the likely side at zero contrast would earn more reward, so either the mice are uncertain which block they are in, or they do not use what they know in the optimal way. Telling these apart is the core of the project.

<Step n={4} title="Response times" minutes={20} />

A prior can enter a decision in two ways you met in Week 45. It can move the **starting point** of evidence accumulation towards the likely bound, or it can add a constant **drift** towards the likely side. With a starting-point shift, choices towards the likely side are reached sooner, so at zero contrast they should be faster than choices against it. With a pure drift bias and an unbiased starting point, the diffusion model makes a surprising prediction: the two choices have identical response time distributions, however unequal their probabilities. Check which pattern the mice show.

At zero contrast, choices that agree with the block take a median of 435 ms and choices against it 535 ms, and 16 of the 18 mice show the same ordering. This is the signature of a starting-point shift. Hold it loosely, though: 13.8% of all responses take longer than 2 s, a tail that no simple diffusion model produces, and part of the difference comes from these slow trials, when a mouse may not be engaged with the task at all. The response times recorded here also include the time to turn the wheel, not only the time to decide. For these reasons the rest of the project models choices, and treats response times as a check rather than as data to fit.
