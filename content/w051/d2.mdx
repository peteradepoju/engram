<Step n={1} title="Set up and the idea" minutes={15} />

Load the data as on Day 1. Two new arrays help: the choice coded as +1 for right and −1 for left (0 for no choice), and a function that shifts any array back by $k$ trials, filling with zeros where the earlier trial belongs to a different session. Without that, the last trial of one session would wrongly predict the first trial of the next.

<PyCell code={String.raw`
  import numpy as np
  import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
  d = np.load(dataset("ibl_biased.npz"))
  mouse, session = d["mouse"].astype(int), d["session"].astype(int)
  contrast, choice, reward = d["contrast"].astype(float), d["choice"].astype(float), d["reward"].astype(int)
  p_left, rt = d["p_left"].astype(float).round(2), d["rt"].astype(float)
  ok = (choice >= 0) & d["included"] & np.isfinite(rt)
  sess = 10 * mouse + session                       # a unique id for each session
  c_pm = np.where(choice >= 0, 2 * choice - 1, 0.0) # +1 right, -1 left, 0 no choice
  f_c = np.tanh(5 * contrast) / np.tanh(5)          # the IBL's compressed contrast regressor

  def lag(x, k):
      """x shifted back by k trials, with zeros where trial t - k is in another session."""
      out = np.zeros_like(x, dtype=float); out[k:] = x[:-k]
      same = np.r_[np.zeros(k, bool), sess[k:] == sess[:-k]]
      return np.where(same, out, 0.0)

  def logreg(X, y, l2=1e-3):
      """Logistic regression by Newton's method with a tiny ridge penalty."""
      w = np.zeros(X.shape[1])
      for _ in range(25):
          p = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-X @ w))
          g = X.T @ (y - p) - l2 * w
          H = -(X * (p * (1 - p))[:, None]).T @ X - l2 * np.eye(len(w))
          w -= np.linalg.solve(H, g)
      return w
  print("choices on the first six trials:", c_pm[:6], "\nthe same, one trial back:", lag(c_pm, 1)[:6])
`} />

The stimulus enters through $\tanh(5c)/\tanh(5)$, the transformation the IBL used in their own GLMs. It compresses contrast so that 25% and 100% have similar effects, which matches the psychometric curves of Day 1 better than contrast itself.

The model is the logistic regression of Week 35, now with behaviour as the output:

$$
P(\text{right}_t) = \sigma\!\left(w_c f(c_t) + w_r\, r_{t-1} + w_u\, u_{t-1} + b\right),
$$

where $r_{t-1}$ is the previous choice ($\pm 1$) if it was rewarded and 0 otherwise, and $u_{t-1}$ the previous choice if it was not. Splitting the previous choice by its outcome separates two ideas. A learner that tracks the stimulus side should repeat a rewarded choice ($w_r \gt 0$) but switch after an error ($w_u \lt 0$), because an error means the stimulus was on the other side. A mouse that simply repeats its actions should have both weights positive.

Every mouse repeats a rewarded choice, with a mean weight of 0.67. The surprise is the error trials: 17 of the 18 mice also tend to repeat a choice that was *not* rewarded, with a mean weight of 0.50, which answers the prediction. That is the opposite of what a learner tracking the stimulus side would do. After an error, the stimulus was on the other side, which is evidence for the other block. The mice instead behave as though their own previous action, rewarded or not, pulls the next choice towards it. Only SWC_013 switches after errors, and it is also the mouse with the smallest block shift on Day 1. Hold on to this observation: it is the clue that decides tomorrow's model comparison.

<Step n={3} title="Does history predict a new session?" minutes={25} />

History improves prediction on the held-out session for 17 of the 18 mice, by a median of 0.032 nats per trial. That sounds small, but over a session of 800 trials it is about 26 nats, a likelihood ratio of more than $10^{11}$ in favour of the model with history. The gains vary widely: ZM_1743 gains 0.14 nats per trial, while CSH_ZAD_002 gains nothing. These are also among the mice with the most and least history-dependent choices, so the individual differences are consistent across analyses, which is what you would want before interpreting them.

<Step n={4} title="How far back?" minutes={30} />

The block lasts tens of trials, so a mouse that tracks it should be influenced by more than the last trial. Replace the two one-trial regressors by the last ten choices, each with its own weight. The weights as a function of lag form a **history kernel**, the behavioural counterpart of the spike-history filters of Week 35.

The weights fall from 0.39 one trial back to 0.14 four trials back. Over the first six lags each step multiplies the weight by about 0.72, so $\alpha \approx 0.28$, and the mice's memory of their choices has a timescale of about 3.6 trials. The tail, though, is flatter than an exponential: lags 7 to 10 still carry weights of 0.06 to 0.1. A single exponential is therefore an approximation, and a slower component may be tracking the block itself. Ten free weights per mouse are also a lot to estimate. A model that summarises the kernel by a single rate $\alpha$ and a single weight would be far more economical, and it is the first of the models you will compare tomorrow.
