Suppose a mouse estimates the block by a leaky average of past stimulus sides, $\pi_t = (1 - \alpha)\pi_{t-1} + \alpha s_{t-1}$. Within a block, the sides are random, $+1$ with probability 0.8, so the average is noisy: its variance is $\alpha/(2 - \alpha)$ times the variance of a single side. A small $\alpha$ averages over many trials and is precise. But after a switch the average approaches its new value only as $(1 - \alpha)^n$, taking about $1/\alpha$ trials, so a small $\alpha$ is slow. The best $\alpha$ balances the two, and it depends on how often the world changes. Glaze, Kable and Gold (2015) showed that people adjust their integration timescale in just this way when the rate of change, the hazard rate, is varied.

The loss is lowest at $\alpha = 0.25$, a memory of about four trials, and is nearly flat between 0.2 and 0.3. That answers the prediction, and it may surprise you: with blocks of 50 trials, the best leaky average remembers only four or five. A longer memory would be more precise within a block, but each block switch would leave it on the wrong side for many trials, and with only 80% of stimuli on the likely side the precision gained is not worth the lag. The mice's rates, the pink lines, cluster around this optimum, with a median of 0.21. So the mice forget at almost exactly the right rate for a leaky average. What they average is a different matter: the Bayesian observer, which uses the task's structure, still predicts better than the best leaky average by 0.009 nats per trial, and the mice average their actions, not the stimuli.

At lag 0 the curve jumps to 0.42, not because the mice anticipate the switch but because the first trial of the new block already has its stimulus drawn from the new side, and 6.25% stimuli are partly visible. That is a lesson in itself: the switch-aligned curve, the figure most papers on this task show, cannot tell the action kernel from the Bayesian observer, while the likelihood, which uses every trial, separates them by about 1,960 nats. Average curves hide trial-by-trial structure, here the effect of the mouse's own errors, that the models disagree about. Note, too, the dip after lag 20: by then some blocks have already switched back, so the curve mixes blocks.

An oracle that knew the block would earn 80.2% at zero contrast. The Bayesian observer, which must infer the block from the stimuli, reaches 73.9%. Choosing by the sign of each mouse's own action kernel would give 66.6%, and the mice actually earn 57.4%. The largest single loss, about nine percentage points, is the last step: the mice do not choose the side their own prior favours on every zero-contrast trial, but only somewhat more often than not. Their prior weight is modest, so they behave probabilistically, close to matching rather than maximising. The second-largest loss, about seven points, comes from averaging actions rather than inferring the block from the stimuli. The forgetting rate itself, as Step 2 showed, costs almost nothing. One caution: this analysis treats a correct choice at zero contrast as reward earned, but in the experiment the mice cannot know that these trials are different, and a strategy tuned to the whole session may sensibly weigh the prior less.
