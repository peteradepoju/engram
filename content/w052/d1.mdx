<Step n={2} title="Recap sheet" minutes={20} />

**Scales and methods (Week 28).** The brain spans nine orders of magnitude in space, from ion channels (nanometres) to long-range pathways (centimetres), and as many in time. Every method trades resolution against coverage: electrodes and Neuropixels record spikes from hundreds of neurons, calcium imaging thousands with slower signals, fMRI and EEG whole brains indirectly.

**Membranes (Weeks 29 and 30).** The Nernst potential of an ion is $E = \frac{RT}{zF}\ln\frac{[\text{out}]}{[\text{in}]}$, about $26.7\ln\frac{[\text{out}]}{[\text{in}]}$ mV at body temperature for a monovalent cation. The resting potential, by the GHK equation, weights each ion by its permeability. A patch of membrane is an RC circuit with time constant $\tau = RC$. Hodgkin and Huxley's model adds voltage-dependent sodium and potassium conductances with gates $m^3h$ and $n^4$, each relaxing towards a voltage-dependent steady state.

**Simplified neurons (Week 31).** The leaky integrate-and-fire neuron, $\tau\dot V = -(V - E_L) + RI$, with a reset, fires periodically above rheobase with period $\tau\ln\frac{V_\infty - V_r}{V_\infty - V_\text{th}}$, where $V_\infty = E_L + RI$. Adaptation currents, exponential spike initiation (AdEx) and two-variable models (Izhikevich, FitzHugh-Nagumo) add realism at low cost.

**Dendrites (Week 32).** The cable equation gives a length constant $\lambda = \sqrt{r_m/r_i}$; signals decay and slow as they spread, and active dendrites can turn branches into nonlinear subunits.

**Synapses (Week 33).** A conductance-based synapse injects $g(t)(E_\text{syn} - V)$, so its effect depends on the voltage; inhibition near rest shunts. Short-term depression and facilitation (Tsodyks and Markram) make the response to each spike depend on recent history, so synapses filter rates.

Potassium's reversal potential is −89 mV and sodium's +61 mV; chloride's, −64 mV, sits near rest. With permeabilities in the ratio 1 : 0.05 : 0.45 the membrane rests at −64.9 mV, between $E_K$ and $E_\text{Na}$ but much closer to $E_K$, because potassium dominates. Without chloride, a sodium permeability only 3.6% of potassium's is enough to pull the resting potential up from −89 to −70 mV. That is why small changes in sodium permeability, the first event of every spike, move the voltage so far (Week 29).

Without adaptation, the simulation agrees with the formula to within about 1 Hz (spike counts in half a second are whole numbers, so rates come in steps of 2 Hz), and the neuron is silent below its rheobase of 2 nA. With an adaptation current that grows by 0.5 nA at each spike and decays over 200 ms, steady rates fall about fourfold and the f-I curve becomes much flatter. The steady amplitude falls from 0.42 at 1 Hz to 0.024 at 50 Hz and halves, relative to the first spike, at only 3.1 Hz: with recovery taking 800 ms, the synapse is depressed at almost any natural rate. The product of amplitude and rate, the average drive per second, rises from 0.42 to about 1.2 and then saturates. Above about 10 Hz the synapse delivers nearly the same drive whatever the presynaptic rate, so a steady rate is barely transmitted, while a sudden change in rate is: the first spikes after a change see recovered resources. Tsodyks and Markram (Week 33) called this transmission of rate changes rather than rates, and it is the synaptic counterpart of the adaptation in Problem 2.

<Prove title="The firing period of a leaky integrate-and-fire neuron" task="For $\tau\dot V = -(V - E_L) + RI$ with constant $I$, threshold $V_\text{th}$ and reset $V_r$, show that the time from reset to threshold is $T = \tau\ln\frac{V_\infty - V_r}{V_\infty - V_\text{th}}$ with $V_\infty = E_L + RI$, and that the rate grows like $RI/(\tau(V_\text{th} - V_r))$ for large currents.">

The equation is $\tau\dot V = V_\infty - V$, so $V(t) = V_\infty + (V_r - V_\infty)e^{-t/\tau}$ after a reset at $t = 0$.

Setting $V(T) = V_\text{th}$ gives $e^{-T/\tau} = \frac{V_\infty - V_\text{th}}{V_\infty - V_r}$, so $T = \tau\ln\frac{V_\infty - V_r}{V_\infty - V_\text{th}}$. This is finite only if $V_\infty \gt V_\text{th}$, which defines the rheobase $I = (V_\text{th} - E_L)/R$.

For large $V_\infty$, write $\frac{V_\infty - V_r}{V_\infty - V_\text{th}} = 1 + \frac{V_\text{th} - V_r}{V_\infty - V_\text{th}}$ and use $\ln(1 + x) \approx x$: $T \approx \tau\frac{V_\text{th} - V_r}{V_\infty - V_\text{th}}$, so the rate $1/T$ grows linearly with the current, with slope $R/(\tau(V_\text{th} - V_r))$. Near rheobase the rate rises steeply, as in Problem 2.
