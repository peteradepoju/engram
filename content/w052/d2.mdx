<Step n={1} title="Encoding and decoding in one picture" minutes={15} />

An encoding model says how a stimulus produces spikes; a decoder runs the model backwards. How well any unbiased decoder can do is limited by the Fisher information, which depends on how steeply the tuning curves change with the stimulus, not on how strongly the neurons fire at their peak.

<Step n={2} title="Recap sheet" minutes={20} />

**Spike trains (Week 34).** A PSTH averages spikes over trials; a tuning curve averages over time for each stimulus. A Poisson process has exponential intervals, a coefficient of variation (CV) of 1 and a Fano factor of 1. For any renewal process the Fano factor in long windows approaches $\text{CV}^2$. Real cortical neurons are often close to Poisson in variability, and noise correlations between them shape what a population can encode.

**Encoding models (Week 35).** The LNP model filters the stimulus linearly, passes the result through a nonlinearity and draws Poisson spikes. The Poisson GLM, $\lambda_t = \exp(\mathbf k \cdot \mathbf x_t + \mathbf h \cdot \mathbf y_{\text{past}} + b)$, adds a spike-history filter that captures refractoriness, bursting and adaptation; its log-likelihood is concave, so Newton's method finds the maximum. The spike-triggered average estimates the linear filter for white-noise stimuli.

**Decoding (Week 36).** The population vector, linear decoders, Bayesian decoders and the Kalman filter read stimuli or movements from populations. The Fisher information of independent Poisson neurons is $I(s) = T\sum_i f_i'(s)^2/f_i(s)$, and no unbiased decoder can have variance below $1/I(s)$.

**Real data (Week 37).** NWB files and DANDI share recordings; quality metrics (refractory violations, presence ratio) filter units; analyses must respect the nesting of trials in sessions in animals, or they pseudoreplicate.

Both trains have the same rate, but their counts differ greatly in reliability. The Poisson train has a CV and Fano factor of 1.0; the gamma train has a CV of 0.50 and a Fano factor of 0.25, its CV squared, which answers the prediction. Regular intervals make counts four times less variable than Poisson counts at the same rate, which would quadruple the Fisher information of a rate code. Cortical neurons in vivo usually have Fano factors near or above 1, which is why the Poisson model works as well as it does; the drop in Fano factor at stimulus onset (Week 34) is one of the few places where they become more regular.

The fitted weights are within 0.07 of the true ones: a stimulus weight of 0.78 (true 0.8), and history weights of −3.02, −1.57, −0.60 and −0.23 one to four bins back, which recover the refractory period and its recovery. Adding the five history terms raises the log-likelihood by 230 for only 1,818 spikes. Without them the GLM would have to explain the gaps after each spike as chance, and its stimulus weight would be biased. This is the same logic as Week 51's history GLM of mouse choices: past outputs are among the most informative regressors of any system with memory.

The Fisher information at 10 degrees is 0.182 per degree squared, so no unbiased decoder can achieve a standard deviation below 2.34 degrees. The maximum-likelihood decoder is essentially unbiased (mean 9.95) with a standard deviation of 2.44 degrees: its variance is only 8% above the bound. Maximum likelihood is asymptotically efficient, and 40 neurons in half a second, with about 20 spikes from the most responsive cells, are already enough to come close. With fewer spikes, the decoder would fall further from the bound, and with correlated noise the bound itself would change (Week 34).

<Prove title="The Fisher information of a Poisson population" task="Neurons $i = 1, \dots, N$ fire independent Poisson counts $n_i$ with means $T f_i(s)$. Show that the Fisher information about $s$ is $I(s) = T\sum_i f_i'(s)^2/f_i(s)$.">

The log-likelihood is $\ell(s) = \sum_i \left[n_i\log(Tf_i(s)) - Tf_i(s) - \log n_i!\right]$.

Its derivative is $\ell'(s) = \sum_i \left[n_i\frac{f_i'}{f_i} - Tf_i'\right] = \sum_i \frac{f_i'}{f_i}(n_i - Tf_i)$.

The Fisher information is the variance of the score: $I(s) = \text{Var}(\ell'(s)) = \sum_i \left(\frac{f_i'}{f_i}\right)^2\text{Var}(n_i)$, because the neurons are independent and the score has mean zero.

For Poisson counts $\text{Var}(n_i) = Tf_i$, so $I(s) = \sum_i \frac{f_i'^2}{f_i^2}Tf_i = T\sum_i\frac{f_i'(s)^2}{f_i(s)}$. Neurons at the peak of their tuning, where $f_i' = 0$, contribute nothing; the flanks carry the information.
