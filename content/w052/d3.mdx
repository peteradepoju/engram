<Step n={1} title="Stabilising Hebb" minutes={15} />

Pure Hebbian learning is unstable: correlated activity strengthens synapses, which increases activity, which strengthens them further. Every plasticity rule in Week 42 is, in part, an answer to this. BCM adds a sliding threshold, synaptic scaling normalises total strength, and Oja's rule subtracts a decay proportional to the square of the output. Marder and Goaillard's review, today's paper, makes the same point at the level of whole neurons: homeostasis keeps function stable while the parts vary.

<Step n={2} title="Recap sheet" minutes={20} />

**Vision (Week 38).** Retinal ganglion cells have centre-surround receptive fields (differences of Gaussians); V1 simple cells are Gabor-like; complex cells pool over phase (the energy model); the ventral stream builds invariance, and deep networks trained on object recognition predict its responses.

**Other senses and efficient coding (Week 39).** The cochlea is a filter bank; sound localisation uses interaural time differences; the fly olfactory system expands and sparsifies. Efficient coding (Barlow; Atick and Redlich) predicts that early sensory stages whiten their inputs where signal is strong and smooth them where noise dominates.

**Motor control (Week 41).** Movements are smooth (minimum jerk) and their noise grows with the command (signal-dependent noise). Optimal feedback control corrects only errors that matter for the goal (minimal intervention); forward models predict the consequences of commands; adaptation has fast and slow processes.

**Plasticity (Week 42).** Hebbian rules strengthen correlated synapses; STDP depends on the relative timing of pre- and postsynaptic spikes; BCM and Oja's rule stabilise Hebbian growth; synaptic scaling keeps rates in range; eligibility traces bridge the gap between activity and later reward.

**Memory (Week 43).** Place cells and grid cells represent space; ring and continuous attractors hold a remembered variable; the dentate gyrus separates similar patterns and CA3 completes partial ones; replay during rest may consolidate memories into cortex. A Hopfield network stores patterns as fixed points of $\mathbf x \leftarrow \text{sign}(W\mathbf x)$ with $W = \frac1N\sum_\mu \boldsymbol\xi^\mu\boldsymbol\xi^{\mu\top}$.

Oja's rule ends with a weight vector of length 1.001 pointing along the top eigenvector of the input covariance, with an alignment of 0.9999 (the sign is arbitrary: $\mathbf w$ and $-\mathbf w$ are equally good). Plain Hebbian learning on the same inputs finds the same direction but its length explodes to $10^{35}$. A single neuron with a stabilised Hebbian rule therefore performs PCA on its inputs, projecting them onto their direction of greatest variance: a link between Week 42's plasticity, Week 39's efficient coding and Week 7's principal components.

After 200 trials the net adaptation has reached 0.75 of the perturbation, mostly carried by the slow process. Five trials of the opposite perturbation bring the net back to −0.04, apparently erasing the learning, but only because the fast process has swung negative while the slow process still holds most of the original adaptation. In the error clamp, nothing is learned and both processes simply decay; the fast one forgets within a few trials, unmasking the slow one, and the net adaptation rebounds to 0.43 by trial 212. Smith, Ghazizadeh and Shadmehr (2006) predicted and found this spontaneous recovery in people adapting to force fields (Week 41). Behaviour that looks like one memory can be the sum of two with different timescales, the same moral as the fast and slow kernels in Week 51's mice.

Recall is perfect up to 20 patterns, then fails progressively: 76% at 25, 57% at 30, 15% at 40 and none at 60. With the strict criterion used here (90% of patterns recovered with overlap above 0.95), capacity is 20 patterns, a load of 0.10; the classical figure of about 0.14N, which answers the prediction (about 28 for 200 neurons), is the load at which the stored patterns stop being stable fixed points in very large networks, and a small network with 10% corrupted cues falls short of it. Either way capacity grows in proportion to the number of neurons, but only with a small constant, and it collapses when the patterns are correlated. This is one reason why the dentate gyrus is thought to decorrelate inputs before they reach CA3 (Week 43).

<Prove title="Oja's rule converges to a unit eigenvector" task="For inputs with covariance $C$ and small learning rate, Oja's rule follows on average $\dot{\mathbf w} = C\mathbf w - (\mathbf w^\top C\mathbf w)\mathbf w$. Show that its fixed points are unit eigenvectors of $C$, and that the length of $\mathbf w$ is driven towards 1.">

Averaging $y(\mathbf x - y\mathbf w)$ with $y = \mathbf w^\top\mathbf x$ gives $C\mathbf w - (\mathbf w^\top C\mathbf w)\mathbf w$.

At a fixed point $C\mathbf w = (\mathbf w^\top C\mathbf w)\mathbf w$, so $\mathbf w$ is an eigenvector with eigenvalue $\lambda = \mathbf w^\top C\mathbf w$. Writing $\mathbf w = a\mathbf u$ with $\mathbf u$ a unit eigenvector, $\lambda a\mathbf u = a^2\lambda\, a\mathbf u$, so $a^2 = 1$.

For the length, $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\lVert\mathbf w\rVert^2 = 2\mathbf w^\top\dot{\mathbf w} = 2(\mathbf w^\top C\mathbf w)(1 - \lVert\mathbf w\rVert^2)$. Since $\mathbf w^\top C\mathbf w \gt 0$, the length grows when it is below 1 and shrinks when it is above 1.

A stability analysis (not needed here) shows that only the eigenvector with the largest eigenvalue is stable, which is why Problem 1 found the first principal component.
