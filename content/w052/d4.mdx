<Step n={1} title="Prediction errors everywhere" minutes={15} />

Dopamine neurons fire for unexpected reward and, after learning, for the cue that predicts it: the temporal difference error of Week 44. Evidence accumulation in Week 45 sums noisy differences until they cross a bound. Predictive coding in Week 48 passes forward only what higher areas failed to predict. Botvinick and colleagues' review, today's paper, follows the same ideas into deep reinforcement learning, where prediction errors train networks that then show brain-like representations.

<Step n={2} title="Recap sheet" minutes={20} />

**Reward (Week 44).** Rescorla-Wagner updates a prediction by a fraction of the error, $V \leftarrow V + \alpha(r - V)$. Temporal difference learning extends this through time with $\delta_t = r_t + \gamma V_{t+1} - V_t$, which matches phasic dopamine. Q-learning learns action values; bandits trade exploration against exploitation; distributional TD learns a whole distribution of returns.

**Decisions (Week 45).** The drift diffusion model accumulates evidence with drift $v$ and noise $\sigma$ until a bound $\pm a$: accuracy $1/(1 + e^{-2va/\sigma^2})$ and mean decision time $\frac{a}{v}\tanh\frac{va}{\sigma^2}$. LIP neurons ramp like accumulators; attractor networks (Wang) implement the decision; confidence reflects the distance from the bound or the posterior.

**Working memory and attention (Week 46).** Bump attractors hold a remembered location; activity-silent mechanisms may store memories in synapses; capacity limits are set by resource sharing; attention acts like normalisation with an attention field; prefrontal networks switch tasks by context.

**Oscillations and states (Week 47).** Gamma rhythms arise from interacting excitation and inhibition (PING); phase locking and coherence may route communication; arousal follows an inverted U; sleep alternates up and down states.

**Inference (Week 48).** Signal detection theory separates sensitivity $d'$ from criterion; perception combines likelihood and prior, so low-contrast motion looks slower (Weiss, Simoncelli and Adelson); population codes can represent probability; predictive coding passes errors forward.

**Networks and language (Week 49).** Brain networks are small-world and modular; language areas respond to predictable structure, and language models' predictions explain their activity; the successor representation links hippocampus to prediction.

On the first trial the error is entirely at the reward. Within ten trials it has fallen to 0.35 there, and by trial 50 it is near zero at both the reward and the cue: in between, it is travelling backwards one time step at a time through the delay, as the plot shows. After 300 trials it sits at the cue, with a size of 0.80, which is $\gamma^{10}$, the discounted value of a reward ten steps away. Omitting the reward then produces an error of −1.00 at the expected time, the dip Schultz recorded in dopamine neurons. The backward-travelling error is a feature of this simple representation of time, not of real dopamine neurons, which do not show it clearly; richer representations of time, such as microstimuli or belief states, remove it (Week 44).

The formulas match the simulation: accuracy 0.881 against 0.874 and decision time 0.76 against 0.78 s, the small differences coming from discrete time steps and sampling. With a 2-second wait after each trial, reward rate peaks at a bound of 0.72, giving 81% accuracy in 0.44 s: an intermediate bound, which answers the prediction. A lower bound wastes trials on errors; a higher one wastes time on certainty, since each extra second of deliberation buys little accuracy when the wait is short. When the wait grows to 8 s, each trial is more costly and the best bound almost doubles to 1.34: careful decisions pay when trials are scarce. Bogacz and colleagues (2006) derived this result, and people and animals do adjust their bounds with the timing of a task, though usually towards being too cautious.

<Prove title="The posterior of two Gaussians" task="A prior $s \sim \mathcal N(\mu_p, \sigma_p^2)$ is combined with a likelihood from a measurement $m \sim \mathcal N(s, \sigma_l^2)$. Show that the posterior is Gaussian with mean $\frac{\sigma_l^2\mu_p + \sigma_p^2 m}{\sigma_p^2 + \sigma_l^2}$ and precision $\frac{1}{\sigma_p^2} + \frac{1}{\sigma_l^2}$.">

The log posterior is, up to a constant, $-\frac{(s - \mu_p)^2}{2\sigma_p^2} - \frac{(m - s)^2}{2\sigma_l^2}$, a quadratic in $s$, so the posterior is Gaussian.

The coefficient of $-s^2/2$ is $\frac{1}{\sigma_p^2} + \frac{1}{\sigma_l^2}$, which is the posterior precision.

The coefficient of $s$ is $\frac{\mu_p}{\sigma_p^2} + \frac{m}{\sigma_l^2}$, and the mean is this divided by the precision: $\frac{\mu_p/\sigma_p^2 + m/\sigma_l^2}{1/\sigma_p^2 + 1/\sigma_l^2} = \frac{\sigma_l^2\mu_p + \sigma_p^2 m}{\sigma_p^2 + \sigma_l^2}$.

Each source is weighted by its precision. With $\mu_p = 0$ the mean is $m\frac{\sigma_p^2}{\sigma_p^2 + \sigma_l^2}$, the shrinkage of Problem 3: the noisier the measurement, the more the percept is pulled towards the prior. The same formula is cue combination (Week 16) and one step of the Kalman filter (Week 36).
