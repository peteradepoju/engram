**Modelling behaviour (Week 50).** Wilson and Collins's rules: design the experiment so the models make different predictions; simulate before collecting data; check parameter and model recovery; fit by maximum likelihood with care; compare by held-out likelihood or an approximation to evidence such as $-\text{BIC}/2$; and validate the winner by simulating it (Palminteri's falsification). Across a group, random-effects selection estimates how common each model is. Diffusion models can be fitted to every response time with series for the first-passage density; GLM-HMMs reveal hidden strategies.

**The project (Week 51).** In 18 IBL mice, the block prior shifted every mouse's psychometric curve; mice repeated their choices even after errors; a leaky average of past actions beat a Bayesian observer and a stimulus kernel for 17 of 18 mice; its rate was close to the optimal one for a leaky average; and only it reproduced the after-error signature, the decisive falsification test.

**The year.** Part 1: arrays, linear algebra, calculus, Fourier analysis, optimisation, probability, statistics, Bayesian inference, information theory, dynamics and stochastic processes, ending with a population analysis of motor cortex. Part 2: membranes to synapses, coding and decoding, senses, movement, plasticity, memory, reward, decisions, cognition and behaviour, ending with a model comparison on real mice.

This is the check Week 50 asked for before any real data are fitted: had the correlations been low, differences between people or animals in fitted parameters would mean little. When the history effect is real (weight 0.5 in 1,000 trials), both criteria choose the history model in all 20 datasets. When it is absent, BIC never chooses it, while cross-validation chooses it in 8 of 20, close to the half you would expect when two models predict new data about equally well and noise decides. The two criteria answer different questions: BIC approximates which model is more probable given the data, and penalises the extra parameter by $\log n$, while cross-validation asks which predicts new data better, and with a single held-out split it is noisy. In Week 51 they agreed because the effects were large; with small effects, report both and prefer repeated cross-validation over a single split.

The fitted psychometric threshold, 0.0535, matches the prediction from the Fisher information, $1/\sqrt{351.3} = 0.0534$, to three significant figures. The choices of an ideal observer reading 50 Poisson neurons are therefore fully described by one number computed from their tuning curves. This joins the year end to end: Poisson variability and the central limit theorem (Part 1), Fisher information of a population code (Week 36), and the psychometric curves you fitted to real mice (Week 51). Doubling the number of neurons doubles the information and shrinks the threshold by $\sqrt 2$, about 30%, which answers the prediction. Real animals are far worse than this bound would suggest from the number of neurons they have, because noise correlations limit the information a large population can carry (Week 34) and because decisions add their own noise and lapses.

<Prove title="A psychometric threshold from Fisher information" task="With rates $r_0 e^{k_i c}$ and the decision statistic $D = \sum_i k_i(n_i - Tr_0)$, show that for small $c$, $D$ is approximately Gaussian with mean $Ic$ and variance $I$, where $I = Tr_0\sum_i k_i^2$ is the Fisher information at $c = 0$. Deduce that $P(\text{right}) \approx \Phi(c\sqrt I)$, so the threshold is $\sigma = 1/\sqrt I$.">

Each count has mean and variance $Tr_0e^{k_ic} \approx Tr_0(1 + k_ic)$ for small $c$.

So $\mathbb E[D] = \sum_i k_i\,Tr_0(e^{k_ic} - 1) \approx Tr_0c\sum_i k_i^2 = Ic$.

The counts are independent, so $\text{Var}(D) = \sum_i k_i^2\,Tr_0e^{k_ic} \approx Tr_0\sum_i k_i^2 = I$.

With many neurons $D$ is approximately Gaussian by the central limit theorem (Week 15), so $P(D \gt 0) = \Phi\!\left(\frac{Ic}{\sqrt I}\right) = \Phi(c\sqrt I)$, a cumulative Gaussian with $\sigma = 1/\sqrt I$.

The Fisher information from $I = T\sum_i f_i'^2/f_i$ with $f_i = r_0e^{k_ic}$ is $T\sum_i r_0k_i^2$ at $c = 0$, the same $I$. Doubling the number of neurons doubles $I$ and shrinks the threshold by $\sqrt 2$, about 30%.

</Prove>
