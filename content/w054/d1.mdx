<Step n={1} title="The supervised learning set-up" minutes={25} />

In **supervised learning** you are given $n$ examples $(\mathbf x_i, y_i)$, inputs paired with targets, drawn independently from some unknown distribution $p(\mathbf x, y)$. You choose a **hypothesis class** $\mathcal H$, a family of functions $h$ that map inputs to predictions, and a **loss** $\ell(y, h(\mathbf x))$ that says how bad a prediction is. The goal is a function that predicts well on *new* draws from the same distribution.

Neuroscience is full of such problems. An **encoding model** predicts a neuron's response $y$ from stimulus features $\mathbf x$ (Week 35). A **decoder** predicts a stimulus, a movement or a choice from population activity (Week 36). A **behavioural model** predicts a choice from the stimulus and history (Week 51). In each, the question that matters is how well the model predicts trials it was not fitted to.

Two quantities make this precise. The **expected risk**, or test error, is the average loss over the whole distribution,

$$
R(h) = \mathbb E_{(\mathbf x, y) \sim p}\left[\ell(y, h(\mathbf x))\right],
$$

which is what you care about but cannot compute, because $p$ is unknown. The **empirical risk**, or training error, is the average over the examples you have,

$$
\hat R_n(h) = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \ell(y_i, h(\mathbf x_i)).
$$

**Empirical risk minimisation** picks the $h \in \mathcal H$ with the smallest $\hat R_n$. For any *fixed* $h$, $\hat R_n(h)$ is an unbiased estimate of $R(h)$. But the minimiser was chosen *because* its empirical risk is small, partly by luck, so its empirical risk is biased downwards. The difference $R(\hat h) - \hat R_n(\hat h)$ is the **generalisation gap**, and most of this part of the course is about controlling it.

Breiman's essay on the "two cultures" of statistical modelling, today's paper, contrasts two attitudes to this set-up: the data-modelling culture, which assumes a stochastic model of how the data arose and interprets its parameters, and the algorithmic culture, which treats the mechanism as unknown and judges models by predictive accuracy on new data. Neuroscience needs both, and you will see both this term.

The grid search finds a slope of 0.74 and an intercept of 0.00, within a grid step of the least-squares line (0.750 and 0.010), with the same training risk of 0.169. For squared loss and a linear class, empirical risk minimisation *is* least squares, which is why Week 5's normal equations solve it exactly. In every case the training risk is lower than the expected risk, but by very different amounts. A straight line fitted to 20 points has a median training risk of 0.189 and an expected risk of 0.238; with 200 points the two nearly agree (0.214 and 0.220), because a two-parameter line cannot chase noise. A degree-9 polynomial on 20 points has a training risk of only 0.042, less than half the noise variance of 0.09, which is impossible for an honest predictor, and a median expected risk of 0.73, eight times the noise. Occasionally such a fit explodes between data points, with an expected risk in the thousands, which is why the medians are reported. With 200 points the same polynomial generalises well (0.086 and 0.097) and beats the line, because it can follow the sine. That answers the prediction: zero training error says nothing reassuring, and with flexible models and little data it usually means overfitting.

<Step n={4} title="Losses and what they estimate" minutes={20} />

The loss is a modelling choice, not a technicality: it decides which summary of the conditional distribution of $y$ the best predictor estimates.

<Derivation title="Squared loss is minimised by the conditional mean">
<div>

Fix an input $\mathbf x$ and consider predicting $y$ by a constant $c$. The expected squared loss is $\mathbb E[(y - c)^2 \mid \mathbf x]$.

</div>
<div>

Write $\mu = \mathbb E[y \mid \mathbf x]$. Then $\mathbb E[(y - c)^2 \mid \mathbf x] = \mathbb E[(y - \mu)^2 \mid \mathbf x] + (\mu - c)^2$, because the cross term $2(\mu - c)\,\mathbb E[y - \mu \mid \mathbf x]$ is zero.

</div>
<div>

The first term does not depend on $c$, so the loss is minimised at $c = \mu$. The best possible predictor under squared loss is $h^*(\mathbf x) = \mathbb E[y \mid \mathbf x]$, and its risk, $\mathbb E[\text{Var}(y \mid \mathbf x)]$, is the irreducible noise. The same argument with absolute loss $|y - c|$ gives the conditional median, and with 0-1 loss for classification the most probable class.

</div>
</Derivation>

Squared loss is minimised at 6.16, the sample mean, and absolute loss at 5.00, the sample median. For these skewed counts the two differ by more than a spike. Neither is wrong: they answer different questions. If large errors are much worse than small ones, squared loss and the mean are right; if you want a prediction that is as often too high as too low, absolute loss and the median. For spike counts, Week 35's Poisson likelihood is a third choice, which, like squared loss, is minimised by the mean.

<li>Supervised learning: inputs, targets, a hypothesis class and a loss.</li>
<li>Training error is an optimistic estimate of the error that matters.</li>
<li>The optimism grows with flexibility and shrinks with data.</li>
<li>The loss decides what the best predictor estimates: the mean, the median or the most probable class.</li>
