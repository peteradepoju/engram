<Step n={1} title="Two ways to fail" minutes={20} />

A model **underfits** when its hypothesis class cannot represent the structure in the data: a straight line through a sine, a linear decoder for a nonlinear boundary. Its training error and test error are both high, and close to each other. A model **overfits** when it is flexible enough to fit the particular noise in its training set: its training error is low, its test error high, and the gap between them large.

What makes a model flexible is not only its number of parameters but how freely those parameters can bend the function: the degree of a polynomial, the number of features in a regression, the depth of a tree, the width of a network. The amount of data sets how much flexibility can be afforded. Domingos's overview, today's paper, puts it bluntly: overfitting has many faces, and the cure is never simply more parameters or fewer, but the right balance for the data you have.

Training error falls steadily from 0.39 at degree 0 to 0.013 at degree 12, far below the noise variance of 0.09. Test error falls too, at first: underfitting gives way as the polynomial gains enough bends to follow the sine, reaching a minimum of 0.117 at degree 3. Then it climbs, to 0.16 at degree 5, 2.5 at degree 8 and a median of over 17,000 at degree 12, where 13 coefficients are pinned by only 15 points and the curve swings wildly between them. The degree with the lowest training error is the worst choice possible, so the training error cannot be used to choose a model: that needs data the model was not fitted to, the subject of tomorrow.

<Step n={3} title="Many features, few trials" minutes={30} />

Polynomials in one variable are a toy. The same failure in neuroscience looks like this: a neuron's response to a white-noise stimulus is modelled as a weighted sum of $p$ stimulus pixels, the receptive field, and fitted to a limited number of trials. Here the true receptive field uses only 5 of the pixels, and you fit models that use the first $p$ pixels, for $p$ from 5 to 55, on 60 trials.

With the 5 true features, the model explains 73% of the variance on its training trials and 71% on new ones: a small, honest gap. Adding irrelevant pixels raises the training R² every time, to 0.97 with 55 features, while the test R² falls, to 0.25 with 40 and to −0.41 with 55. That answers the prediction: the 55-feature model predicts new trials worse than simply guessing their mean, although by its training fit it looks like the best model of all. With 56 parameters and 60 trials, least squares has enough freedom to fit the noise on almost every trial, and the fitted receptive field is mostly noise. An analysis that reported only the training fit would be badly wrong.

<Step n={4} title="Taming flexibility with a penalty" minutes={25} />

Throwing features away works when you know which ones matter, which is rare. A gentler remedy keeps every feature but penalises large weights: **ridge regression** minimises $\lVert\mathbf y - X\mathbf w\rVert^2 + \lambda\lVert\mathbf w\rVert^2$, whose solution $(X^\top X + \lambda I)^{-1}X^\top\mathbf y$ you met in Week 5. Next week is devoted to it; today, just see what it does to the 55-feature model.

  def ridge_fit(A, y, lam):
      D = np.eye(A.shape[1]); D[-1, -1] = 0           # do not penalise the intercept
      return np.linalg.solve(A.T @ A + lam * D, A.T @ y)

With $\lambda \approx 18$, the same 55 features give a test R² of 0.41 instead of −0.41. The penalty shrinks the weights of the 50 irrelevant pixels towards zero, while the 5 real ones, supported by the data, shrink less. It does not reach the 0.71 of the model that used only the true features, because it still has to estimate all 55 weights from 60 trials, but it does this without being told which features matter. Choosing $\lambda$ here used the test set, which is cheating; tomorrow's lesson shows how to choose it honestly.

<Key title="Before you go">
<ul>
<li>Underfitting and overfitting are diagnosed by training and test error together.</li>
<li>Test error is U-shaped in complexity; training error is not a guide to its minimum.</li>
<li>Models with nearly as many features as trials can predict worse than a constant.</li>
<li>A penalty can rescue a flexible model without knowing which features matter.</li>
