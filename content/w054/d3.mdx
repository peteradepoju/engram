<Step n={1} title="Three sets, three jobs" minutes={20} />

A learning procedure usually has two levels. **Parameters** (the weights of a regression) are fitted by minimising training loss. **Hyperparameters** (the degree of a polynomial, the ridge penalty, the number of features, even the choice between model families) are chosen by the analyst, and choosing them is also a kind of fitting. Each level needs data it was not fitted to:

1. The **training set** fits the parameters, for each candidate setting of the hyperparameters.
2. The **validation set** compares those candidates and picks one.
3. The **test set** is used once, at the very end, to estimate how well the chosen model generalises.

Once a model has been chosen by its validation score, that score is optimistic for the same reason that training error is: it was the best of several noisy estimates. Only the untouched test set gives an unbiased estimate. When data are scarce, the validation set can be replaced by cross-validation within the training data (Week 59), but the principle stands. Recht and colleagues, today's paper, tested it at scale by collecting a new test set for ImageNet, the benchmark that drove a decade of computer vision.

The validation set chose $\lambda \approx 4$, and the refitted model scores R² = 0.46 on the untouched test set, against 0.53 for the best penalty on the grid, which you could only find by peeking. That gap is the honest cost of having to choose with 40 validation trials. The bigger lesson is in the 50 repeated splits: the chosen $\lambda$ ranges from 0.01 to 40 depending only on which trials happened to be held out. A validation set of 40 trials is a noisy instrument, which is why cross-validation, averaging over many splits, is usually preferred (Week 59). The test score of 0.46 is still honest; it is just the score of one particular, somewhat unlucky, choice.

With one model the reported accuracy is 50%, as it should be. Comparing 10 models and reporting the best gives 55.4%, 100 models 58.8%, and 200 models nearly 60%, all from classifiers that know nothing. That answers the prediction. <Step n={4} title="Splits that respect structure" minutes={25} />

A random split assumes the examples are independent. Neural and behavioural data rarely are: neighbouring trials share slow drifts in excitability, arousal, electrode position and strategy. Here a neuron's activity drifts slowly for reasons unrelated to the behavioural variable you want to predict, which also drifts slowly. A nearest-neighbour regressor can then "predict" behaviour by recognising *when* a trial happened, if trials adjacent in time land on both sides of the split.

  X_neural = drift_x + 0.03 * r.normal(size=(T, 3))

      return y_tr[np.argmin(d, axis=1)]

The random split reports R² = 0.58: the neurons appear to predict behaviour well. The blocked split, which holds out the last fifth of the session, gives a strongly negative R², because the decoder predicts behavioural values from earlier in the session, which have since drifted away. The neurons carry no information about behaviour at all. In the random split, each test trial's nearest neighbour in neural space is usually a trial from a few seconds earlier or later, whose behaviour was similar simply because both drift slowly: the decoder has learned *when* each trial happened, not anything about behaviour. Any slow signal in neural data, from electrode drift to changes in arousal, can create this illusion. When trials are not independent, split them as new data would arrive, in time blocks or by session.

<Key title="Before you go">
<ul>
<li>Training, validation and test sets do different jobs; mixing them inflates results.</li>
<li>Selecting among many models on one test set rewards luck.</li>
<li>Hyperparameters chosen on small validation sets vary a lot, even when the final model does well.</li>
<li>Split the data the way new data will arrive: by time block or by session, not at random, when trials are not independent.</li>
</ul>
