<Step n={1} title="The decomposition" minutes={25} />

Imagine repeating an experiment many times: each time you collect a fresh training set $\mathcal D$, fit a model $\hat f_{\mathcal D}$, and predict at a fixed input $x$, where the truth is $y = f(x) + \varepsilon$ with noise of variance $\sigma^2$. The prediction is a random variable, because the training set is.

<Derivation title="Bias, variance and noise">
<div>

Write $\bar f(x) = \mathbb E_{\mathcal D}[\hat f_{\mathcal D}(x)]$ for the average prediction over training sets. The expected squared error at $x$, over both the training set and the new noise, is $\mathbb E[(y - \hat f_{\mathcal D}(x))^2]$.

</div>
<div>

Add and subtract $f(x)$ and $\bar f(x)$: $y - \hat f = \varepsilon + (f - \bar f) + (\bar f - \hat f)$. The three terms are uncorrelated: $\varepsilon$ is new noise, independent of the training set; $f - \bar f$ is a constant; and $\bar f - \hat f$ has mean zero over training sets.

</div>
<div>

So the cross terms vanish and

$$
\mathbb E\left[(y - \hat f_{\mathcal D}(x))^2\right] = \underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{noise}} + \underbrace{\left(f(x) - \bar f(x)\right)^2}_{\text{bias}^2} + \underbrace{\mathbb E_{\mathcal D}\left[\left(\hat f_{\mathcal D}(x) - \bar f(x)\right)^2\right]}_{\text{variance}}.
$$

Averaging over $x$ gives the same split of the expected test error.

</div>
</Derivation>

Geman, Bienenstock and Doursat's paper, today's paper, introduced this decomposition to the neural network community in 1992, and argued that it limits any learning system that is not given the right prior structure: a flexible enough model has low bias, but must pay in variance unless it has a great deal of data.

The three terms add up: noise plus bias squared plus variance matches the directly measured test error to within 1% at every degree. Bias squared falls from 0.335 for a constant to 0.002 at degree 3 and is essentially zero beyond, because a cubic already captures most of a sine on this interval. Variance does the opposite: 0.013 for a constant, 0.015 at degree 3, 0.19 at degree 6 and 0.80 at degree 7. So the answer to the prediction is variance: at degree 7 it is nine times the noise and two hundred times the bias. The best model, degree 3, is where the falling bias meets the rising variance, with a total of 0.107, close to the noise floor of 0.09.

<Step n={3} title="A penalty as a dial" minutes={25} />

Degree is a coarse dial: it changes the model in jumps. A ridge penalty on a flexible model is a smooth one. Fix the degree at 9, too flexible for 30 points, and turn $\lambda$.

With almost no penalty, the degree-9 polynomial has a variance of 4.15, hopelessly large. At $\lambda = 0.03$ the variance falls to 0.023 while the bias squared stays tiny at 0.004: the penalty removes the wild swings without stopping the curve following the sine. At $\lambda = 10$ the curve is flattened, the variance is lower still (0.011), but the bias squared has risen to 0.18. Between these extremes lies a best penalty, just as there was a best degree, but reached smoothly and without deciding which powers of $x$ to drop. This is why regularisation, not model shrinking, is the usual tool, and next week studies it in depth.

<Step n={4} title="Averaging away variance" minutes={20} />

Variance comes from the particular training set. If you could fit to many independent training sets and average, the variance would shrink while the bias stayed the same. You have only one training set, but you can imitate many by resampling it with replacement, the bootstrap of Week 18. Averaging fits to bootstrap resamples is called **bagging** (Breiman, 1996), and it returns in Week 58 as the basis of random forests.

Bagging halves the variance of nearest-neighbour regression, from 0.093 to 0.047, while the bias squared barely moves (0.0009 to 0.0011). Each bootstrap resample leaves out about a third of the points and duplicates others, so the 30 fits differ; their average smooths over the idiosyncrasies of any one sample, much as averaging over independent training sets would. Bagging helps most for models like this one, or deep decision trees, that are unbiased but unstable. It does not help models that are already stable, and it can even hurt models such as high-degree polynomials whose fits explode on resamples with repeated points, which is worth trying for yourself.

<li>Expected test error is noise plus bias squared plus variance.</li>
<li>Flexibility lowers bias and raises variance; the best model balances them.</li>
<li>A penalty is a smooth dial between the two.</li>
<li>Averaging fits to bootstrap resamples lowers variance without much cost in bias.</li>
