<Step n={1} title="What learning curves show" minutes={20} />

A learning curve has two lines. The **training error** usually rises with $n$: more points are harder to fit exactly. The **validation error** falls: more points pin the model down. Both approach the same limit, the best error the hypothesis class can achieve, which is the noise plus the model's bias.

Two shapes are common. If the curves meet quickly, at an error well above what you need, the model is limited by **bias**: more data will not help, and a richer model or better features will. If there is a wide gap between them that closes slowly, the model is limited by **variance**: more data, or more regularisation, will help. In both cases the curve answers the experimenter's question of whether to record more trials before anyone records them.

  import numpy as np
  import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
  r = np.random.default_rng(545)
  N = 20
  centres = np.linspace(-1.2, 1.2, N)

  def simulate(n, g):
      s = g.uniform(-1, 1, n)
      rates = 2 + 8 * np.exp(-0.5 * ((s[:, None] - centres) / 0.5) ** 2)
      return s, g.poisson(rates * 0.25)

  def decoder_fit(X, s, lam=1.0):
      mu, sd = X.mean(0), X.std(0) + 1e-6
      A = np.c_[(X - mu) / sd, np.ones(len(s))]
      D = np.eye(A.shape[1]); D[-1, -1] = 0
      w = np.linalg.solve(A.T @ A + lam * D, A.T @ s)
      return lambda Xn: np.c_[(Xn - mu) / sd, np.ones(len(Xn))] @ w

  ns = np.array([25, 50, 100, 200, 400, 800, 1600])
  s_va, X_va = simulate(4000, r)
  tr_err, va_err = np.zeros(len(ns)), np.zeros(len(ns))
  for k, n in enumerate(ns):
      tr, va = [], []
      for rep in range(10):
          s_tr, X_tr = simulate(n, r)
          pred = decoder_fit(X_tr, s_tr)
          tr.append(np.mean((pred(X_tr) - s_tr) ** 2))
          va.append(np.mean((pred(X_va) - s_va) ** 2))
      tr_err[k], va_err[k] = np.mean(tr), np.mean(va)
      print(f"n = {n:4d}: training MSE {tr_err[k]:.4f}, validation MSE {va_err[k]:.4f}")
  plt.loglog(ns, tr_err, "o-", label="training"); plt.loglog(ns, va_err, "o-", label="validation"); plt.xlabel("training trials"); plt.ylabel("MSE"); plt.legend(); plt.show()

With 25 trials the decoder fits its training data with an error of 0.013 but makes errors of 0.17 on new trials: a wide variance gap. By 200 trials the gap has mostly closed (0.050 against 0.068), and by 1,600 the two curves have met at about 0.062. That meeting point is not the noise floor of the spike counts: it is the error of the best *linear* decoder of a population with bell-shaped tuning, so the decoder is now limited by bias. For this population, recording more than about 400 trials would barely improve a linear decoder; improving the decoder would.

<Step n={3} title="Power laws and extrapolation" minutes={25} />

Hestness and colleagues, today's paper, found across speech, translation and vision that validation error falls as a power law in the amount of data, $E(n) \approx a\,n^{-b} + c$, with an exponent $b$ characteristic of the problem and a floor $c$ set by noise and bias. If that holds for a decoder, a pilot experiment can predict how many trials a full one needs.

Fitted to the first five sizes only, the power law $E(n) = 10.9\,n^{-1.42} + 0.062$ predicts errors of 0.0630 and 0.0625 at 800 and 1,600 trials, against measured values of 0.0632 and 0.0622: a pilot of 400 trials predicted the rest of the curve almost exactly. The fitted floor, 0.062, is the bias limit seen in Step 2, and the exponent of 1.42 says the excess error shrinks fast, so that halving it from 400 trials needs only about 650. For the prediction, with an exponent of one half, halving the excess error needs $2^{1/0.5} = 4$ times the data; slow exponents of this kind are typical of hard problems such as image and speech recognition, which is why their models need so much data.

<Step n={4} title="Curves that cross" minutes={25} />

Is the linear decoder the best choice? The stimulus is coded by bell-shaped tuning curves, so the relation between counts and $s$ is not linear, and a flexible decoder could in principle do better. Compare it with a $k$-nearest-neighbour decoder, which averages the stimuli of the $k$ training trials whose population vectors are closest, across training-set sizes.

With 25 training trials the linear decoder wins clearly (error 0.134 against 0.190): the nearest-neighbour decoder has too few examples to average over in 20 dimensions. From 100 trials onwards the order reverses, and at 1,600 the nearest-neighbour decoder's error (0.035) is little more than half the linear decoder's (0.059), because it can follow the nonlinear relation between counts and stimulus that the linear decoder cannot. The curves cross. So "which decoder is best?" has no answer without "with how much data?". Perlich and colleagues found the same for logistic regression and decision trees across dozens of real datasets, and it is a reason to report learning curves rather than a single comparison at one size.
