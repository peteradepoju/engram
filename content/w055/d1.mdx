<Step n={1} title="From Gaussian noise to least squares" minutes={25} />

The word regression comes from Francis Galton, today's paper, who found in 1886 that tall parents have children who are tall but less extreme, a "regression towards mediocrity" that is really a property of any imperfect linear relation. The method has kept the name.

Suppose each response is a linear function of the inputs plus independent Gaussian noise: $y_i = \mathbf x_i^\top\mathbf w + \varepsilon_i$ with $\varepsilon_i \sim \mathcal N(0, \sigma^2)$.

<Derivation title="Least squares is maximum likelihood">
<div>

The likelihood of the weights is the probability of the observed responses:

$$
p(\mathbf y \mid X, \mathbf w, \sigma^2) = \prod_{i=1}^n \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{(y_i - \mathbf x_i^\top\mathbf w)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right).
$$

</div>
<div>

Its logarithm is $-\frac n2\log(2\pi\sigma^2) - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\lVert\mathbf y - X\mathbf w\rVert^2$. Only the second term depends on $\mathbf w$, so maximising the likelihood is minimising the squared error: $\hat{\mathbf w} = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top\mathbf y$.

</div>
<div>

Setting the derivative with respect to $\sigma^2$ to zero gives $\hat\sigma^2 = \lVert\mathbf y - X\hat{\mathbf w}\rVert^2/n$. This is biased low, because the fitted weights have already absorbed some of the noise; the unbiased estimate divides by $n - p$ instead, where $p$ is the number of weights.

</div>
</Derivation>

The derivation also shows what least squares assumes: noise that is Gaussian, of equal variance on every trial, and independent between trials. Spike counts violate the first two (Week 35's Poisson GLM is the fix) and time series often the third (Week 54's blocked splits). Least squares can still be a good estimator then, but the uncertainty formulas below become unreliable.

<Step n={2} title="Standard errors of the weights" minutes={30} />

Since $\hat{\mathbf w} = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top\mathbf y$ is a linear function of the Gaussian noise, it is Gaussian too, with mean $\mathbf w$ and covariance

$$
\text{Cov}(\hat{\mathbf w}) = \sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1}.
$$

The standard error of weight $j$ is the square root of the $j$-th diagonal entry, with $\sigma^2$ replaced by its estimate. A 95% confidence interval is $\hat w_j \pm t_{0.975, n-p}\,\text{SE}_j$. Check both claims by simulation.

The reported standard errors (0.160, 0.173, 0.181) match the actual spread of the estimates across 2,000 datasets (0.159, 0.171, 0.183), and the 95% intervals cover the true weights 94.6% to 95.6% of the time. When the model's assumptions hold, one dataset is enough to know how much to trust its weights. They held here because the noise was simulated to be Gaussian and independent; with overdispersed spike counts or autocorrelated time series the same formulas would report intervals that are too narrow, and the bootstrap of Week 18, resampling whole trials or blocks, is the safer route.

For standardised regressors, the variance of weight $j$ is multiplied by the **variance inflation factor** $\text{VIF}_j = 1/(1 - R_j^2)$, where $R_j^2$ is the R² of regressing regressor $j$ on all the others. With two regressors correlated at $\rho$, $R_j^2 = \rho^2$.

The spread of the first weight grows with the correlation: 1.14 times at 0.5, 1.55 at 0.8, 2.1 at 0.9 and 2.9 at 0.95, close to the square root of the VIF (1.15, 1.67, 2.29, 3.20; the simulation's empirical correlations wander a little from the nominal values). <Step n={4} title="Weights against predictions" minutes={20} />

Collinearity makes the *weights* uncertain. Does it make the *predictions* uncertain? The variance of a prediction at a new input $\mathbf x_0$ is $\sigma^2\mathbf x_0^\top(X^\top X)^{-1}\mathbf x_0$. Compare it for a typical input, where the two regressors move together as in the training data, and for an atypical one, where they disagree.

Each weight has a standard error of about 0.31 per unit noise, large for 100 trials. Yet the prediction for an input where the two regressors agree, as they nearly always do in the data, has a standard deviation of only 0.14. For an input where they disagree, a combination the data almost never show, it is 0.64, more than four times larger. The model knows the combined effect of the two regressors well and the separate effects badly, so it predicts reliably for familiar inputs and unreliably for unfamiliar ones. To separate the effects you need data that break the correlation, such as trials where the animal runs with a small pupil, or an experiment designed to manipulate one variable independently.

<Key title="Before you go">
<ul>
<li>Least squares assumes Gaussian, equal-variance, independent noise.</li>
<li>The weights' covariance, σ²(XᵀX)⁻¹, gives standard errors and confidence intervals that work when the assumptions hold.</li>
<li>Correlated regressors inflate the variance of their weights by the VIF.</li>
<li>Predictions stay reliable for inputs like the training data, even when the weights are not.</li>
</ul>
</Key>
