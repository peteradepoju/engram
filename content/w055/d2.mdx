<Step n={1} title="Ridge as a prior" minutes={20} />

Ridge minimises $\lVert\mathbf y - X\mathbf w\rVert^2 + \lambda\lVert\mathbf w\rVert^2$. Week 17 showed that a penalty is a prior in disguise; here is the exact correspondence.

<Derivation title="Ridge is the posterior mode under a Gaussian prior">
<div>

Take Gaussian noise of variance $\sigma^2$ and a Gaussian prior on the weights, $\mathbf w \sim \mathcal N(\mathbf 0, \tau^2 I)$. The log posterior is the log likelihood plus the log prior:

$$
\log p(\mathbf w \mid \mathbf y) = -\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\lVert\mathbf y - X\mathbf w\rVert^2 - \frac{1}{2\tau^2}\lVert\mathbf w\rVert^2 + \text{const}.
$$

</div>
<div>

Multiplying by $-2\sigma^2$, maximising it is minimising $\lVert\mathbf y - X\mathbf w\rVert^2 + \frac{\sigma^2}{\tau^2}\lVert\mathbf w\rVert^2$: ridge with $\lambda = \sigma^2/\tau^2$.

</div>
<div>

So a large penalty means a prior that weights are small relative to the noise. Setting the gradient to zero gives $\hat{\mathbf w}_\lambda = (X^\top X + \lambda I)^{-1}X^\top\mathbf y$, which always exists for $\lambda \gt 0$, even when $X^\top X$ is singular because there are more features than trials.

</div>
</Derivation>

Hastie's short essay, today's paper, collects the many faces of ridge: a penalty, a prior, a remedy for collinearity, a way of handling more features than samples, and the hidden ingredient of methods from kernel machines to neural networks trained with weight decay.

Write the centred design as $X = UDV^\top$ (Week 7). Then the ridge fit is

$$
X\hat{\mathbf w}_\lambda = \sum_j \mathbf u_j\,\frac{d_j^2}{d_j^2 + \lambda}\,\mathbf u_j^\top\mathbf y,
$$

the least-squares fit with the component along each left singular vector multiplied by $d_j^2/(d_j^2 + \lambda)$. The sum of these factors, $\text{df}(\lambda) = \sum_j d_j^2/(d_j^2 + \lambda)$, is the **effective degrees of freedom**: $p$ when $\lambda = 0$, falling to zero as $\lambda$ grows.

      return d ** 2 / (d ** 2 + lam)

The SVD formula agrees with the direct solution, and the shrinkage factors add up to the trace of the ridge hat matrix. The squared singular values of this correlated design run from 79 down to 0.12, so the directions are very unequal. With $\lambda = 1$ the model uses about 13 effective parameters of 20: the strong directions are kept almost whole and the weakest, where least squares would amplify noise, are shrunk away. With $\lambda = 100$ only about 2 effective parameters remain. For the prediction: with $\lambda = 40$ the factors are 400/440 = 0.91, 40/80 = 0.5 and 4/44 = 0.09, so the weakest direction keeps less than a tenth of its least-squares weight.

<Step n={3} title="Why scale matters" minutes={20} />

Least squares does not care about units: measure a regressor in millivolts instead of volts and its weight simply scales by a thousand. Ridge cares, because it penalises all weights equally. A regressor measured in small units needs a large weight, which is penalised heavily; one in large units needs a small weight and escapes. Standardising each regressor to unit variance before fitting puts them on equal terms.

Changing the units of the most influential regressor leaves least-squares predictions unchanged to machine precision, but moves ridge predictions by up to 0.13. In its new units that regressor needs a weight a thousand times larger, which the penalty punishes, so ridge quietly gives up on it. After standardising, both versions are identical again. In practice this means: standardise the columns of the design (using the training data's means and standard deviations only, as Week 59 will stress), fit, and transform new inputs with the same constants. It also means that a ridge penalty is not a property of the data alone but of the data and their scaling, which is worth stating in any methods section.

<Step n={4} title="Leave-one-out for free" minutes={30} />

Leave-one-out cross-validation fits the model $n$ times, each time predicting the one trial left out. For any linear smoother $\hat{\mathbf y} = H\mathbf y$, including ridge with $H = X(X^\top X + \lambda I)^{-1}X^\top$, there is an exact shortcut: the leave-one-out residual of trial $i$ is the ordinary residual divided by $1 - H_{ii}$. One fit gives all $n$ held-out errors.

The shortcut and the brute-force computation agree to five decimal places (0.28133), but the shortcut needed one fit instead of 80. Leave-one-out chooses $\lambda \approx 0.56$, while a large fresh test set prefers about 3.2; the two are within a factor of six, and the test error curve is flat between them, so little is lost. With only 80 trials, any data-driven choice of the penalty is noisy, as Week 54 found with validation sets. The shortcut makes it cheap to scan many penalties, which is why it is built into most ridge software, often in its generalised form (GCV), which replaces each $H_{ii}$ by their average.

<Key title="Before you go">
<ul>
<li>Ridge is maximum a posteriori estimation with a Gaussian prior.</li>
<li>It shrinks low-variance directions of the inputs most, by d²/(d² + λ).</li>
<li>Standardise regressors so that the penalty is fair.</li>
<li>For ridge, leave-one-out cross-validation costs a single fit.</li>
