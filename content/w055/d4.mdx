The largest useful penalty is $\lambda_\text{max} = \max_j |\mathbf x_j^\top\mathbf y|/n$: at or above it, every weight is zero, by the optimality conditions of yesterday. Starting there and decreasing $\lambda$ on a log scale, each solution is a good starting point, a **warm start**, for the next, so coordinate descent needs only a few sweeps per value. This is how the glmnet software of Friedman, Hastie and Tibshirani computes entire paths in roughly the time of one least-squares fit.

At $\lambda_\text{max} = 1.32$ every weight is zero. As the penalty falls, inputs enter in the order 0, 1, 3, 2, 4, 5, which is almost exactly the order of their true effect sizes (1.2, −0.9, 0.7, 0.5, −0.3, 0.2), with inputs 2 and 3 swapping places because of noise. Only after all six real inputs are in do the null inputs 9 and 11 appear, at small penalties where the model begins to fit noise. The whole path of 40 fits took a fraction of a second, because each fit started from its neighbour's solution.

<Step n={2} title="Correlated inputs and the elastic net" minutes={30} />

When several inputs are near-copies of each other, the lasso tends to pick one of the group, more or less arbitrarily, and zero the rest: spreading weight over the copies costs more L1 penalty for the same fit than concentrating it. Zou and Hastie's **elastic net**, today's paper, adds a ridge term, $\lambda\left(\alpha\lVert\mathbf w\rVert_1 + \frac{1-\alpha}{2}\lVert\mathbf w\rVert_2^2\right)$, whose strict convexity encourages correlated inputs to share weight: a grouping effect. The coordinate update just divides the soft threshold by $1 + \lambda(1 - \alpha)$, as in the function above.

The three inputs are correlated at 0.99 and contribute equally to the target, yet the lasso gives them weights of 0, 0.57 and 0.88: it drops one entirely and loads most of the effect onto another. Which one wins is decided by tiny differences in noise, so a different dataset would choose differently, and an analyst reading the lasso's weights would wrongly conclude that the second neuron does not matter. That answers the prediction. The elastic net gives the three weights of 0.41, 0.52 and 0.55, sharing the effect roughly equally, which is the honest summary when the data cannot tell the copies apart. The price is a little less sparsity elsewhere, since the ridge part of the penalty shrinks without selecting.

<Step n={3} title="Choosing λ by cross-validation" minutes={35} />

K-fold cross-validation splits the trials into K folds, fits on K − 1 of them and tests on the one left out, for every fold and every λ. The mean held-out error traces a curve with a minimum, $\lambda_\text{min}$. Because the curve is itself an estimate, it comes with a standard error at each λ (the spread of the K fold errors divided by $\sqrt K$). The **one-standard-error rule** picks the largest λ whose error is within one standard error of the minimum: a simpler model that is statistically indistinguishable from the best.

The cross-validation curve is lowest at a very small penalty, $\lambda_\text{min} \approx 0.003$, where all 12 inputs are in the model, but it is almost flat over a wide range. The one-standard-error rule moves up to $\lambda_\text{1se} \approx 0.09$, where the model uses 7 inputs: the 6 that truly matter and one false one. Its predictions are statistically indistinguishable from the best, and it is far easier to interpret. Minimum-error selection is tuned for prediction, which tolerates many small spurious weights; the one-SE rule leans towards selection. <Step n={4} title="Reading the path" minutes={15} />

A path is a summary of the data, not only a route to one model. Three habits help. Look at the order of entry: inputs that enter early carry the strongest, most robust effects. Look for inputs that enter and later leave or change sign: usually a sign of correlation with something that entered after them. And remember that the weights of a selected model are shrunk, so if you want unbiased estimates of the selected effects, refit them by least squares (the "relaxed lasso"), accepting that the selection itself used the data and so ordinary standard errors will be too small.
