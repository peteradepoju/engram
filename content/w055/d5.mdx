<Step n={1} title="The problem" minutes={20} />

A neuron's response on trial $t$ is modelled as $y_t = \mathbf k^\top\mathbf x_t + \varepsilon_t$, where $\mathbf x_t$ is the stimulus (here 30 pixels of a one-dimensional image) and $\mathbf k$ the receptive field. A Poisson GLM would be more realistic for spikes (Week 35), but the linear Gaussian model shows every issue more simply and the conclusions carry over.

Natural images are smooth: neighbouring pixels are strongly correlated. In this lesson the stimuli are built by smoothing white noise, which gives each pixel a correlation of about 0.8 with its neighbour. Two estimators from Part 2 then disagree. The spike-triggered average, $\hat{\mathbf k}_\text{STA} = \frac1n X^\top\mathbf y$, estimates $C\mathbf k$, where $C$ is the stimulus covariance: the true field blurred by the stimulus correlations. Least squares, $(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top\mathbf y$, undoes the blur, but dividing by the small eigenvalues of $X^\top X$ amplifies noise. Sound familiar? It is exactly the situation that ridge regression was invented for.

The spike-triggered average is too wide (5.6 pixels against the true 3.9) and has the wrong shape: the stimulus correlations have blurred the field's alternating bands together, so its relative error is large (1.73, partly because blurring also changes its amplitude). Least squares has the right centre but is jagged with noise, wider still because the noise spreads to every pixel (relative error 1.47). Ridge with a modest penalty cuts the error to 0.44. Neither classical estimator is good from 200 trials of correlated stimuli; the remedy is regularisation.

<Step n={3} title="Priors as penalties" minutes={35} />

Ridge assumes each pixel's weight is independently small. Real receptive fields are smooth: neighbouring weights are similar. A **smoothness penalty** encodes that: $\lambda\lVert D\mathbf k\rVert^2$, where $D$ takes second differences ($k_{i-1} - 2k_i + k_{i+1}$). The estimate is $(X^\top X + \lambda D^\top D)^{-1}X^\top\mathbf y$, the posterior mode under a Gaussian prior whose covariance favours smooth shapes. Park and Pillow, today's paper, went further, with priors that are smooth *and* localised in space and frequency, fitted by maximising the evidence. Compare ridge and smoothness here, each with its penalty chosen by five-fold cross-validation.

Cross-validation chose penalties of about 32 for ridge and 18 for smoothness. Both regularised estimates beat least squares by a wide margin. The smoothness prior gives the best receptive field, with an error of 0.30 against ridge's 0.43, because it shrinks towards the right kind of shape rather than towards zero. Yet the two predict new responses almost equally well (R² 0.666 and 0.668, against 0.682 for the true field). This is a lesson worth remembering: the stimulus rarely contains patterns along which the two estimates differ, so prediction cannot tell them apart, while their pictures of the receptive field differ visibly. Judge an encoding model by prediction, but do not assume that equal prediction means equally good parameters. That answers the prediction: the smoothness-penalised estimate is closest to the truth.

With 60 trials least squares explains only 35% of the variance in new responses, while the smoothness-penalised estimate already explains 62%, close to the ceiling of 68%. The advantage shrinks steadily with data: 0.12 at 120 trials, 0.03 at 250 and essentially nothing at 1,000, where least squares has enough data to average its noise away. A well-chosen prior is worth hundreds of trials when data are scarce and costs almost nothing when they are plentiful, provided the penalty is chosen by the data, as cross-validation did here.
