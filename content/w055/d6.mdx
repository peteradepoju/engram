Least squares, with 60 weights from 150 trials, reaches a test R² of 0.61. Every penalised model does better: ridge 0.733, the lasso 0.730 and the elastic net 0.745, the best, because the behaviour depends on whole groups of correlated neurons, the situation it was designed for. Ridge uses all 60 neurons; the lasso and elastic net each use 24, more than the 10 that truly matter, because penalties chosen for prediction tolerate small spurious weights (Day 4). For prediction the three penalised models are nearly interchangeable; for interpretation they tell different stories, which Part B makes concrete.

Across 30 bootstrap resamples, the ten truly relevant neurons are selected in 40% to 100% of fits, and the fifty irrelevant neurons in 10% on average. The groups are clearly picked out as groups. But which *members* of a group are selected varies from resample to resample: within group 1, neuron 4 is chosen every time and neuron 2 less than half the time, although all five contribute equally. Only two neurons pass an 80% stability threshold. A single lasso fit would have named a specific subset with false confidence; the bootstrap shows that the data support a statement about groups of correlated neurons, not about individual ones.

<Prove title="1. Ridge in the SVD basis" task="With $X = UDV^\top$ (thin SVD, $D$ diagonal with entries $d_j$), show that $(X^\top X + \lambda I)^{-1}X^\top\mathbf y = V\,\text{diag}\!\left(\frac{d_j}{d_j^2 + \lambda}\right)U^\top\mathbf y$, and that the fitted values are $\sum_j \frac{d_j^2}{d_j^2 + \lambda}\mathbf u_j\mathbf u_j^\top\mathbf y$.">

$X^\top X = VD U^\top UDV^\top = VD^2V^\top$, so $X^\top X + \lambda I = V(D^2 + \lambda I)V^\top$ (using $VV^\top = I$ when $X$ has full column rank, and adding the null-space directions otherwise, where $X^\top\mathbf y$ has no component).

Its inverse is $V(D^2 + \lambda I)^{-1}V^\top$, and $X^\top\mathbf y = VDU^\top\mathbf y$, so the product is $V(D^2 + \lambda I)^{-1}DU^\top\mathbf y = V\,\text{diag}\!\left(\frac{d_j}{d_j^2 + \lambda}\right)U^\top\mathbf y$.

The fitted values are $X\hat{\mathbf w} = UDV^\top V\,\text{diag}\!\left(\frac{d_j}{d_j^2 + \lambda}\right)U^\top\mathbf y = U\,\text{diag}\!\left(\frac{d_j^2}{d_j^2 + \lambda}\right)U^\top\mathbf y = \sum_j \frac{d_j^2}{d_j^2 + \lambda}\mathbf u_j\mathbf u_j^\top\mathbf y$: least squares, with each direction's component shrunk by its own factor.

</Prove>

<Prove title="2. The lasso's largest useful penalty" task="For the objective $\frac{1}{2n}\lVert\mathbf y - X\mathbf w\rVert^2 + \lambda\lVert\mathbf w\rVert_1$, show that $\mathbf w = \mathbf 0$ is optimal if and only if $\lambda \ge \max_j |\mathbf x_j^\top\mathbf y|/n$.">

The objective is convex, so $\mathbf 0$ is optimal exactly when zero is in its subdifferential there.

The gradient of the squared-error term at $\mathbf 0$ is $-X^\top\mathbf y/n$. The subdifferential of $\lambda|w_j|$ at $w_j = 0$ is the interval $[-\lambda, \lambda]$.

So $\mathbf 0$ is optimal if and only if, for every $j$, $-\mathbf x_j^\top\mathbf y/n + s_j = 0$ for some $s_j \in [-\lambda, \lambda]$, that is $|\mathbf x_j^\top\mathbf y|/n \le \lambda$ for all $j$, which is $\lambda \ge \max_j |\mathbf x_j^\top\mathbf y|/n$.

The input achieving the maximum is the first to enter the path as $\lambda$ falls below this value: the one most correlated with the response.

</Prove>
