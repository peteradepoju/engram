<Step n={1} title="From scores to probabilities" minutes={20} />

Logistic regression models the probability that $y = 1$ given inputs $\mathbf x$ as

$$
p(\mathbf x) = \sigma(\mathbf w^\top\mathbf x + b) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-(\mathbf w^\top\mathbf x + b)}}.
$$

The score $z = \mathbf w^\top\mathbf x + b$ is the **log-odds**, $\log\frac{p}{1-p}$, so each weight is the change in log-odds per unit change in its input, and $e^{w_j}$ the factor by which the odds are multiplied. The **decision boundary**, where $p = 0.5$, is the hyperplane $\mathbf w^\top\mathbf x + b = 0$; contours of equal probability are parallel to it, closer together when $\lVert\mathbf w\rVert$ is large. Cox's 1958 paper, today's paper, set out this model for binary data, and it remains the default first classifier in almost every field.

<Step n={2} title="Cross-entropy and its gradient" minutes={30} />

<Derivation title="The loss of logistic regression">
<div>

Each label is a Bernoulli draw with probability $p_i = \sigma(z_i)$, so the likelihood is $\prod_i p_i^{y_i}(1 - p_i)^{1 - y_i}$, and the negative log-likelihood is the **cross-entropy**

$$
L(\mathbf w) = -\sum_i \left[y_i\log p_i + (1 - y_i)\log(1 - p_i)\right].
$$

</div>
<div>

Using $\sigma'(z) = \sigma(z)(1 - \sigma(z))$, the derivative of each term with respect to $z_i$ simplifies to $p_i - y_i$. By the chain rule, with the bias folded into $\mathbf w$ through a column of ones,

$$
\nabla L = X^\top(\mathbf p - \mathbf y),
$$

the same form as least squares, with predicted probabilities in place of predicted values.

</div>
<div>

The Hessian is $X^\top SX$ with $S = \text{diag}(p_i(1 - p_i))$, which is positive semi-definite: the loss is convex, so any minimum is the global one.

</div>
</Derivation>

Gradient descent recovers weights of −0.59, 2.20 and −1.28 against true values of −0.5, 2.0 and −1.0, which is as close as 400 noisy labels allow. The loss falls quickly at first (from 245 to 157 in 100 steps) and then creeps: after 2,000 steps it is still improving in the second decimal place. The plot on a log scale shows the slow, geometric approach typical of gradient descent on a problem whose curvature differs between directions, as you saw in Week 12.

<Step n={3} title="Newton's method" minutes={25} />

Because the Hessian is cheap for a few weights, Newton's method, $\mathbf w \leftarrow \mathbf w - (X^\top SX)^{-1}X^\top(\mathbf p - \mathbf y)$, converges in a handful of steps. Each step is a weighted least-squares problem, which is why it is also called **iteratively reweighted least squares**, the algorithm inside most GLM software.

Newton's method reaches the same weights in four or five steps: the loss is 174.5, 159.0, 156.79, 156.715 and then constant to four decimal places. Each step uses the curvature to jump towards the minimum, and near the minimum the number of correct digits roughly doubles each step. For models with a few hundred weights this makes Newton the method of choice; for millions of weights, as in neural networks, the Hessian is too large to form, and variants of gradient descent take over.

<Step n={4} title="When the classes separate" minutes={30} />

Decoders are often fitted to more neurons than trials, and then the training classes can usually be separated perfectly by some hyperplane. On such data, scaling up the weights pushes every $p_i$ closer to its label, so the cross-entropy keeps decreasing towards zero and has no minimum. Fit 40 trials of 50 neurons and watch.

With 50 neurons and 40 trials, the training trials are perfectly separable: unpenalised training accuracy reaches 100%, and the weight norm keeps growing, from 5.1 after 5,000 steps to 5.8 after 10,000 and 6.4 after 20,000. It grows only logarithmically in the number of steps, but it never stops, because the loss has no minimum: any separating direction can be scaled up to drive every probability closer to its label. That answers the prediction. The model's predicted probabilities become absurdly confident while its test accuracy, 72%, is unremarkable. With a penalty of 0.1 the norm settles at 1.47, the probabilities stay sensible, and test accuracy is slightly better (75%). In practice, decoders with many neurons should always be penalised, with the penalty chosen by cross-validation.

<Key title="Before you go">
<ul>
<li>Logistic regression is a linear model of the log-odds, fitted by minimising cross-entropy.</li>
<li>The gradient is Xᵀ(p − y); Newton's method converges in a few steps.</li>
<li>Weights change the odds multiplicatively; the boundary is a hyperplane.</li>
<li>With more neurons than trials, classes separate and weights diverge unless penalised.</li>
