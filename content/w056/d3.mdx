<Step n={1} title="Two ways to classify" minutes={20} />

A **discriminative** classifier models $p(y \mid \mathbf x)$ directly and learns only the boundary between classes. A **generative** classifier models the class-conditional distributions $p(\mathbf x \mid y)$ and the class frequencies $p(y)$, and classifies by Bayes' rule:

$$
p(y = k \mid \mathbf x) = \frac{p(\mathbf x \mid y = k)\,p(y = k)}{\sum_j p(\mathbf x \mid y = j)\,p(y = j)}.
$$

Generative models make stronger assumptions, but they also do more: they can simulate data, handle missing inputs, and, when their assumptions are right, learn from fewer examples. Ng and Jordan made this last point precise, and you will reproduce their study in Week 75.

<Step n={2} title="Linear discriminant analysis" minutes={30} />

Suppose each class is Gaussian with its own mean $\boldsymbol\mu_k$ but a **shared** covariance $\Sigma$. Then the log-odds between two classes is

$$
\log\frac{p(y = 1 \mid \mathbf x)}{p(y = 0 \mid \mathbf x)} = (\boldsymbol\mu_1 - \boldsymbol\mu_0)^\top\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf x + \text{const},
$$

because the quadratic terms $\mathbf x^\top\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf x$ cancel. The boundary is linear, with weight vector $\mathbf w = \Sigma^{-1}(\boldsymbol\mu_1 - \boldsymbol\mu_0)$. Fisher, today's paper, found the same direction in 1936 by a different argument: it is the projection that maximises the separation of the class means relative to the spread within each class. Week 36's optimal linear decoder in the presence of correlated noise used exactly this formula.

When its assumptions hold, LDA does as well as logistic regression at both sizes: 68.3% with 30 training trials and 77.4% with 2,000, identical to logistic regression to three decimal places. LDA gets there in one step, from two means and a covariance, without any iterative optimisation, which is why it remains a favourite for decoding from many neurons with few trials, usually with the covariance shrunk towards its diagonal for stability. Its weights are not simply the difference of the class means: the inverse covariance subtracts the correlated noise, the same correction that made Week 36's optimal linear decoder better than one that ignored correlations.

If each class has its own covariance $\Sigma_k$, the quadratic terms no longer cancel and the boundary is a quadric: **quadratic discriminant analysis** (QDA). The classifier compares $\log p(\mathbf x \mid k) = -\frac12\log|\Sigma_k| - \frac12(\mathbf x - \boldsymbol\mu_k)^\top\Sigma_k^{-1}(\mathbf x - \boldsymbol\mu_k)$ across classes. It needs a covariance per class, $D(D+1)/2$ numbers each, so it needs more data.

When one class is three times as spread out as the other but their means nearly coincide, LDA can do little better than guessing (55.8%), because no straight line separates a tight cloud from a wide one around it. QDA, with a covariance for each class, draws a closed boundary around the tighter class and reaches 82.9%. Differences in variability are common in neural data: a neuron's response may become more or less variable with attention or arousal, so a class can be distinguishable by spread alone.

<Step n={4} title="Naive Bayes for spike counts" minutes={30} />

**Naive Bayes** assumes the inputs are independent given the class, so $p(\mathbf x \mid k) = \prod_j p(x_j \mid k)$. For spike counts with Poisson rates $\lambda_{jk}$ this is the Bayesian decoder of Week 36: $\log p(\mathbf x \mid k) = \sum_j [x_j\log\lambda_{jk} - \lambda_{jk}] + \text{const}$. It needs only one mean per neuron and class, so it learns fast. But real neurons share noise. Decode eight directions from neurons whose counts include a shared fluctuation, and check its confidence: if it says 90%, is it right 90% of the time?

With independent noise, naive Bayes is right on 98.9% of trials and claims 99.0% confidence: its probabilities are trustworthy, because its assumption is true. With a shared fluctuation in excitability, its accuracy falls to 88.3% but its confidence only to 96.0%: it is overconfident by 7.7 points, which answers the prediction. Every neuron's count rises and falls together on a trial, and naive Bayes counts that one shared event sixty times over as independent evidence. Its *ranking* of the classes remains useful, so accuracy is respectable, but its probabilities cannot be used as confidence without recalibration (Week 68), and any analysis that interprets them as the brain's uncertainty would be misled.

<Key title="Before you go">
<ul>
<li>Generative classifiers model each class's data and invert with Bayes' rule.</li>
<li>LDA is optimal for Gaussian classes with a shared covariance, and competitive when data are scarce.</li>
<li>QDA captures differences in spread, at the cost of more parameters.</li>
<li>Naive Bayes is fast and often accurate, but its probabilities are overconfident when inputs share noise.</li>
</ul>
