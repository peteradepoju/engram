<Step n={1} title="The Bayes decision rule" minutes={20} />

Suppose a classifier gives the probability $p = P(y = 1 \mid \mathbf x)$, a false positive costs $c_\text{FP}$ and a false negative $c_\text{FN}$ (correct decisions cost nothing). Saying "1" has expected cost $(1 - p)\,c_\text{FP}$ and saying "0" has expected cost $p\,c_\text{FN}$. Saying "1" is better when $p\,c_\text{FN} \gt (1 - p)\,c_\text{FP}$, that is, when

$$
p \gt p^* = \frac{c_\text{FP}}{c_\text{FP} + c_\text{FN}}.
$$

With equal costs the threshold is 0.5. If missing a seizure is ten times worse than a false alarm, the threshold drops to $1/11 \approx 0.09$. This is the **Bayes decision rule**, and it requires calibrated probabilities: a model whose 0.3 means 0.3. Signal detection theory (Week 48) is the same analysis, with the criterion playing the role of the threshold.

The empirical minimum of the total cost sits at thresholds of 0.47, 0.20 and 0.11 for cost ratios of 1, 4 and 9, against Bayes thresholds of 0.50, 0.20 and 0.10. When misses are expensive, the right policy is to say "yes" on evidence that is far from convincing: at a ratio of 9, a 15% chance of an event is enough to act. The match depends on the probabilities being calibrated; with an overconfident model such as yesterday's naive Bayes decoder, the formula would put the threshold in the wrong place.

When costs are unknown, or the classifier will be used at different thresholds, describe it at all of them. The **ROC curve** plots the true positive rate against the false positive rate as the threshold sweeps from high to low. Its **area** (AUC) has a direct meaning: the probability that a randomly chosen positive gets a higher score than a randomly chosen negative, which is also the Mann-Whitney statistic. For Gaussian scores with equal variance separated by $d'$, $\text{AUC} = \Phi(d'/\sqrt 2)$, linking ROC analysis to the psychophysics of Week 48.

  dp = 1.2
  lab = r.integers(0, 2, 4000)

  def roc_curve(scores, labels):

The three numbers agree: 0.802 by the trapezoid rule, 0.800 from counting correctly ranked pairs on a subsample, and 0.802 from $\Phi(1.2/\sqrt 2)$. The area is a property of the scores, not of any threshold, which makes it a good summary of how separable two classes are. Its limitation is the other side of the same coin: it averages over thresholds you would never use, and two classifiers with equal AUC can differ greatly at the one operating point that matters for your application.

<Step n={4} title="Rare events" minutes={25} />

Ripples, spikes in a noisy trace, seizures and lapses are rare. With 5% positives, a detector that always says "no" is 95% accurate and useless. Better scores for rare events are **balanced accuracy** (the mean of the true positive and true negative rates), **recall** (the true positive rate) and **precision** (the fraction of reported events that are real). The ROC curve is unaffected by prevalence; precision is not.

The detector that never reports an event scores 95.1% accuracy and 50% balanced accuracy, which answers the prediction and shows why accuracy is the wrong score here. The real detector has lower accuracy (91.9%) but a balanced accuracy of 81%, and finds 68% of events. At 5% prevalence only 34% of its reports are real events; at 30% prevalence the same detector, with the same threshold and the same recall (69%), has a precision of 81%. Nothing about the detector changed, only how rare the events were. A ripple detector tuned on a dataset rich in ripples will therefore disappoint on long recordings where ripples are scarce, unless its threshold is reset.
