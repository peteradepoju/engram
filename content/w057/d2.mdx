<Step n={1} title="Ridge in the dual" minutes={25} />

<Derivation title="Ridge regression needs only inner products">
<div>

Ridge's solution $\mathbf w = (X^\top X + \lambda I)^{-1}X^\top\mathbf y$ satisfies $(X^\top X + \lambda I)\mathbf w = X^\top\mathbf y$, so $\mathbf w = X^\top(\mathbf y - X\mathbf w)/\lambda = X^\top\boldsymbol\alpha$ with $\boldsymbol\alpha = (\mathbf y - X\mathbf w)/\lambda$. The weights are a combination of the training inputs.

</div>
<div>

Substituting $\mathbf w = X^\top\boldsymbol\alpha$ into $\boldsymbol\alpha = (\mathbf y - X\mathbf w)/\lambda$ gives $\lambda\boldsymbol\alpha = \mathbf y - XX^\top\boldsymbol\alpha$, so

$$
\boldsymbol\alpha = (K + \lambda I)^{-1}\mathbf y, \qquad K = XX^\top.
$$

</div>
<div>

The prediction at a new input is $\mathbf w^\top\mathbf x = \sum_i \alpha_i\,\mathbf x_i^\top\mathbf x$. Both the fit and the prediction use the data only through inner products, so replacing $\mathbf x_i^\top\mathbf x_j$ by $k(\mathbf x_i, \mathbf x_j)$ gives **kernel ridge regression**: $\hat f(\mathbf x) = \sum_i \alpha_i k(\mathbf x_i, \mathbf x)$.

</div>
</Derivation>

That the best function is a combination of kernels centred on the training points is not a coincidence of ridge: the **representer theorem** says it holds for any loss plus a penalty on the function's norm in the kernel's feature space. The price is that fitting needs the $n \times n$ matrix $K$, and solving with it costs about $n^3$ operations. Rahimi and Recht, today's paper, showed how to sidestep that with random features, which you build in Step 4.

<Step n={4} title="Random features" minutes={25} />

For a Gaussian kernel, Rahimi and Recht showed that $k(\mathbf x, \mathbf x') \approx \mathbf z(\mathbf x)^\top\mathbf z(\mathbf x')$ with $\mathbf z(\mathbf x) = \sqrt{2/D}\,\cos(W\mathbf x + \mathbf b)$, where the $D$ rows of $W$ are drawn from a Gaussian with standard deviation $1/\ell$ and $\mathbf b$ is uniform on $[0, 2\pi)$. The approximation follows from writing the kernel as the Fourier transform of a probability distribution (Bochner's theorem) and sampling it. Ordinary ridge on $D$ random features then costs about $nD^2$ instead of $n^3$.

Ten random features are far too few (an error of 26% of the predictions' spread), but 30 already bring it to 3%, and the error then shrinks slowly, roughly as $1/\sqrt D$, reaching 1.3% at 1,000 features and 0.7% at 3,000, which answers the prediction. With 2,000 trials the exact method needs a 2,000 by 2,000 system; the random-feature method with 1,000 features is about four times cheaper and agrees to about 1%, and the advantage grows quickly with $n$: for a million trials, exact kernel ridge is impossible on a laptop while random features remain easy.

<li>Ridge can be written using only inner products; any kernel then gives a nonlinear regression.</li>
<li>Kernel ridge fits flexible tuning curves with one linear solve.</li>
<li>The length-scale and penalty are chosen together by cross-validation.</li>
<li>Random features trade a little accuracy for a large saving in computation.</li>
