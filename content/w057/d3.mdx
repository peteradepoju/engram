<Step n={1} title="The margin" minutes={20} />

For a linear classifier with score $f(\mathbf x) = \mathbf w^\top\mathbf x + b$ and labels $y_i \in \{-1, +1\}$, the signed distance of example $i$ from the boundary is $y_if(\mathbf x_i)/\lVert\mathbf w\rVert$: positive when it is on the correct side. The **margin** of the classifier is the smallest such distance over the training set. A large margin means no example is close to the boundary, so small perturbations of the inputs, from noise or from slightly different trials, do not change any classification. That robustness is the intuition behind the support vector machine, and learning theory (Week 67) turns it into bounds on test error that do not depend on the number of features.

<Step n={2} title="The hard-margin SVM" minutes={25} />

Scaling $\mathbf w$ and $b$ together does not move the boundary, so fix the scale by requiring $y_if(\mathbf x_i) \ge 1$ for every example, with equality for the closest ones. The margin is then $1/\lVert\mathbf w\rVert$, and maximising it is

$$
\min_{\mathbf w, b}\ \tfrac12\lVert\mathbf w\rVert^2 \quad\text{subject to}\quad y_i(\mathbf w^\top\mathbf x_i + b) \ge 1 \text{ for all } i,
$$

a convex quadratic programme with a unique solution. The examples whose constraints are active, $y_if(\mathbf x_i) = 1$, are the **support vectors**; every other example could be moved or removed without changing the answer. Boser, Guyon and Vapnik, today's paper, combined this optimal-margin idea with kernels in 1992.

The solver returns $\mathbf w = (1.71, 1.29)$ and $b = 0.36$, a margin of 0.467, and only two support vectors, examples 13 and 36, one from each class, sitting exactly on the dashed lines. The other 38 examples have no say in where the boundary goes. This sparsity is the SVM's signature: the solution is determined by the hardest cases near the boundary, not by the bulk of each class, which makes it robust to how the easy examples are distributed but sensitive to mislabelled or outlying examples near the boundary.

<Step n={3} title="Soft margins and the hinge loss" minutes={30} />

Real classes overlap, and a hard margin may not exist. The **soft-margin SVM** lets examples violate the margin at a price:

$$
\min_{\mathbf w, b}\ \tfrac12\lVert\mathbf w\rVert^2 + C\sum_i \max\!\left(0,\ 1 - y_if(\mathbf x_i)\right).
$$

The second term is the **hinge loss**: zero for examples beyond the margin on the right side, rising linearly for those inside it or misclassified. It is ridge-penalised empirical risk minimisation with a particular loss, just like penalised logistic regression, whose loss $\log(1 + e^{-yf})$ is a smooth version of the hinge. A simple solver takes subgradient steps on randomly chosen examples (the Pegasos algorithm).

On overlapping classes, 20,000 subgradient steps give a boundary with 83.1% test accuracy, close to the best possible for these classes, and a mean hinge loss of 0.37: most examples lie beyond the margin, while those inside it or on the wrong side pay linearly. The plot of the three losses shows the family resemblance. <Step n={4} title="Logistic regression's hidden margin" minutes={25} />

On separable data, unpenalised logistic regression has no finite solution, and its weights grow forever. Soudry and colleagues proved in 2018 that the *direction* of the weights nevertheless converges, slowly, to the direction of the hard-margin SVM. Check it on the separable data of Step 2.

The weight norm keeps growing (3.7, 6.4, 9.9 and 12.8 at the checkpoints), as Week 56 showed, but the direction converges: the angle to the hard-margin SVM falls from 4.4 degrees after 100 steps to 0.7 degrees after 50,000. Among the infinitely many separating directions, gradient descent has an implicit preference, and this is the simplest proven case of a phenomenon that is thought to matter a great deal for deep networks.
