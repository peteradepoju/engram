<Step n={1} title="One split is not enough" minutes={20} />

Week 54 split the data into training, validation and test sets. With the few hundred trials of a typical neuroscience session, each of those sets is small, and the score on a small test set is noisy: a test set of 50 trials measures an accuracy of 70% with a standard error of about 6.5 percentage points. **K-fold cross-validation** uses the data more efficiently. Split the trials into $K$ folds; train on $K - 1$ of them and test on the one left out; rotate, so that every trial is tested exactly once; and average. Varoquaux and colleagues (2017), today's paper, studied how well this works for brain decoding, and found the error bars to be much larger than most papers assumed.

<PyCell code={String.raw`
  import numpy as np
  import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

  def population(n, g, p=50, signal=0.25):
      """n trials of p neurons; the stimulus (+1 or -1) shifts every neuron's rate a little, along a random direction."""
      w = np.random.default_rng(0).normal(size=p)
      y = np.where(g.random(n) < 0.5, 1.0, -1.0)
      X = signal * np.outer(y, w) + g.normal(size=(n, p))
      return X, y

  def ridge_fit(X, y, lam):
      mu = X.mean(0)
      Xc = X - mu
      w = np.linalg.solve(Xc.T @ Xc + lam * np.eye(X.shape[1]), Xc.T @ (y - y.mean()))
      return w, mu, y.mean()

  def ridge_acc(model, X, y):
      w, mu, b = model
      return np.mean(np.sign((X - mu) @ w + b) == y)

  print("ready: population(n, g) simulates trials; ridge_fit and ridge_acc fit and score a ridge decoder")

Both methods agree on the mean, about 85%, but a single split with 30 test trials wanders with a standard deviation of 5 percentage points depending on which trials happened to land in the test set, while the 5-fold estimate, which tests every trial once, wanders by about 2.4. Notice what the spread does not include: both numbers vary only with how this one session was split. A new session, a new animal or a new day would add variability that no amount of reshuffling can reveal.

<Callout kind="note" title="Which K?">

Each training set in K-fold cross-validation has $(K - 1)/K$ of the data, so the estimate describes a model trained on slightly less data than the final one, a small pessimistic bias that shrinks as $K$ grows. Leave-one-out ($K = n$) removes most of that bias but gives highly correlated training sets and, as Varoquaux and colleagues found for decoding, unstable estimates. Five or ten folds, repeated with several shuffles, is the usual compromise.

</Callout>

<Step n={3} title="The optimism of the winner" minutes={25} />

Cross-validation estimates the performance of one fixed procedure. As soon as you compare several, say 12 values of the ridge penalty, and keep the best, the winning score is no longer an estimate of anything: it is the maximum of 12 noisy numbers, and maxima of noisy numbers are biased upwards. Cawley and Talbot (2010) showed that this selection bias can be as large as the differences between learning algorithms that papers report.

Across 20 sessions, the winning cross-validated score averages 78.6%, but the decoders it picked are right on 75.6% of fresh trials: three points of optimism from choosing among 12 penalties with 80 trials. That is a large error in a field where effects of a few points are reported, and it was produced by an analysis that used cross-validation at every step. The penalty chosen is usually a fine one; it is only the reported number that is wrong.

<Step n={4} title="Nested cross-validation" minutes={30} />

The fix is to treat the choice of hyperparameter as part of the learning procedure, and to cross-validate the whole procedure. In **nested cross-validation**, an outer loop splits off a test fold; on the remaining data an inner cross-validation chooses the penalty; the model refitted with that penalty is scored on the outer test fold, which played no part in the choice. The outer average estimates how well "choose a penalty by inner cross-validation, then fit" performs on new data.

The nested estimate averages 71.3%, below the fresh-data accuracy of 75.6%, not above it. The optimism has gone, replaced by the small pessimism of Step 2: each outer model, with its penalty, was trained on 64 trials rather than 80, and with so few trials that costs accuracy. Nested estimates err on the safe side, which is the side to err on. The price is computation, $5 \times 5 \times 12$ fits per session here, and a subtler point: nested cross-validation scores the procedure, so it may choose different penalties in different outer folds. To report a final model, rerun the inner selection on all the data and keep the nested score as its estimate.

<Key title="Before you go">
<ul>
<li>K-fold cross-validation uses every trial for testing once and gives steadier estimates than one split.</li>
<li>The best score among many candidates is optimistic, by more with many candidates and few trials.</li>
<li>Nested cross-validation evaluates the selection procedure honestly.</li>
<li>Report the procedure, the folds and an uncertainty, not just the best number.</li>
</ul>
