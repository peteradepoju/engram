<Step n={1} title="Metrics for decoders" minutes={25} />

On 900 trials of a detection task, the animal saw a faint stimulus on 90 of them. A decoder that always says "no stimulus" is right on 90% of trials and knows nothing. **Balanced accuracy**, the mean of the accuracies within each class, gives it 50%. The **area under the ROC curve** (Week 56) goes further and uses the decoder's continuous score rather than its thresholded decisions: it is the probability that a randomly chosen positive trial scores higher than a randomly chosen negative one.

  import numpy as np
  from scipy.stats import rankdata
  gm = np.random.default_rng(592)

  def balanced_acc(y, pred):

The lazy decoder scores 90% accuracy and exactly 50% balanced accuracy: it has learned the base rate and nothing else. The real decoder, which thresholds the neuron's response, has lower accuracy, 81%, because it raises false alarms on some of the 810 blank trials, but its balanced accuracy of 73.5% shows that it detects the stimulus. Its AUC, 0.83, does not depend on the threshold at all; it would be the same whether the decoder were tuned for few false alarms or few misses. When classes are unbalanced, as with rare events, lapses or a minority of hit trials, report balanced accuracy or AUC, and state chance.

<Step n={2} title="Correlation against R squared" minutes={20} />

For encoding models, two scores dominate. The **correlation** $r$ between predicted and measured responses asks whether the prediction goes up and down at the right times. **R squared**, $1 - \sum(y - \hat y)^2/\sum(y - \bar y)^2$, asks whether it gets the actual values right. A prediction with the right shape but the wrong gain or offset has a perfect correlation and a poor, even negative, R squared.

The mis-scaled prediction and the correct one have the same correlation with the data, 0.97, because correlation is unchanged by multiplying or shifting a prediction. R squared tells them apart: 0.94 for the correct prediction and about −23 for the mis-scaled one, which is far worse than predicting the mean. Which to use depends on the claim. If the model is meant to predict firing rates, say to drive a simulation or a prosthesis, use R squared or a likelihood. If the claim is only that the model captures which stimuli drive the neuron more or less, correlation is defensible, but say so.

<Step n={3} title="The noise ceiling" minutes={35} />

Neurons respond differently to repeats of the same stimulus. Only the part of the response that is the same across repeats, the **signal**, can be predicted by any model of the stimulus; the rest is noise. Schoppe and colleagues (2016), today's paper, compared ways to correct for this, and recommended the **normalised correlation**

$$
CC_{\text{norm}} = \frac{\text{Cov}(\hat y, \bar y)}{\sqrt{\text{Var}(\hat y)\,SP}},
$$

where $\bar y$ is the response averaged over $N$ repeats and $SP$ is the **signal power** of Sahani and Linden (2003), estimated without bias from the repeats as

$$
SP = \frac{1}{N - 1}\left(N\,\text{Var}(\bar y) - \overline{\text{Var}(y_n)}\right),
$$

with $\overline{\text{Var}(y_n)}$ the average over repeats of the variance of each single repeat across stimuli. A perfect model has $CC_{\text{norm}}$ close to 1 however noisy the neuron.

The perfect model, the true tuning curve, correlates with the measured means by only 0.81 on average, and by as little as 0.66 for the noisiest neuron. Read naively, that says the model misses a third of the structure in some neurons; in fact it misses nothing. The normalised correlation averages 1.06, close to 1, with a spread from 0.93 to 1.39 that reflects how hard signal power is to estimate from 5 repeats: for noisy neurons the estimate of $SP$ in the denominator can be small, so individual values can exceed 1. Schoppe and colleagues recommend excluding neurons whose signal power is not reliably above zero, and reporting normalised scores together with the number of repeats.

<Step n={4} title="Bits per spike" minutes={25} />

For spike counts, a natural score is the log-likelihood of held-out data under the model's Poisson rates (Week 35). Raw log-likelihoods depend on the amount of data, so they are reported relative to a model that predicts the neuron's mean rate on every trial, and divided by the number of spikes:

$$
\text{bits per spike} = \frac{\log L_{\text{model}} - \log L_{\text{mean}}}{n_{\text{spikes}}\log 2}.
$$

Zero means no better than the mean rate; negative means worse, which a model that overfits can easily be.

The true rates gain 0.22 bits per spike over the mean-rate model, the half-strength model 0.16, and the overconfident model loses 0.78 bits per spike: exaggerating the tuning makes rare spikes at the troughs, where it predicts almost none, extremely surprising. Likelihood-based scores punish confident errors much more than they reward confident successes, which is why they are good at exposing overfitted encoding models that R squared on trial-averaged data would forgive.

<Key title="Before you go">
<ul>
<li>Choose the metric to match the question; plain accuracy misleads with unbalanced classes.</li>
<li>Correlation measures shape; R squared also requires the right scale and offset.</li>
<li>Trial-to-trial noise caps any model's score; normalise by the noise ceiling.</li>
<li>Score spiking models by held-out bits per spike relative to the mean rate.</li>
</ul>
</Key>
