<Step n={1} title="What leakage is" minutes={15} />

Kaufman and colleagues (2012), today's paper, defined leakage as the introduction of information about the target that would not be legitimately available when the model is used. They gave it a formal definition and a catalogue drawn from data-mining competitions, where leaks repeatedly let teams win with models that would have been useless in practice. Neuroscience has its own catalogue, and its signature is the same: a result that is better than it should be.

<Step n={2} title="Preprocessing inside the loop" minutes={30} />

Any step that learns from the data, whether choosing features, z-scoring, fitting PCA or imputing missing values, is part of the model and must be fitted on the training folds only. The most damaging case is feature selection, because it directly searches for features that separate the labels, including in the trials that will later be used for testing.

Selecting on all 40 trials and then cross-validating reports 92.5% accuracy from neurons that carry no information. With 2,000 candidates, some neurons separate the two labels well by chance in these particular trials, test trials included, and the selection found them. Repeating the selection inside each training fold gives 47.5%, which is chance. Ambroise and McLachlan (2002) found exactly this error in published gene-expression classifiers, where tens of thousands of genes and a few dozen patients produced near-perfect, entirely spurious accuracy.

<Step n={3} title="Time: blocked cross-validation" minutes={30} />

Neural recordings drift: electrodes move, cells adapt, the animal's arousal wanders, the calcium indicator bleaches. Neighbouring trials are therefore more alike than distant ones. If conditions are presented in blocks, a decoder can identify the condition from the drift alone, and a random split puts the near neighbours of every test trial in the training set.

With random folds the nearest-neighbour decoder reaches 80% accuracy, although the neurons know nothing about the condition: every test trial has a neighbour in time, in the same block and so with the same label, sitting in the training set with almost the same drifted rates. Folds of consecutive trials remove the neighbours and the accuracy falls to 48%. For data that are correlated in time, such as blocked designs, continuous behaviour or calcium imaging, use contiguous folds, and consider leaving a gap of a few trials between training and test segments, so that slow drift cannot bridge the boundary.

<Step n={4} title="Sessions and animals: group splits" minutes={30} />

The unit of splitting must match the unit of the claim. A claim that "this decoder will work on a new trial from these sessions" is tested by splitting trials. A claim that "the code generalises to new sessions or new animals" must be tested by leaving out whole sessions or animals, because trials from one session share things, from electrode positions to the animal's strategy, that a decoder can exploit without learning the code at all.

Splitting by trial gives 82% accuracy: the decoder recognises which session a trial came from by its offsets, and in each session one choice dominates. Leaving each session out in turn gives 58%, close to chance (with only six sessions, chance itself wobbles by several points). Both numbers are correct answers to different questions. The first says that within these sessions choice can be predicted, which here is true only because the animal's bias differed between sessions; the second says that nothing about choice transfers to a new session, which is the claim a paper about a neural code would usually want to make.

<Callout kind="warning" title="Target leakage">

The subtlest leaks are features that are partly computed from the answer. Decoding a choice from activity in a window that ends after the movement began; predicting reaction time from a feature normalised by the trial's duration; using a pupil measure that responds to the reward that followed the choice. Before trusting a decoder, write down, for every feature, the moment it becomes available, and check that it precedes the thing being predicted.

</Callout>

<Key title="Before you go">
<ul>
<li>Leakage produces results that are too good, with no warning.</li>
<li>Fit every data-dependent step inside the cross-validation loop.</li>
<li>Use blocked folds when trials are correlated in time.</li>
<li>Split by the unit your claim generalises over: trial, block, session or animal.</li>
</ul>
