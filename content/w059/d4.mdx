<Step n={1} title="Paired comparison on one test set" minutes={30} />

Dietterich (1998), today's paper, compared statistical tests for the question "is algorithm A more accurate than algorithm B?" by simulating situations where the true answer was known. When there is one test set, he recommended **McNemar's test**. It uses only the trials where the decoders disagree: $b$, where A is right and B wrong, and $c$, the reverse. If they are equally good, disagreements should split evenly, and

$$
\chi^2 = \frac{(|b - c| - 1)^2}{b + c}
$$

has approximately a chi-squared distribution with one degree of freedom. Because both decoders see the same trials, easy and hard trials cancel out, which makes the paired test much more sensitive than comparing the two accuracies as if they were independent.

Decoder A is right on 71.7% of the 300 test trials and B on 65.0%. Treated as two independent proportions, the difference gives p = 0.08, not significant. McNemar's test, which looks only at the trials where they disagree, gives p = 0.003, and the paired bootstrap interval for the difference, 2.7 to 10.7 points, excludes zero. The difference between the analyses is the shared difficulty: most trials are easy or hard for both decoders, and the unpaired test counts that shared variation as noise. Report the interval as well as the p-value: it says the improvement is real but could be as small as three points.

<Step n={2} title="Better than chance? Permutation tests" minutes={30} />

Is a cross-validated accuracy of 62% on 80 trials better than chance? The binomial distribution is the wrong null, because cross-validation folds are not independent tests. The robust answer is a **permutation test** (Ojala and Garriga, 2010): shuffle the labels, which destroys any real relation between activity and label, run the entire cross-validation pipeline on the shuffled data, and repeat many times. The p-value is the fraction of shuffles that score at least as well as the real labels, counting the real labels as one of them.

The decoder scores 72.5%. Across 200 shuffles the null accuracies centre on 49.7%, and only 5% of them reach 60%, so 72.5% is well outside the null: p = 0.005, which is the smallest value 200 shuffles can give, 1/201. With more shuffles the p-value could be smaller, so for a strong effect report "p below 0.005 with 200 permutations" rather than a precise number. The key detail is that each shuffle reran the folds and the centroid fitting: any leak or bias in the pipeline is present in the null too, so it cannot fake significance.

<Step n={3} title="The trouble with t-tests on folds" minutes={30} />

The obvious way to compare two algorithms is to run them on many random train and test splits and do a paired t-test on the differences. Dietterich showed that this test rejects far too often when the algorithms are equally good, because the splits overlap: the training sets share most of their trials, so the differences are positively correlated, and the t-test treats them as independent. Nadeau and Bengio (2003) proposed a corrected variance: with $J$ splits and $n_{\text{te}}$ test and $n_{\text{tr}}$ training trials, replace $\hat\sigma^2/J$ by $\left(\frac1J + \frac{n_{\text{te}}}{n_{\text{tr}}}\right)\hat\sigma^2$.

When the two decoders are equally good, the naive t-test declares a significant difference in 36% of datasets, seven times the nominal 5%. The corrected test rejects in 8%, close to nominal. The naive test is wrong because each pair of splits shares most of its training trials and many test trials, so 15 splits contain far less independent information than 15 experiments would. The same mistake appears whenever cross-validation folds, bootstrap resamples or trials from one animal are counted as if they were independent replications.

<Step n={4} title="Reporting" minutes={15} />

A good model comparison reports, for each model, the cross-validated score with its uncertainty and the splitting scheme; the paired difference between models with an interval, not only a p-value; and the result against chance from a permutation test of the whole pipeline. When the question is whether a result holds across animals or sessions, the unit of replication is the animal or the session: compute one score per animal and test across animals, rather than pooling trials.

<Callout kind="note" title="Is chance really 50%?">

With unbalanced classes, small samples or a decoder that tends to predict one class, the accuracy expected by chance can differ from one over the number of classes. Permutation tests take care of this automatically, because the null distribution is computed with the same decoder, the same folds and the same class balance as the real analysis. Combrisson and Jerbi (2015) showed how far theoretical chance levels can mislead with the small samples typical of neuroscience.

</Callout>

<Key title="Before you go">
<ul>
<li>Compare decoders on the same test trials with a paired test or a paired bootstrap.</li>
<li>Test against chance by permuting labels through the whole pipeline.</li>
<li>Do not trust ordinary t-tests across overlapping splits; correct them or avoid them.</li>
<li>Report effect sizes with intervals, and replicate across the unit of your claim.</li>
</ul>
