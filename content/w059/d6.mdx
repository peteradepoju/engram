Leaving each session out, with the penalty chosen inside by leaving out each of the other three sessions in turn, the decoder is right on 75.7% of trials in sessions it never saw. None of 40 within-session label shuffles reaches more than 53%, so p = 1/41, the smallest value 40 permutations can give. Every choice in this analysis followed from the claim: sessions are the unit, so they define the folds; the scaling and the penalty are part of the model, so they are fitted inside; and the null keeps each session's structure, offsets and drift intact while breaking only the link to choice. It is slower than a single cross-validation, and that is the cost of a number that means what it says.

Datasets of 100 trials, all drawn from the same population, report cross-validated accuracies from 52% to 73% (the middle 95%), around a mean of 64%. Their spread, 5.4 points, is a little larger than the binomial standard error of 4.8 points, because each estimate also depends on the particular training sets. Two laboratories running the same experiment with a hundred trials each could honestly report 55% and 72%. This is Varoquaux's central warning: with typical sample sizes, the error bars on decoding accuracy are around ten points wide in each direction, wider than many of the differences that papers interpret.

<Prove title="1. McNemar's test" task="Two classifiers are equally accurate. Of the $m = b + c$ test trials on which they disagree, A is right on $b$. Show that, given $m$, $b$ is Binomial($m$, 1/2), and derive the statistic $(b - c)^2/(b + c)$ from a normal approximation.">

Under the null, each disagreement is equally likely to favour A or B, independently across trials, so given $m$, $b \sim \text{Binomial}(m, \frac12)$, with mean $m/2$ and variance $m/4$.

Standardise: $z = (b - m/2)/\sqrt{m/4} = (2b - m)/\sqrt m = (b - c)/\sqrt{b + c}$, since $m = b + c$.

For large $m$, $z$ is approximately standard normal, so $z^2 = (b - c)^2/(b + c)$ is approximately chi-squared with one degree of freedom. Replacing $|b - c|$ by $|b - c| - 1$ is a continuity correction for the discreteness of $b$. Trials on which both classifiers agree carry no information about which is better, which is why they drop out.

</Prove>

<Prove title="2. Regression to the mean" task="A neuron's true response is $\mu_i \sim \mathcal N(\mu, \tau^2)$ and each day's measurement adds independent noise $\mathcal N(0, \sigma^2)$. Show that the expected day-2 measurement given the day-1 measurement $x_1$ is $\mu + \rho(x_1 - \mu)$ with $\rho = \tau^2/(\tau^2 + \sigma^2)$.">

The day-2 measurement is $x_2 = \mu_i + \epsilon_2$ with $\epsilon_2$ independent of everything on day 1, so $E[x_2 \mid x_1] = E[\mu_i \mid x_1]$.

$(\mu_i, x_1)$ are jointly Gaussian with $\text{Cov}(\mu_i, x_1) = \tau^2$ and $\text{Var}(x_1) = \tau^2 + \sigma^2$. The conditional mean of a Gaussian (Week 16) is

$$
E[\mu_i \mid x_1] = \mu + \frac{\tau^2}{\tau^2 + \sigma^2}(x_1 - \mu).
$$

So day-2 values are pulled towards the mean by the factor $\rho$, the reliability of a single measurement. With $\tau^2 = \sigma^2$, as in Day 5, half of any day-1 deviation is expected to vanish.

</Prove>
