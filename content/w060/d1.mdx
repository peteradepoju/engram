<Step n={1} title="Clustering without labels" minutes={15} />

A **clustering** assigns each of $n$ points to one of $k$ groups so that points in the same group are alike. "Alike" must be defined, and the definition is the model. k-means defines it by squared Euclidean distance to a group centre and asks for the assignment $z_i$ and centres $\boldsymbol\mu_1, \dots, \boldsymbol\mu_k$ that minimise the **distortion**, or within-cluster sum of squares,

$$
J = \sum_{i=1}^n\lVert\mathbf x_i - \boldsymbol\mu_{z_i}\rVert^2.
$$

Lloyd devised the algorithm in 1957 at Bell Labs to choose the levels of a quantiser for pulse-code modulation, finding the $k$ values that best represent a signal; his report was published in 1982, today's paper. Representing each neuron by one of $k$ prototype responses is the same problem.

<Step n={2} title="Lloyd's algorithm" minutes={35} />

<Derivation title="k-means is coordinate descent">
<div>

$J$ depends on two sets of unknowns, the assignments and the centres. Minimising over both at once is hard (it is NP-hard in general), but minimising over one with the other fixed is easy.

</div>
<div>

**Assignment step.** With the centres fixed, each term of $J$ involves only one $z_i$, so set $z_i = \arg\min_j\lVert\mathbf x_i - \boldsymbol\mu_j\rVert^2$: the nearest centre. This cannot increase $J$.

</div>
<div>

**Update step.** With the assignments fixed, $J$ splits into one sum per cluster. Setting the gradient of $\sum_{i: z_i = j}\lVert\mathbf x_i - \boldsymbol\mu_j\rVert^2$ to zero gives $\boldsymbol\mu_j = $ the mean of the points in cluster $j$. This cannot increase $J$ either.

</div>
<div>

$J$ is bounded below by 0 and never increases, and there are finitely many assignments, so the algorithm stops after finitely many steps, at a point where neither step can improve $J$: a local minimum, not necessarily the global one.

</div>
</Derivation>

Seven of the ten starts reach the same distortion, 850.6, and that solution matches the true classes on 98.7% of neurons, converging in five iterations. The other three stop at 1,115 or 1,428: local minima in which two centres share one class while another class is merged with a neighbour. Nothing in a single run warns you of this; only comparing the distortion across starts does. The few neurons the best solution misassigns are mostly low-gain ones whose responses are hard to tell apart through the noise, sustained cells with weak gain looking like ramping ones, for example.

<Step n={3} title="Starting points and k-means++" minutes={25} />

Random starts often place two centres in one group and none in another, and Lloyd's algorithm cannot move a centre across a gap. Arthur and Vassilvitskii (2007) proposed **k-means++**: choose the first centre at random, then each further centre at random with probability proportional to its squared distance from the nearest centre already chosen. Distant points are favoured, so centres spread out, and the expected distortion is provably within a factor of order $\log k$ of the optimum.

With eight groups, single random starts reach the best solution only 15% of the time, and k-means++ seeds 55% of the time. k-means++ is not a guarantee, which is why in practice it is combined with several restarts, but it makes each restart much more likely to count. Its cost is a pass over the data per centre, negligible beside the iterations that follow.

<Step n={4} title="What k-means assumes" minutes={30} />

Because it uses only distances to centres, k-means carves space into convex cells, the **Voronoi** regions of the centres, with boundaries halfway between them. It works best when clusters are round, of similar spread and of similar size. Unequal spreads and sizes pull boundaries in the wrong place; elongated clusters are cut across; and scaling the features changes the answer, because it changes the distances.

Overall agreement is 95%, which sounds like success, but it hides a systematic error: 12 of the 40 wide-group neurons, 30%, are assigned to the tight cluster, and the wide cluster's centre is pushed out to 5.6 instead of 4. k-means places the boundary halfway between the centres, ignoring that one group is five times as spread as the other, so wide-group points that stray towards the tight group are captured. Whenever the agreement statistic is dominated by a large group, check each group separately. Day 2's Gaussian mixtures give each cluster its own spread and weight, which is the principled fix.

<Key title="Before you go">
<ul>
<li>k-means minimises within-cluster squared distance by alternating assignment and mean updates.</li>
<li>It converges to a local minimum; use k-means++ seeds and several restarts.</li>
<li>Its boundaries are halfway between centres, so it prefers round, equal clusters.</li>
<li>Feature scaling and the choice of k are decisions you make, not ones it makes.</li>
</ul>
</Key>
