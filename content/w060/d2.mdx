<Step n={1} title="A generative model for clusters" minutes={20} />

A **Gaussian mixture model** describes how each data point could have been generated: first choose a cluster $z \in \{1, \dots, K\}$ with probability $\pi_z$, then draw $\mathbf x \sim \mathcal N(\boldsymbol\mu_z, \Sigma_z)$. The label $z$ is hidden, so the probability of a point is a sum over the clusters it might have come from,

$$
p(\mathbf x) = \sum_{k=1}^K\pi_k\,\mathcal N(\mathbf x;\boldsymbol\mu_k, \Sigma_k).
$$

Given the parameters, Bayes' rule gives the probability that point $i$ came from cluster $k$, its **responsibility**,

$$
r_{ik} = \frac{\pi_k\,\mathcal N(\mathbf x_i;\boldsymbol\mu_k, \Sigma_k)}{\sum_j\pi_j\,\mathcal N(\mathbf x_i;\boldsymbol\mu_j, \Sigma_j)}.
$$

This is the generative classifier of Week 56 (quadratic discriminant analysis), but with the labels unknown.

<Step n={2} title="Expectation maximisation" minutes={35} />

The log-likelihood $\sum_i\log\sum_k\pi_k\mathcal N(\mathbf x_i;\boldsymbol\mu_k, \Sigma_k)$ has a sum inside the logarithm, so setting its gradient to zero gives no closed form. Dempster, Laird and Rubin (1977), today's paper, unified many earlier special cases into one algorithm for such problems.

<Derivation title="EM for a Gaussian mixture">
<div>

If the labels were known, the problem would be easy: each cluster's mean and covariance would be the sample mean and covariance of its points, and $\pi_k$ the fraction of points in it, exactly as in Week 56.

</div>
<div>

**E step.** We do not know the labels, but with the current parameters we know each point's responsibilities $r_{ik}$. Treat them as soft labels: point $i$ counts as $r_{ik}$ of a point in cluster $k$.

</div>
<div>

**M step.** Refit each cluster with these fractional counts. With $N_k = \sum_ir_{ik}$:

$$
\pi_k = \frac{N_k}{n},\quad \boldsymbol\mu_k = \frac{1}{N_k}\sum_ir_{ik}\mathbf x_i,\quad \Sigma_k = \frac{1}{N_k}\sum_ir_{ik}(\mathbf x_i - \boldsymbol\mu_k)(\mathbf x_i - \boldsymbol\mu_k)^\top.
$$

</div>
<div>

Dempster and colleagues proved that each E step followed by an M step cannot decrease the log-likelihood. Week 63 shows why: EM is coordinate ascent on a lower bound of the log-likelihood, and the E step makes the bound tight. Like k-means, EM finds a local maximum, so it needs good starts.

</div>
<div>

k-means is the limit of this algorithm when all covariances are fixed at $\sigma^2I$ with $\sigma \to 0$: the responsibilities become 0 or 1, the nearest centre wins, and the M step computes means.

</div>
</Derivation>

The mixture assigns 98.3% of points to their true group, k-means 87.5%. k-means draws straight boundaries halfway between centres, so the long tilted group, whose ends reach towards its neighbours, loses its tips to them. The mixture's boundaries are curves where two weighted Gaussian densities are equal, the quadratic boundaries of Week 56, and they follow each group's shape. The price is parameters: each full covariance in $d$ dimensions has $d(d+1)/2$ of them, so in high dimensions mixtures need diagonal or shared covariances, or a reduction to a few dimensions first.

<Step n={4} title="Collapsing components" minutes={25} />

The likelihood of a Gaussian mixture has no maximum. Put one component's mean exactly on a data point and shrink its covariance: the density at that point grows without bound, while the other components keep every other point's density finite, so the total likelihood goes to infinity. EM can fall into these singularities, especially with many components, few points or duplicated values. The standard remedy is to add a small multiple of the identity to every covariance, which is equivalent to a prior on the covariances (a maximum a posteriori estimate rather than maximum likelihood).

Adding a fourth component that sits on one data point with a vanishing covariance raises the log-likelihood from −1,999 to −1,963, although it explains nothing: it is one point, described perfectly. Make the covariance smaller still and the likelihood rises further, without limit. With regularisation of 0.01 on the diagonal, no fitted covariance in the small-sample fit can shrink below a determinant of $10^{-4}$, and the smallest sits exactly there: a component that has collapsed onto one or two of the 20 points as far as the regularisation allows. That is the warning sign to look for: a component with tiny weight and tiny covariance means too many components for the data.

<li>A Gaussian mixture is a generative model with a hidden cluster label.</li>
<li>EM alternates soft assignment (E) and weighted refitting (M), never decreasing the likelihood.</li>
<li>Mixtures handle clusters of different shapes, sizes and weights; k-means is a limiting case.</li>
<li>The likelihood is unbounded; regularise covariances and use several starts.</li>
