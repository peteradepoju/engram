<Step n={1} title="Agglomerative clustering" minutes={30} />

**Agglomerative** hierarchical clustering starts with every point in its own cluster and repeatedly merges the two closest clusters until one remains. The sequence of merges, and the distance at which each happened, form a tree called a **dendrogram**; cutting it at a height gives a clustering, and different heights give coarser or finer ones. What "closest clusters" means is the **linkage**: single linkage uses the closest pair of points between the clusters, complete linkage the farthest pair, average linkage the mean over all pairs.

<Step n={2} title="Linkage and the shape of clusters" minutes={25} />

Single linkage merges clusters that have any pair of close points, so it follows chains and curved shapes, but a few points bridging two groups can join them (**chaining**). Complete linkage requires all pairs to be close, so it prefers compact, roughly equal clusters, much like k-means. Average linkage sits between. The dendrogram from scipy's `linkage` can be cut into a fixed number of clusters with `fcluster(Z, k, criterion="maxclust")`.

On clean rings single linkage is perfect: within each ring, neighbouring points are 0.1 or less apart, while the rings are 2 apart, so all within-ring merges happen first. Complete linkage scores 66%, cutting both rings with a straight line much as k-means does, because it insists on compact clusters. <Step n={3} title="Graphs and the Laplacian" minutes={25} />

Spectral clustering, reviewed by von Luxburg (2007), today's paper, starts from a **similarity graph**: nodes are points, and edge weights $w_{ij}$ are large for nearby points, for instance $w_{ij} = e^{-\lVert\mathbf x_i - \mathbf x_j\rVert^2/2\sigma^2}$. A good clustering cuts few heavy edges.

<Derivation title="Cuts and the Laplacian">
<div>

Let $D$ be the diagonal matrix of degrees $d_i = \sum_jw_{ij}$ and define the **graph Laplacian** $L = D - W$. For any vector $\mathbf f$,

$$
\mathbf f^\top L\mathbf f = \frac12\sum_{i,j}w_{ij}(f_i - f_j)^2.
$$

</div>
<div>

So $L$ is positive semi-definite, and $\mathbf f^\top L\mathbf f$ is small when $\mathbf f$ changes little across heavy edges. The constant vector gives zero. If the graph has $k$ disconnected components, the indicator vectors of the components all give zero: the multiplicity of the eigenvalue 0 is the number of components.

</div>
<div>

If $\mathbf f$ is the indicator of a cluster, $\mathbf f^\top L\mathbf f$ is the total weight of edges cut. Minimising cuts with balanced cluster sizes (the ratio cut or normalised cut) is NP-hard, but relaxing $\mathbf f$ from indicators to real vectors turns it into an eigenvalue problem: take the eigenvectors of the smallest eigenvalues.

</div>
<div>

In practice: compute the first $k$ eigenvectors of the normalised Laplacian $L_{\text{sym}} = I - D^{-1/2}WD^{-1/2}$, put them as columns of an $n \times k$ matrix, normalise its rows to unit length, and run k-means on the rows (Ng, Jordan and Weiss, 2002). Points in the same weakly connected piece of the graph land close together in this embedding, whatever their shape in the original space.

</div>
</Derivation>

  from scipy.cluster.vq import kmeans2
  Xb = np.vstack([Xr, bridge])
  sigma = 0.3
  D2 = ((Xb[:, None] - Xb[None]) ** 2).sum(-1)
  W = np.exp(-D2 / (2 * sigma ** 2)); np.fill_diagonal(W, 0)
  d = W.sum(1)
  Lsym = np.eye(len(d)) - W / np.sqrt(np.outer(d, d))
  vals, vecs = np.linalg.eigh(Lsym)
  U = vecs[:, :2]
  U = U / np.linalg.norm(U, axis=1, keepdims=True)
  _, lab_spec = kmeans2(U, 2, minit="++", seed=1)
  acc_spec = acc2(lab_spec[:300], yr)

Spectral clustering separates the rings perfectly, bridging points and all. The smallest eigenvalues show why: the first is 0, as always, and the second is only 0.0003, nearly a second zero, which means the graph almost falls into two components. The few bridging points add only light edges, because with a width of 0.3 each is weakly connected to anything, and the normalised cut sees through them where single linkage could not. The price is the width $\sigma$: too small and the graph shatters into many pieces; too large and the rings blur into one. Von Luxburg's tutorial discusses rules for choosing it, such as connecting each point to its nearest neighbours instead.
