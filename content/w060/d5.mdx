<Step n={1} title="What a spike's shape says" minutes={20} />

The extracellular spike is roughly the negative of the time derivative of the membrane potential near the electrode, filtered by the tissue. Neurons with fast potassium channels, such as parvalbumin-expressing fast-spiking interneurons (Kv3 channels, Week 30), repolarise quickly and have **narrow** spikes, with a short time from trough to peak; most pyramidal cells have **broad** spikes. The split works well in rodent cortex, but not everywhere: some pyramidal neurons in primate motor cortex, including the large Betz cells, have narrow spikes (Vigneswaran and colleagues, 2011), and spike shape changes with the electrode's position relative to the cell. Lee and colleagues (2021), today's paper, showed that clustering the full waveform with nonlinear methods finds more than two types in monkey premotor cortex, with different physiology and laminar positions.

The three types separate along the features as designed: narrow-spiking units reach their peak 0.31 ms after the trough, with a tall after-peak (0.68 of the trough) that is over by 0.8 ms; the two broad types peak at 0.65 and 0.84 ms, and differ most in how much of the after-peak remains at 0.8 ms. Timing is located to a fraction of a sample with a parabola through the three samples around each extremum, because at 30 kHz one sample is 0.033 ms, and features that can take only a few discrete values make mixture models collapse onto the lattice (Day 2). Real sorters upsample waveforms for the same reason.

BIC falls steeply to three components, then barely changes, reaching its minimum at five; the curve from 3 to 6 is almost flat, so the data support at least three groups and say little about more. Forcing three components, the hand-made features match the true types on 92% of units and the first three principal components of the normalised waveform on 96%. The principal components use the whole shape, including features that no one thought to measure, which is the argument Lee and colleagues made for clustering full waveforms. The hand-made features have the advantage that each axis means something physiologically, which matters when the clusters must be explained.

<Step n={4} title="Validation with tagged cells" minutes={30} />

In a real recording the true types are unknown for almost every unit. A few can be identified independently: by **optogenetic tagging**, expressing a light-gated channel in one genetic class and finding the units that spike within a few milliseconds of a light pulse; by juxtacellular labelling; or by connectivity inferred from cross-correlograms. These few cells are the only check on the clusters. Here, 40 units of type 0 have been tagged, but tagging is biased towards cells close to the light and with large spikes.

All 40 tagged cells fall in one cluster, the narrow-spiking one. Even so, the 95% interval for the capture rate runs from 0.91 to 1: forty cells cannot show that more than about 90% of the class falls in the cluster. And tagging tells you nothing directly about purity, here 99.5%, known only because this is a simulation; in a real recording, broad-spiking cells of other classes in the narrow cluster would go unnoticed unless they too were tagged. The bias of tagging towards large-amplitude units adds a further caveat: the cells most easily tagged are those with the cleanest waveforms, which are also the easiest to classify.

<Callout kind="warning" title="What a waveform cluster can claim">

A cluster of narrow-spiking units is a **putative** type. Tagging measures how many of one class fall in the cluster (its sensitivity) but says little about how many units in the cluster belong to the class (its purity), which needs tagged cells of the other classes too. Waveform shape also depends on where the electrode sits relative to the soma and dendrites, on the filter settings, and on the species and area. Report clusters with these caveats, and treat claims such as "interneurons do X" as hypotheses for tagging experiments to test.

<Key title="Before you go">
<ul>
<li>Spike width reflects repolarisation and gives a clue, not a label, to cell type.</li>
<li>Mixtures on standardised features, with BIC, give putative types; whole-waveform PCs may separate more.</li>
<li>Validate clusters with independently identified cells, and report intervals.</li>
<li>Distinguish sensitivity from purity, and call clusters putative.</li>
</ul>
