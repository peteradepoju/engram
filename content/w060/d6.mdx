  gA = np.random.default_rng(606)
  state = gA.random(1000) < 0.35                       # True in the up state
  counts = gA.poisson(np.where(state, 6.0, 0.4))

  def poisson_mixture(x, K, iters=200):

      pi = np.full(K, 1 / K); hist = []
      for _ in range(iters):
          logp = np.log(pi) + x[:, None] * np.log(lam) - lam - gammaln(x[:, None] + 1)
          hist.append(logsumexp(logp, axis=1).sum())
          R = np.exp(logp - logsumexp(logp, axis=1, keepdims=True))   # E step

          lam = (R * x[:, None]).sum(0) / Nk

              break
      return pi, lam, R, hist

In 15 iterations EM recovers an up-state fraction of 0.36 (truth 0.35) and rates of 0.38 and 6.06 spikes per bin (truth 0.4 and 6), and labels the hidden state correctly in 97.5% of bins. The misses are bins with one or two spikes, which are plausible in either state, and their responsibilities say so: they sit near 0.5 rather than at 0 or 1. At $\sigma = 2$ every responsibility is 1/3: the components are so wide that all three centres are pulled to the overall mean of the data and stay there, so the soft clustering sees one group. By $\sigma = 1$ the centres have separated, responsibilities average 0.93, and the hard assignments already agree with k-means on every point; by $\sigma = 0.05$ the responsibilities are 0 or 1 to three decimals and EM is k-means. The collapse at large $\sigma$ is a phase transition: below a critical width, set by the spread of the data, the single solution splits. Deterministic annealing (Rose, 1998) exploits it, starting with a wide $\sigma$ and lowering it gradually, so that clusters split one at a time and poor local minima are avoided.

<Prove title="1. The M step for a mixture mean" task="Holding the responsibilities $r_{ik}$ fixed, show that the value of $\boldsymbol\mu_k$ maximising $\sum_ir_{ik}\log\mathcal N(\mathbf x_i;\boldsymbol\mu_k, \Sigma_k)$ is $\sum_ir_{ik}\mathbf x_i/\sum_ir_{ik}$.">

Up to terms that do not involve $\boldsymbol\mu_k$, the objective is $-\frac12\sum_ir_{ik}(\mathbf x_i - \boldsymbol\mu_k)^\top\Sigma_k^{-1}(\mathbf x_i - \boldsymbol\mu_k)$.

Its gradient with respect to $\boldsymbol\mu_k$ is $\sum_ir_{ik}\Sigma_k^{-1}(\mathbf x_i - \boldsymbol\mu_k)$. Setting it to zero and multiplying by $\Sigma_k$ gives $\sum_ir_{ik}\mathbf x_i = \boldsymbol\mu_k\sum_ir_{ik}$.

So $\boldsymbol\mu_k = \sum_ir_{ik}\mathbf x_i/N_k$: the weighted mean, whatever $\Sigma_k$ is. The objective is concave in $\boldsymbol\mu_k$, so this is the maximum. The Poisson rate of Part A follows in exactly the same way.

</Prove>

<Prove title="2. The Laplacian quadratic form" task="For a symmetric weight matrix $W$ with degrees $d_i = \sum_jw_{ij}$ and $L = D - W$, show that $\mathbf f^\top L\mathbf f = \frac12\sum_{i,j}w_{ij}(f_i - f_j)^2$.">

Expand the right-hand side: $\frac12\sum_{i,j}w_{ij}(f_i^2 - 2f_if_j + f_j^2)$.

The first and last terms are equal by the symmetry of $W$, and each is $\frac12\sum_id_if_i^2$. Together they give $\sum_id_if_i^2 = \mathbf f^\top D\mathbf f$.

The middle term is $-\sum_{i,j}w_{ij}f_if_j = -\mathbf f^\top W\mathbf f$.

So the sum is $\mathbf f^\top(D - W)\mathbf f = \mathbf f^\top L\mathbf f$. Since every term $w_{ij}(f_i - f_j)^2$ is non-negative, $L$ is positive semi-definite, and $\mathbf f^\top L\mathbf f = 0$ exactly when $\mathbf f$ is constant on every connected piece of the graph.

</Prove>
