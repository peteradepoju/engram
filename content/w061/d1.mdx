<Step n={1} title="Shared and private variability" minutes={20} />

**Factor analysis** (FA) says that each population vector $\mathbf x \in \mathbb R^d$ is generated from $q \ll d$ latent factors:

$$
\mathbf z \sim \mathcal N(\mathbf 0, I_q),\qquad \mathbf x = \boldsymbol\mu + L\mathbf z + \boldsymbol\epsilon,\qquad \boldsymbol\epsilon \sim \mathcal N(\mathbf 0, \Psi),
$$

with a **loading matrix** $L$ ($d \times q$) and a diagonal $\Psi$ of private variances. The model's covariance is $LL^\top + \Psi$: shared covariance of low rank, plus independent noise on each neuron. If $\Psi = \sigma^2I$, all neurons equally noisy, the model is probabilistic PCA (Week 63), and its maximum-likelihood loadings span the top principal components. With unequal private noise, the two differ. Santhanam and colleagues (2009), today's paper, showed that modelling the shared and private parts separately in this way improved the decoding of reach targets from motor cortex for neural prostheses.

<Step n={2} title="EM for factor analysis" minutes={35} />

<Derivation title="The E and M steps">
<div>

Given $\mathbf x$, the factors have a Gaussian posterior (Week 16). With $\beta = L^\top(LL^\top + \Psi)^{-1}$, its mean is $E[\mathbf z \mid \mathbf x] = \beta(\mathbf x - \boldsymbol\mu)$ and its second moment, averaged over the data with covariance $S$, is $E[\mathbf z\mathbf z^\top] = I - \beta L + \beta S\beta^\top$.

</div>
<div>

If the factors were known, $L$ would be found by regressing $\mathbf x$ on $\mathbf z$. Replacing the unknown sufficient statistics by their expectations gives the M step:

$$
L \leftarrow S\beta^\top\left(E[\mathbf z\mathbf z^\top]\right)^{-1},\qquad \Psi \leftarrow \text{diag}\left(S - L\beta S\right).
$$

</div>
<div>

The model is unchanged if $L$ is replaced by $LR$ for any rotation $R$, so only the subspace spanned by the loadings is identifiable, not the individual factors. Compare subspaces, for example by principal angles, not individual columns.

</div>
</Derivation>

EM climbs steadily for 235 iterations, and factor analysis finds a subspace within 5 degrees of the truth. The top two principal components are 35 degrees away: the two neurons with private variance of 12 have the largest total variance in the population, so PCA spends part of its two components on them. Nothing about those neurons is shared with the rest; their variance is just large. Factor analysis assigns it to $\Psi$ and fits the loadings to the covariance between neurons, which is what the shared factors produce.

<Step n={3} title="How many factors?" minutes={25} />

Factor analysis is a probability model, so the number of factors can be chosen as in Week 59, by held-out likelihood: fit on training trials, then evaluate $\log\mathcal N(\mathbf x;\boldsymbol\mu, LL^\top + \Psi)$ on test trials. Unlike the variance explained by PCA, which always grows with more components, held-out likelihood falls once extra factors start fitting noise.

Held-out likelihood rises steeply from one factor to two, by about 480 units, and then is flat: three factors score 0.6 units higher than two, and four and five slightly lower. Differences of a unit or two on 100 test trials are noise, so the rule takes the smallest number of factors within 2 units of the best, the same logic as the one-standard-error rule. Probabilistic PCA with two components scores far lower, −3,714 against −3,024, because forcing equal noise on every neuron fits the two noisy neurons badly. When neurons differ in reliability, as recorded neurons always do, factor analysis is the better model of the population.

<Step n={4} title="Shared variance per neuron" minutes={25} />

Factor analysis splits each neuron's variance into a shared part, the diagonal of $LL^\top$, and a private part, $\psi_i$. The fraction $\text{diag}(LL^\top)_i/(\text{diag}(LL^\top)_i + \psi_i)$, sometimes called the neuron's **communality** or percent shared variance, has become a standard summary in population studies: it changes with attention, learning and brain state, and these changes can be measured without knowing what the shared factors represent (Williamson and colleagues, 2016).

<Key title="Before you go">
<ul>
<li>Factor analysis separates shared covariance (LLᵀ) from private noise (diagonal Ψ).</li>
<li>EM fits it with the same logic as mixtures: infer the latent, refit as if it were known.</li>
<li>PCA is pulled by noisy neurons; factor analysis is not.</li>
<li>Choose the number of factors by held-out likelihood; summarise neurons by percent shared variance.</li>
