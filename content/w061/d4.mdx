<Step n={1} title="From distances to neighbours" minutes={25} />

t-SNE, introduced by van der Maaten and Hinton (2008), today's paper, turns distances into probabilities. For each point $i$, it defines the probability that $i$ would pick $j$ as its neighbour,

$$
p_{j\mid i} = \frac{\exp(-\lVert\mathbf x_i - \mathbf x_j\rVert^2/2\sigma_i^2)}{\sum_{k \ne i}\exp(-\lVert\mathbf x_i - \mathbf x_k\rVert^2/2\sigma_i^2)},
$$

with a bandwidth $\sigma_i$ chosen separately for each point so that the distribution has a fixed **perplexity**, $2^{H}$ where $H$ is its entropy in bits. Perplexity is a smooth count of effective neighbours, typically 5 to 50. Points in dense regions get small $\sigma_i$ and points in sparse regions large ones, so every point has about the same number of neighbours. The joint affinities are symmetrised, $p_{ij} = (p_{j\mid i} + p_{i\mid j})/2n$.

Every row now has exactly the entropy of a distribution over 30 equally likely neighbours, $\log 30 = 3.40$ nats, whatever the local density. The bisection does the work: trials in the spread class get wider kernels, trials in tight classes narrower ones. That equalisation is deliberate, and it is also the source of t-SNE's best-known distortion, which Step 4 measures: a point in a sparse cluster and a point in a dense one are each given the same number of close neighbours.

<Step n={2} title="The embedding and its gradient" minutes={30} />

<Derivation title="Student t similarities and the KL gradient">
<div>

In the two-dimensional embedding, similarities use a Student t distribution with one degree of freedom, which has heavy tails:

$$
q_{ij} = \frac{(1 + \lVert\mathbf y_i - \mathbf y_j\rVert^2)^{-1}}{\sum_{k \ne l}(1 + \lVert\mathbf y_k - \mathbf y_l\rVert^2)^{-1}}.
$$

</div>
<div>

The heavy tails solve the **crowding problem**: in 50 dimensions a point can have many neighbours all at moderate distance, but in two there is not enough room. Heavy tails let moderately distant points be placed far apart at little cost, which opens up gaps between clusters.

</div>
<div>

The embedding minimises the Kullback-Leibler divergence $\text{KL}(P\Vert Q) = \sum_{ij}p_{ij}\log(p_{ij}/q_{ij})$. Its gradient has a simple form:

$$
\frac{\partial\,\text{KL}}{\partial\mathbf y_i} = 4\sum_j(p_{ij} - q_{ij})(\mathbf y_i - \mathbf y_j)(1 + \lVert\mathbf y_i - \mathbf y_j\rVert^2)^{-1}.
$$

Pairs with $p_{ij} \gt q_{ij}$ attract; pairs with $p_{ij} \lt q_{ij}$ repel. KL punishes placing true neighbours far apart heavily, but placing non-neighbours close together only lightly, so t-SNE preserves local structure and largely ignores global structure.

</div>
<div>

Two tricks help optimisation: **early exaggeration**, multiplying $P$ by about 12 for the first iterations so that clusters form tightly and can move past each other, and momentum.

</div>
</Derivation>

Your gradient matches the finite-difference gradient of the KL divergence, and 500 iterations produce five well-separated islands: every trial's ten nearest neighbours in the embedding belong to its own class. In the PCA plane the figure is 92%, because the two largest directions of variance are spent on the far class and on the spread one, leaving the others partly overlapping. Try a different seed in run_tsne: the islands move and rotate, because the KL divergence does not care about global arrangement.

<Step n={4} title="What t-SNE does not keep" minutes={30} />

Because every point is given the same number of effective neighbours, dense and sparse clusters are scaled to similar sizes; because KL largely ignores pairs that are far apart, the distances between clusters in the embedding say little about the distances in the data. Wattenberg, Viégas and Johnson (2016) showed many such effects in an interactive essay.

For questions about sizes and distances, measure them in the original space, or in a linear projection such as PCA, which preserves them as far as two dimensions allow.

<Key title="Before you go">
<ul>
<li>t-SNE matches neighbour probabilities in the data and in the embedding.</li>
<li>Perplexity sets each point's number of effective neighbours.</li>
<li>Heavy-tailed similarities and KL make it preserve local structure and open gaps between clusters.</li>
<li>Do not read cluster sizes, densities or between-cluster distances from a t-SNE plot.</li>
</ul>
</Key>
