From three principal components, speed is decoded with a test R squared of 0.69; from three factor-analysis latents, 0.97. The six very noisy neurons have the largest variances, so PCA devotes much of its three dimensions to their private noise, which carries no information about speed. Factor analysis attributes that variance to $\Psi$, and its posterior mean weights each neuron by its reliability, through the $\Psi^{-1}$ hidden in $\beta$. The two would agree if all neurons were equally noisy, which is never true of real recordings. Santhanam and colleagues found the same advantage for decoding reach targets.

**Part B: choosing the rank of NMF by held-out entries.** There is no test set in a single movie, but there are held-out entries: hide a random 10% of the matrix, fit NMF to the rest with a mask, and measure the error on the hidden entries. Too low a rank cannot reconstruct them; too high a rank fits noise in the visible entries and predicts the hidden ones worse. The masked updates are $H \leftarrow H \odot \frac{W^\top(M \odot X)}{W^\top(M \odot WH)}$ and the same for $W$, with $M$ the mask of visible entries.

Held-out error falls steeply up to rank 4, from 1.60 to 0.12, and then flattens: ranks 5 to 7 lie within a few per cent of each other and of rank 4, with rank 5 nominally lowest by less than the run-to-run variation of NMF itself. The rule of taking the smallest rank close to the best chooses 4, the true number of parts. The same masked-entry trick, sometimes called bi-cross-validation or entry-wise cross-validation, works for PCA, factor analysis and any low-rank model, and it is the honest way to choose a rank when there is only one dataset.

<Prove title="1. The factor-analysis posterior" task="With $\mathbf z \sim \mathcal N(\mathbf 0, I)$ and $\mathbf x = L\mathbf z + \boldsymbol\epsilon$, $\boldsymbol\epsilon \sim \mathcal N(\mathbf 0, \Psi)$ (zero mean for simplicity), show that $E[\mathbf z \mid \mathbf x] = L^\top(LL^\top + \Psi)^{-1}\mathbf x$ and $\text{Cov}(\mathbf z \mid \mathbf x) = I - L^\top(LL^\top + \Psi)^{-1}L$.">

$(\mathbf z, \mathbf x)$ is jointly Gaussian with $\text{Cov}(\mathbf z) = I$, $\text{Cov}(\mathbf x) = LL^\top + \Psi$ and $\text{Cov}(\mathbf z, \mathbf x) = E[\mathbf z(L\mathbf z + \boldsymbol\epsilon)^\top] = L^\top$.

For jointly Gaussian vectors, $E[\mathbf z \mid \mathbf x] = \text{Cov}(\mathbf z, \mathbf x)\text{Cov}(\mathbf x)^{-1}\mathbf x$ and $\text{Cov}(\mathbf z \mid \mathbf x) = \text{Cov}(\mathbf z) - \text{Cov}(\mathbf z, \mathbf x)\text{Cov}(\mathbf x)^{-1}\text{Cov}(\mathbf x, \mathbf z)$ (Week 48).

Substituting gives the result, with $\beta = L^\top(LL^\top + \Psi)^{-1}$. The posterior covariance does not depend on $\mathbf x$, and $E[\mathbf z\mathbf z^\top \mid \mathbf x] = \text{Cov} + \beta\mathbf x\mathbf x^\top\beta^\top$, whose average over the data is the $I - \beta L + \beta S\beta^\top$ of Day 1.

</Prove>

<Prove title="2. Gaussian sources cannot be unmixed" task="Let $\mathbf s \sim \mathcal N(\mathbf 0, I)$ and $\mathbf x = A\mathbf s$. Show that for any orthogonal $R$, the sources $\mathbf s' = R\mathbf s$ with mixing $A' = AR^\top$ produce exactly the same distribution of $\mathbf x$, so no method can tell $A$ from $A'$.">

First, $A'\mathbf s' = AR^\top R\mathbf s = A\mathbf s = \mathbf x$, so the mixtures are identical sample by sample.

Second, $\mathbf s' = R\mathbf s$ is Gaussian with covariance $RIR^\top = I$, so $\mathbf s'$ has exactly the same distribution as $\mathbf s$: its components are again independent standard Gaussians.

So the pairs $(A, \mathbf s)$ and $(AR^\top, R\mathbf s)$ are equally valid explanations of any dataset, and ICA cannot choose between them. For non-Gaussian sources, $R\mathbf s$ has independent components only when $R$ is a permutation with signs (the Darmois-Skitovich theorem), which is why ICA works then.

</Prove>
