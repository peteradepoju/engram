<Step n={1} title="Graphs as factorisations" minutes={25} />

A **Bayesian network** is a directed acyclic graph whose nodes are random variables, together with a conditional distribution for each node given its parents. It represents the joint distribution

$$
p(x_1, \dots, x_n) = \prod_{i=1}^np(x_i \mid \text{pa}(x_i)).
$$

By the chain rule, any joint can be written as $\prod_ip(x_i \mid x_1, \dots, x_{i-1})$; the graph's content is in the parents it leaves out, each missing edge an assertion of conditional independence. With $n$ binary variables the full joint needs $2^n - 1$ numbers, while a network whose nodes have at most $k$ parents needs at most $n2^k$. Lauritzen and Spiegelhalter (1988), today's paper, showed how to compute exact posteriors in such networks by local operations, using a small medical example, the "Asia" network, that became a standard test case.

Today's network describes a detection experiment. A faint stimulus ($S$) is present on 20% of trials; arousal ($U$) is high on 30%; attention ($A$) is cued on half. A neuron's response ($R$) depends on the stimulus, attention and arousal; the pupil ($P$) reports arousal; the animal's report ($B$) depends on the neuron.

The 64 probabilities sum to one, as they must, but they were specified by only 15 numbers: one for each of the three root nodes, eight for the neuron (one per combination of its three parents), and two each for the pupil and the report. The saving grows quickly: with 30 binary variables, each with three parents, it is 240 numbers against a billion. Every numerical saving is also a scientific claim. Drawing no arrow from attention to the pupil, for example, asserts that attention affects the pupil only through arousal, which a dataset could refute.

<Step n={2} title="Inference by enumeration" minutes={30} />

To compute a posterior such as $p(S = 1 \mid B = 1)$, sum the joint over all configurations consistent with the evidence, with and without $S = 1$, and divide. This is exact and always works, but the number of terms grows as $2^n$, which is why the rest of the week looks for better methods.

  def posterior(query, evidence):
      num = den = 0.0
      for vals in itertools.product((0, 1), repeat=len(nodes)):
          x = dict(zip(nodes, vals))
          if any(x[k] != v for k, v in evidence.items()):

A report of "seen" doubles the probability that the stimulus was present, from 0.20 to 0.40, and a spike from the neuron raises it to 0.49. The report is weaker evidence than the neuron because it is a noisy readout of the neuron: the network contains no path from stimulus to report except through it. Each posterior here summed 64 terms. With 40 binary variables it would be about $10^{12}$ terms, which is the cost that the exact algorithm of Day 4 and the sampling of Day 5 avoid.

<Step n={3} title="Explaining away" minutes={20} />

Stimulus and arousal are independent causes in this network; nothing links them except their common effect, the neuron. Once the neuron's response is known, they are no longer independent: evidence for one cause weakens the case for the other. This is **explaining away**, the signature of a collider, and Day 2 turns it into a general rule.

Given that the neuron fired, the stimulus has probability 0.49. In data analysis the same mechanism creates spurious associations: selecting trials on a neural response, then relating the stimulus to arousal within them, produces a correlation that does not exist across all trials.

<Step n={4} title="Ancestral sampling" minutes={30} />

A Bayesian network is also a simulator. Sample each node in an order where parents come before children (a topological order), drawing it from its conditional distribution given its parents' sampled values. Posteriors can then be estimated by keeping only samples that match the evidence, **rejection sampling**, which is simple and unbiased but wastes most samples when the evidence is unlikely.

  def ancestral_sample(n, g):
      out = {}
      for v in nodes:
          pa = parents[v]
          p1 = np.array([cpt[v][tuple(int(out[u][i]) for u in pa)] for i in range(n)]) if pa else np.full(n, cpt[v][()])

The sampler reproduces the exact marginals, and keeping the 3,502 of 20,000 trials in which the neuron fired and the pupil was dilated gives an estimate of 0.341 against the exact 0.339. Rejection used only 18% of the samples here; for evidence with probability one in a million it would keep almost none. Week 19's Gibbs sampler avoids this by sampling directly from conditionals given the evidence, and Day 5 uses it on a model of a whole population of neurons.
