<Step n={1} title="Three patterns" minutes={25} />

Every path between two variables in a directed graph passes through intermediate nodes of three kinds. In a **chain** $A \to B \to C$, information flows through $B$; observing $B$ blocks it. In a **fork** $A \leftarrow B \to C$, $B$ is a common cause; observing it blocks the path too. In a **collider** $A \to B \leftarrow C$, the path is blocked unless $B$, or one of its descendants, is observed, which opens it: explaining away. Pearl (1986), today's paper, set out how beliefs propagate through such networks and why these patterns govern which pieces of evidence are relevant to which.

The chain and the fork behave alike: $A$ and $C$ are correlated (0.58 and 0.49), and the correlation vanishes once $B$ is accounted for. The collider is the mirror image: $A$ and $C$ are uncorrelated, $-0.005$, but given $B$ their partial correlation is $-0.80$. Knowing that $B = A + C$ is large, a high $A$ implies a lower $C$. The partial correlation is the linear-Gaussian version of conditioning; for non-Gaussian variables, conditional independence is a stronger property than zero partial correlation.

<Step n={2} title="d-separation for any graph" minutes={35} />

Two sets of nodes $X$ and $Y$ are **d-separated** by a set $Z$ if every path between them is blocked: some chain or fork node on it is in $Z$, or some collider on it is neither in $Z$ nor has a descendant in $Z$. If they are d-separated, they are conditionally independent given $Z$ in every distribution that factorises over the graph. Checking every path is tedious, but there is an equivalent test (Lauritzen and colleagues, 1990):

1. Keep only the **ancestors** of $X \cup Y \cup Z$ (including those nodes).
2. **Moralise**: connect every pair of parents that share a child, then drop the directions.
3. Delete $Z$. $X$ and $Y$ are d-separated by $Z$ exactly when no path connects them in what remains.

Marrying the parents is how colliders are handled: two causes become linked only if their common effect, or a descendant, survives the first step, which happens only if it is observed.

Stimulus and arousal are independent with nothing observed, and become dependent given the neuron's response, or given the report, a descendant of the response, which also opens the collider. The pupil and the report are dependent, through the path pupil, arousal, response, report, and independent given the response, which blocks the chain. <Step n={3} title="Correlations between neurons" minutes={30} />

Correlations between simultaneously recorded neurons are often read as evidence of connections ("functional connectivity"). d-separation says when that reading fails. A **common input** (a fork) correlates two unconnected neurons, and conditioning on the input removes the correlation, but only if the input is recorded. **Selection** on a common target (a collider) does the opposite: two independent neurons become correlated within the selected trials.

A shared input with modest weights gives two unconnected neurons a correlation of 0.27, as large as many correlations reported between connected cells, and conditioning on the input removes it entirely. In a real recording the input is usually one of thousands of unrecorded neurons, so the conditioning cannot be done. The second case is subtler. Two neurons with no relation at all are correlated at $-0.57$ on the trials where their common target burst: if one contributed little, the other must have contributed a lot. Any analysis that selects trials on a downstream event, such as a burst, a correct response or a fast reaction, conditions on a collider.

<Step n={4} title="What graphs can and cannot tell" minutes={15} />

d-separation goes from graph to independences. Going backwards, from independences found in data to a graph, is the problem of **structure learning**, and it has a fundamental limit: different graphs can imply exactly the same independences. The chain $A \to B \to C$, the reversed chain $A \leftarrow B \leftarrow C$ and the fork $A \leftarrow B \to C$ all say only that $A$ and $C$ are independent given $B$; they form a **Markov equivalence class**, and observational data cannot tell them apart. The collider $A \to B \leftarrow C$ is different, which is why colliders, when they can be found, fix the direction of arrows. Telling the members of a class apart needs interventions or extra assumptions, the subject of Week 72.
