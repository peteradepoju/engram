<Step n={1} title="Undirected models" minutes={25} />

A **Markov random field** (MRF) is an undirected graph with a non-negative **potential** $\psi_C$ on each clique $C$ (a set of mutually connected nodes):

$$
p(\mathbf x) = \frac{1}{Z}\prod_C\psi_C(\mathbf x_C),\qquad Z = \sum_{\mathbf x}\prod_C\psi_C(\mathbf x_C).
$$

The independences are simpler than in directed graphs: two sets of nodes are independent given a third set if every path between them passes through it, with no colliders to worry about. In return, the local potentials are not probabilities, and the **partition function** $Z$ couples them all, which is what makes undirected models hard to fit. Writing $\psi_C = e^{-E_C}$ gives an **energy-based model**, $p(\mathbf x) \propto e^{-E(\mathbf x)}$. The **Ising model** for binary units $s_i \in \{-1, +1\}$ has energy

$$
E(\mathbf s) = -\sum_ih_is_i - \sum_{i \lt j}J_{ij}s_is_j,
$$

with biases $h_i$ and pairwise couplings $J_{ij}$.

<Step n={2} title="Maximum entropy" minutes={30} />

<Derivation title="The least committal distribution with given moments">
<div>

Jaynes (1957), today's paper, argued that when all we know about a distribution is the averages of some functions, $E_p[f_k(\mathbf x)] = \mu_k$, we should choose the distribution with the largest **entropy** $H(p) = -\sum_{\mathbf x}p(\mathbf x)\log p(\mathbf x)$ among those that match them: it adds no structure beyond what the constraints force.

</div>
<div>

Maximise $H(p)$ subject to $\sum_{\mathbf x}p(\mathbf x) = 1$ and the moment constraints, with Lagrange multipliers $\lambda_0$ and $\lambda_k$. Setting the derivative with respect to each $p(\mathbf x)$ to zero gives $-\log p(\mathbf x) - 1 + \lambda_0 + \sum_k\lambda_kf_k(\mathbf x) = 0$, so

$$
p(\mathbf x) = \frac{1}{Z}\exp\left(\sum_k\lambda_kf_k(\mathbf x)\right).
$$

</div>
<div>

If the constraints are the mean of each unit, $E[s_i]$, the solution is a product of independent units. If they also include all pairwise products $E[s_is_j]$, the solution is exactly the Ising model, with $h_i$ and $J_{ij}$ as the multipliers. Matching more statistics adds more terms: triplets give three-way couplings, and so on.

</div>
</Derivation>

All four alternative distributions have exactly the same three means as the independent one, and every one has lower entropy, from 1.763 to 1.798 nats against 1.804. The direction used to build them changes only the three-way structure, so the alternatives assert a dependence among the units that the means alone give no reason to believe. Maximum entropy forbids that: it adds structure only where a constraint requires it. Fitting a model with measured pairwise correlations is therefore a test, since any structure the data show beyond the Ising model's predictions is evidence of higher-order interactions.

<Step n={3} title="Exact computation in a small model" minutes={20} />

For a handful of units, $Z$ can be computed by summing over all $2^N$ states, and with it every marginal and correlation. This is how small maximum entropy models of neurons are evaluated, and it makes the effect of couplings concrete.

The coupled pair has a correlation of 0.34 and the anti-coupled pair $-0.23$, while uncoupled pairs are exactly uncorrelated, because in this model nothing connects them. Couplings also change means: neurons 0 and 1 are more often silent (mean $-0.83$) than uncoupled neurons ($-0.66$), since when both are mostly silent, agreement favours silence. Correlations and couplings are not the same thing: in larger networks, a chain of couplings correlates neurons that are not directly coupled, and inverting correlations into couplings is the hard problem Day 5 tackles.

<Step n={4} title="Order from pairwise couplings" minutes={30} />

For large $N$, $Z$ cannot be computed, but the model can still be sampled. Gibbs sampling (Week 19) needs only each unit's conditional given the others, which in an Ising model depends only on its neighbours: $p(s_i = +1 \mid \text{rest}) = \sigma\left(2(h_i + \sum_jJ_{ij}s_j)\right)$. On a square lattice with uniform coupling $J$ and no field, Onsager showed in 1944 that a **phase transition** occurs at $J_c = \frac12\log(1 + \sqrt2) \approx 0.44$: below it, agreement stays local; above it, one state spreads across the whole lattice.

At $J = 0.2$ the lattice stays disordered, with an absolute mean state of 0.03; at $J = 0.7$ it is almost uniform, 0.93. At $J = 0.44$, close to the critical point, the value of 0.23 is unstable from run to run: large patches form and dissolve, and 300 sweeps are not enough to settle. That slowing down near the critical point is real, and it is also a warning for samplers in general: Gibbs sampling mixes slowly when variables are strongly coupled, because it changes one unit at a time.

<Key title="Before you go">
<ul>
<li>Undirected models are normalised products of potentials; Z is the hard part.</li>
<li>Maximum entropy under mean constraints gives independence; adding pairwise constraints gives the Ising model.</li>
<li>Small models can be enumerated exactly; large ones can be sampled by Gibbs sampling.</li>
<li>Pairwise couplings can produce sudden collective order: a phase transition.</li>
</ul>
