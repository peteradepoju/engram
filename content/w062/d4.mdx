<Step n={1} title="Pushing sums inside products" minutes={25} />

Take a chain $x_1 - x_2 - \cdots - x_T$ with $p(\mathbf x) \propto \prod_t\phi_t(x_t)\prod_t\psi(x_t, x_{t+1})$, where $\phi_t$ holds the evidence about $x_t$ and $\psi$ the coupling between neighbours. The marginal of $x_T$ is

$$
p(x_T) \propto \phi_T(x_T)\sum_{x_{T-1}}\psi(x_{T-1}, x_T)\phi_{T-1}(x_{T-1})\sum_{x_{T-2}}\psi(x_{T-2}, x_{T-1})\cdots\sum_{x_1}\psi(x_1, x_2)\phi_1(x_1).
$$

Because each factor involves only neighbouring variables, every sum can be moved as far right as possible and done once. The innermost sum is a function of $x_2$ alone, a **message** from $x_1$ to $x_2$; it is combined with $x_2$'s evidence and passed on. Instead of $K^T$ terms, the cost is $T$ sums of $K \times K$ products. Kschischang, Frey and Loeliger (2001), today's paper, showed that this single idea, the **sum-product algorithm** on a factor graph, includes the forward-backward algorithm, the Kalman filter, Viterbi decoding, turbo decoding and Pearl's belief propagation as special cases.

<Step n={2} title="Forward and backward messages" minutes={30} />

Messages flow in both directions. The **forward** message $\alpha_t(x_t)$ summarises the evidence from $1, \dots, t$; the **backward** message $\beta_t(x_t)$ the evidence from $t + 1, \dots, T$:

$$
\alpha_t(x_t) = \phi_t(x_t)\sum_{x_{t-1}}\alpha_{t-1}(x_{t-1})\psi(x_{t-1}, x_t),\qquad \beta_t(x_t) = \sum_{x_{t+1}}\psi(x_t, x_{t+1})\phi_{t+1}(x_{t+1})\beta_{t+1}(x_{t+1}),
$$

and the marginal is $p(x_t \mid \text{all evidence}) \propto \alpha_t(x_t)\beta_t(x_t)$. Messages are normalised at each step to avoid underflow, which changes nothing but a constant.

<Step n={3} title="A hidden state through a session" minutes={25} />

Animals drift in and out of engagement during a session, and their choices on a trial reflect it: engaged, they are mostly correct; disengaged, they guess (Ashwood and colleagues, 2022, found such states in mice). Treat engagement as a hidden chain that persists from trial to trial, and each trial's outcome as evidence.

Calling a trial engaged whenever the animal was correct gets the state right on 71% of trials, because disengaged animals are still right by luck 55% of the time. The chain gets 89%: a run of correct trials is strong evidence of engagement, and a single error within it is treated as a lapse rather than a switch. The persistence probability, here 0.97 per trial, sets how much evidence it takes to believe a switch. In practice it, and the accuracies in each state, are unknown and must be learned from the data with EM, which is exactly the hidden Markov model of Week 65.

<Step n={4} title="Trees and loops" minutes={25} />

On any tree, the same rule works. The message from node $i$ to neighbour $j$ is

$$
m_{i \to j}(x_j) = \sum_{x_i}\phi_i(x_i)\psi_{ij}(x_i, x_j)\prod_{k \in N(i) \setminus j}m_{k \to i}(x_i),
$$

the evidence at $i$ times everything $i$ has heard from its other neighbours, pushed through the edge. A node's marginal is its evidence times all incoming messages. Two passes, inwards to a root and outwards again, compute every marginal exactly. On graphs with loops, messages can circulate and double-count evidence; running the same updates anyway, **loopy belief propagation**, often gives good approximations, as in turbo codes, but carries no guarantee. Exact inference on loopy graphs needs clustering nodes into a tree of cliques (the junction tree of Lauritzen and Spiegelhalter, Day 1), at a cost exponential in the size of the largest clique.

Recursive messages on the seven-node tree reproduce the enumerated marginals exactly. The recursion recomputes some messages several times, which is wasteful but harmless here; a real implementation schedules them, inwards then outwards, so that each of the $2(n - 1)$ messages is computed once. Add a single edge between two leaves and the graph has a loop: the recursion would never terminate, and the iterative loopy version would count some evidence twice.
