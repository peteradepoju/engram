The shared input makes the number of active neurons bimodal: most bins have zero or one active neuron, but there is a second bump at four to seven, the up states. The independent model sees none of it, giving 4 to 6 active neurons a probability of 1% instead of 6%. The pairwise model, which matches every rate and correlation, does much better but still has the wrong shape: it spreads the probability of large events into a smooth tail, underestimating 4 to 6 active neurons by a third and overestimating 9 or 10 by a factor of five. A single hidden cause that switches all neurons together produces interactions of every order, and no set of pairwise terms can mimic them exactly. This is what Okun and colleagues (2015) and Tkačik and colleagues (2014) found in large cortical and retinal populations.

With weak couplings, loopy belief propagation converges to beliefs within 0.0001 of the exact marginals; with strong couplings the error rises to 0.08. On a cycle, the message a node sends around the loop eventually returns to it, so its own evidence is counted again as if it came from elsewhere. When couplings are weak the echo is faint by the time it returns; when they are strong it arrives nearly undiminished and the beliefs become overconfident. For a single cycle the error is well understood (Weiss, 2000); for graphs with many short loops, loopy belief propagation may oscillate and never converge.

<Prove title="1. Maximum likelihood is moment matching" task="For $p_\theta(\mathbf x) = \exp(\theta^\top f(\mathbf x))/Z(\theta)$ with $Z(\theta) = \sum_{\mathbf x}\exp(\theta^\top f(\mathbf x))$, show that $\nabla_\theta\log Z(\theta) = E_{p_\theta}[f(\mathbf x)]$, and hence that the gradient of the average log-likelihood of data $\mathbf x_1, \dots, \mathbf x_n$ is the data average of $f$ minus its model average.">

Differentiate: $\nabla_\theta\log Z = \frac{1}{Z}\sum_{\mathbf x}f(\mathbf x)\exp(\theta^\top f(\mathbf x)) = \sum_{\mathbf x}f(\mathbf x)p_\theta(\mathbf x) = E_{p_\theta}[f]$.

The average log-likelihood is $\frac1n\sum_i\theta^\top f(\mathbf x_i) - \log Z(\theta)$, so its gradient is $\frac1n\sum_if(\mathbf x_i) - E_{p_\theta}[f]$.

Setting it to zero gives moment matching. The Hessian of $\log Z$ is the covariance of $f$ under $p_\theta$, which is positive semi-definite, so the log-likelihood is concave and any stationary point is a global maximum.

</Prove>

<Prove title="2. A collider blocks when unobserved" task="In the network $A \to B \leftarrow C$ with $p(a, b, c) = p(a)p(c)p(b \mid a, c)$, show that $A$ and $C$ are independent, and give a numerical example showing that they are dependent given $B$.">

Marginalise $B$: $p(a, c) = \sum_bp(a)p(c)p(b \mid a, c) = p(a)p(c)\sum_bp(b \mid a, c) = p(a)p(c)$, since the conditional distribution of $B$ sums to one. So $A$ and $C$ are independent.

Example: $A$ and $C$ are fair coins and $B = A \text{ or } C$. Given $B = 1$, the three configurations $(1, 0), (0, 1), (1, 1)$ are equally likely, so $p(A = 1 \mid B = 1) = 2/3$ but $p(A = 1 \mid B = 1, C = 1) = 1/2$: learning $C$ changes the belief about $A$. They are dependent given $B$, which is explaining away.

</Prove>
