<Step n={1} title="Latent variable models" minutes={20} />

A **latent variable model** has parameters $\theta$, a prior $p(\mathbf z)$ over unobserved variables and a likelihood $p_\theta(\mathbf x \mid \mathbf z)$. Learning maximises the **marginal likelihood**, or evidence,

$$
p_\theta(\mathbf x) = \int p_\theta(\mathbf x \mid \mathbf z)p(\mathbf z)\,d\mathbf z,
$$

summed over observations in logarithms. For mixtures the integral is a sum over components and is easy; for factor analysis it is a Gaussian integral and is easy. For almost anything else, a neural network decoder, Poisson observations of a continuous latent, a nonlinear dynamical system, it has no closed form. The same integral sits in the denominator of the posterior $p_\theta(\mathbf z \mid \mathbf x)$, so learning and inference are hard for the same reason.

<Video id="FMuvUZXMzKM" title="Latent variable models and variational autoencoders (CS294-158, lecture 4)" by="Pieter Abbeel, UC Berkeley" note="Pieter Abbeel on why latent variable models are useful, why their likelihood is hard to maximise, and how a variational lower bound with an inference network makes training possible." />

**Probabilistic PCA** (Tipping and Bishop, 1999, today's paper) is the factor analysis model of Week 61 with equal noise on every dimension, $\mathbf x = W\mathbf z + \boldsymbol\mu + \boldsymbol\epsilon$, $\mathbf z \sim \mathcal N(\mathbf 0, I)$, $\boldsymbol\epsilon \sim \mathcal N(\mathbf 0, \sigma^2I)$. Everything is Gaussian: the evidence is $\mathcal N(\mathbf x; \boldsymbol\mu, WW^\top + \sigma^2I)$ and the posterior is $\mathcal N(M^{-1}W^\top(\mathbf x - \boldsymbol\mu), \sigma^2M^{-1})$ with $M = W^\top W + \sigma^2I$. Tipping and Bishop showed that the maximum-likelihood $W$ spans the principal subspace, giving PCA a probability model.

<Step n={2} title="The bound, two ways" minutes={30} />

<Derivation title="Two decompositions of the ELBO">
<div>

For any distribution $q(\mathbf z)$, define the evidence lower bound

$$
\mathcal L(q, \theta) = E_q\left[\log p_\theta(\mathbf x, \mathbf z) - \log q(\mathbf z)\right].
$$

</div>
<div>

**Gap form.** Write $p_\theta(\mathbf x, \mathbf z) = p_\theta(\mathbf z \mid \mathbf x)p_\theta(\mathbf x)$. Then $\mathcal L = \log p_\theta(\mathbf x) - \text{KL}(q(\mathbf z)\,\Vert\,p_\theta(\mathbf z \mid \mathbf x))$. Since KL is non-negative, the ELBO is a lower bound, equal to the log evidence exactly when $q$ is the true posterior.

</div>
<div>

**Reconstruction form.** Write $p_\theta(\mathbf x, \mathbf z) = p_\theta(\mathbf x \mid \mathbf z)p(\mathbf z)$. Then $\mathcal L = E_q[\log p_\theta(\mathbf x \mid \mathbf z)] - \text{KL}(q(\mathbf z)\,\Vert\,p(\mathbf z))$: how well latents drawn from $q$ reconstruct the data, minus how far $q$ strays from the prior. This form contains only quantities that can be computed or sampled.

</div>
<div>

The two forms together explain variational learning. Raising $\mathcal L$ over $q$ shrinks the gap, which is inference; raising it over $\theta$ raises a lower bound on the evidence, which is learning.

</div>
</Derivation>

  import numpy as np
  from scipy.stats import multivariate_normal as mvn
  g0 = np.random.default_rng(631)
  d, k = 12, 2
  W = g0.normal(size=(d, k)); mu = g0.normal(size=d); s2 = 0.5
  Z = g0.normal(size=(500, k))
  X = Z @ W.T + mu + np.sqrt(s2) * g0.normal(size=(500, d))

  def log_evidence(x):
      return mvn.logpdf(x, mu, W @ W.T + s2 * np.eye(d))

      return np.linalg.solve(M, W.T @ (x - mu)), s2 * np.linalg.inv(M)
  print("ready: a 12-dimensional probabilistic PCA model with 2 latents, and 500 observations")

With $q$ set to the exact posterior the ELBO equals the log evidence, $-16.8695$, to every printed digit. Shift the mean by 0.5 in each latent dimension and double the covariance, and the ELBO drops by more than 9 nats: the latent dimensions are well determined here, because twelve observed dimensions constrain two latents, so the posterior is narrow and a modest error in $q$ is a large KL divergence. The test checks the gap identity exactly: log evidence minus ELBO equals KL from $q$ to the posterior.

Sampling 20,000 latents from $q$ gives an ELBO of $-26.230$ against the exact $-26.219$, well within the standard error of 0.043. This estimator needs only the ability to sample $q$ and to evaluate $\log p(\mathbf x \mid \mathbf z)$, $\log p(\mathbf z)$ and $\log q(\mathbf z)$, which is why it works for any model. Its variance is the practical problem: a few samples per observation, as in stochastic training, give noisy estimates, and Day 4 is about computing their gradients well.

<Step n={4} title="Encoders" minutes={25} />

Every observation has its own posterior, so a dataset of a million trials has a million $q$s to optimise. **Amortised inference** learns instead a single function, an **encoder** or recognition model, that maps each $\mathbf x$ to the parameters of its $q$. In probabilistic PCA the exact posterior mean is a linear function of $\mathbf x$, $M^{-1}W^\top(\mathbf x - \boldsymbol\mu)$, so a linear encoder can be exact. The variational autoencoder of Day 4 uses a neural network encoder for models where no such formula exists.

Regressing sampled latents on observations recovers the exact posterior-mean matrix to within 0.003. Regression estimates $E[\mathbf z \mid \mathbf x]$, which is the posterior mean, so an encoder can be learned from simulations of the generative model alone. This is also how amortised inference trades accuracy for speed: a single encoder must work for every observation, and if its form is too restricted, its $q$ for some observations will be worse than a separately optimised one, an extra loss known as the amortisation gap.

<Key title="Before you go">
<ul>
<li>Latent variable models are fitted by maximising the marginal likelihood, usually intractable.</li>
<li>The ELBO is a lower bound whose gap is the KL divergence from q to the posterior.</li>
<li>In reconstruction form, the ELBO can always be estimated by sampling from q.</li>
<li>Encoders amortise inference across observations.</li>
</ul>
