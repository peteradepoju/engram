<Step n={1} title="EM in one picture" minutes={25} />

For a model with latent variables, write the ELBO for all observations as $\mathcal L(q, \theta) = \sum_i\left(\log p_\theta(\mathbf x_i) - \text{KL}(q_i\,\Vert\,p_\theta(\mathbf z_i \mid \mathbf x_i))\right)$. EM alternates:

- **E step:** maximise $\mathcal L$ over $q$ with $\theta$ fixed. The KL terms are minimised, to zero, by $q_i = p_{\theta}(\mathbf z_i \mid \mathbf x_i)$. The bound now touches the log-likelihood at the current $\theta$.
- **M step:** maximise $\mathcal L$ over $\theta$ with $q$ fixed. In the reconstruction form this is maximising $\sum_iE_{q_i}[\log p_\theta(\mathbf x_i, \mathbf z_i)]$, the expected complete-data log-likelihood, which for mixtures gives the weighted means of Week 60.

After the M step, $\log p_{\theta_{\text{new}}}(\mathbf x) \ge \mathcal L(q, \theta_{\text{new}}) \ge \mathcal L(q, \theta_{\text{old}}) = \log p_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\mathbf x)$. That chain of inequalities is the whole proof that EM never decreases the likelihood, and Neal and Hinton (1998), today's paper, pointed out that it needs neither step to be complete: any increase in $\mathcal L$ will do.

<Step n={3} title="Incremental EM" minutes={25} />

The E step for one observation changes only that observation's $q_i$. Neal and Hinton's **incremental EM** updates the responsibilities of one block of data, adjusts the sufficient statistics (weighted counts, sums and sums of squares) by the change, and immediately takes an M step. Each block step increases $\mathcal L$, so the algorithm still converges, and because parameters improve after every block rather than every full pass, it often needs fewer passes through the data. This is the ancestor of the stochastic variational methods of Day 4.

After one pass, incremental EM is 5 nats ahead of standard EM, and after three passes 7 nats, because each block's improved responsibilities feed into the parameters straight away and the next block is processed with better parameters. Both reach the same maximum after 20 passes. The gain matters most when the dataset is large and each pass is expensive, which is the setting of stochastic variational inference (Hoffman and colleagues, 2013), where the "block" is a random minibatch.

<Step n={4} title="Generalised EM" minutes={25} />

When the M step has no closed form, as for a Poisson GLM with a latent input or a mixture of experts, it can be replaced by any step that increases the expected complete-data log-likelihood, such as one or a few gradient steps. This **generalised EM** keeps the guarantee that the likelihood never falls, as long as each step really improves $\mathcal L$.

With a step one tenth of the exact M step for the means, generalised EM climbs more slowly, $-857$ after 10 iterations against $-843$ for full EM after two, but it never steps down, and after 200 iterations it reaches the same maximum. Any improvement of the bound is enough for monotonicity; a full maximisation only makes each iteration count for more. In models whose M step needs an inner optimisation, a few gradient steps per iteration are usually the better use of computation.
