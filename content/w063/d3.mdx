<Step n={1} title="The mean-field update" minutes={25} />

<Derivation title="Optimal factors under independence">
<div>

Restrict $q(\mathbf z) = \prod_jq_j(z_j)$. Fix all factors except $q_j$, and write the ELBO as a function of $q_j$ alone:

$$
\mathcal L = \int q_j(z_j)E_{-j}[\log p(\mathbf x, \mathbf z)]\,dz_j - \int q_j\log q_j\,dz_j + \text{const},
$$

where $E_{-j}$ is the expectation over all the other factors.

</div>
<div>

This is $-\text{KL}(q_j\,\Vert\,\tilde p_j) + \text{const}$, with $\tilde p_j(z_j) \propto \exp(E_{-j}[\log p(\mathbf x, \mathbf z)])$. It is maximised by setting

$$
\log q_j^*(z_j) = E_{-j}\left[\log p(\mathbf x, \mathbf z)\right] + \text{const}.
$$

</div>
<div>

Each factor is updated in turn; each update cannot decrease the ELBO, so **coordinate-ascent variational inference** (CAVI) converges to a local optimum. When the model is built from conjugate exponential-family pieces, each $q_j^*$ has the same form as the prior for $z_j$, and the updates are closed-form. Jordan, Ghahramani, Jaakkola and Saul (1999), today's paper, set out this approach for graphical models.

</div>
</Derivation>

<Step n={2} title="A neuron's mean and noise" minutes={30} />

A neuron's response to a stimulus is measured on $n$ trials, $x_i \sim \mathcal N(\mu, \tau^{-1})$, with unknown mean $\mu$ and noise precision $\tau$. With priors $\mu \mid \tau \sim \mathcal N(\mu_0, (\lambda_0\tau)^{-1})$ and $\tau \sim \text{Gamma}(a_0, b_0)$ the exact posterior is known (normal-gamma), which makes this a good test case. The mean-field approximation $q(\mu)q(\tau)$ gives, from the general rule,

$$
q(\mu) = \mathcal N\left(\frac{\lambda_0\mu_0 + n\bar x}{\lambda_0 + n}, \frac{1}{(\lambda_0 + n)E[\tau]}\right),\qquad q(\tau) = \text{Gamma}\left(a_0 + \frac{n + 1}{2},\ b_0 + \frac12E_\mu\left[\textstyle\sum_i(x_i - \mu)^2 + \lambda_0(\mu - \mu_0)^2\right]\right).
$$

Each update needs only moments of the other factor.

The variational posterior mean of $\mu$ is exact, 10.131 spikes per second, and the mean of the precision matches the exact 0.169 to four decimals, a noise standard deviation of about 2.4. The variance of $\mu$ is where the approximation shows: 0.586 against the exact 0.703, a sixth too small. In the exact posterior, $\mu$ and $\tau$ are dependent (if the noise is large, the mean is less certain), and the exact marginal of $\mu$ is a Student t with heavier tails; the factorised $q$ cannot represent either, so it uses the average precision and reports a Gaussian that is too narrow.

<Step n={3} title="Mean-field theory of the Ising model" minutes={25} />

For an Ising model, $\log p(\mathbf s) = \sum_ih_is_i + \sum_{i \lt j}J_{ij}s_is_j - \log Z$. Under $q = \prod_iq_i(s_i)$ with means $m_i = E_q[s_i]$, the expected log joint as a function of $s_i$ is $s_i(h_i + \sum_jJ_{ij}m_j) + \text{const}$, so the optimal $q_i$ gives

$$
m_i = \tanh\left(h_i + \sum_jJ_{ij}m_j\right),
$$

the **naive mean-field equations** of statistical physics, here derived as variational inference. Each unit feels its neighbours only through their average.

With weak couplings, mean field is within about 0.03 of the exact means for all eight units. With couplings four times stronger, it errs by up to 0.44: a unit whose exact mean is 0.43 is given 0.77, and another with $-0.52$ gets $-0.96$. Mean field pushes units towards the state their neighbours favour on average, ignoring that the neighbours themselves fluctuate, so with strong couplings it commits too firmly. Corrections exist: the TAP equations add a reaction term (Thouless, Anderson and Palmer, 1977), and belief propagation (Week 62) keeps pairwise information that mean field throws away.

<Step n={4} title="Overconfidence" minutes={25} />

For a Gaussian target $\mathcal N(\boldsymbol\mu, \Lambda^{-1})$, the mean-field factors can be found exactly: $q_j$ is Gaussian with variance $1/\Lambda_{jj}$, one over the diagonal of the precision matrix. The true marginal variance is $(\Lambda^{-1})_{jj}$, which is never smaller and is much larger when variables are correlated. Mean field matches the conditional variance, how uncertain $z_j$ is if the others were known, not the marginal one. Because it minimises $\text{KL}(q\Vert p)$, which heavily punishes $q$ for putting mass where $p$ has little, it fits inside the posterior rather than around it.

With no correlation, mean field is exact. With correlation 0.5 its variance is 0.75 of the truth, and with correlation 0.9 only 0.19, exactly $1 - \rho^2$, the variance of one variable given the other. The means are right in every case. So a mean-field posterior can locate the answer correctly while claiming five times too much certainty about it, and the error grows precisely when variables are entangled, which in a decoder is when neurons carry redundant information.
