<Step n={1} title="Two gradient estimators" minutes={25} />

We need $\nabla_\phi E_{q_\phi(\mathbf z)}[f(\mathbf z)]$, where $f$ is the integrand of the ELBO. The **score-function** (or REINFORCE) estimator uses $\nabla_\phi q = q\nabla_\phi\log q$:

$$
\nabla_\phi E_q[f] = E_q\left[f(\mathbf z)\nabla_\phi\log q_\phi(\mathbf z)\right],
$$

which works for any $q$ and any $f$, even non-differentiable ones, but multiplies by the value of $f$ and is often very noisy. The **reparameterisation** estimator writes $\mathbf z = g_\phi(\boldsymbol\epsilon)$ with $\boldsymbol\epsilon$ from a fixed distribution, for a Gaussian $\mathbf z = \mathbf m + \mathbf s \odot \boldsymbol\epsilon$, so that

$$
\nabla_\phi E_q[f] = E_{\boldsymbol\epsilon}\left[\nabla_\phi f(g_\phi(\boldsymbol\epsilon))\right],
$$

using the slope of $f$. Kingma and Welling (2014) and, independently, Rezende, Mohamed and Wierstra (2014), today's paper, built variational learning of deep generative models on this estimator. Week 19 used it once; today it does the heavy lifting.

Both estimators average to the true gradient of $-2$, as they must, but a single score-function estimate has a standard deviation of 5.5, against exactly 2.0 for the reparameterised one: more than seven times the variance, so seven times as many samples for the same precision. The gap widens as the function's values move further from zero, since the score function multiplies by them, and as the dimension grows, since every latent's noise contributes to every component of the estimate. That is why, for continuous latents, reparameterised gradients are the default.

<Step n={2} title="A Bayesian decoder by stochastic VI" minutes={30} />

A logistic-regression decoder predicts a choice from 6 neurons. With a Gaussian prior on the weights the posterior has no closed form. Approximate it by $q(\mathbf w) = \mathcal N(\mathbf m, \text{diag}(\mathbf s^2))$ and climb the ELBO, $E_q[\log p(\mathbf y \mid X, \mathbf w) + \log p(\mathbf w)] + \sum_j\log s_j + \text{const}$, with reparameterised gradients: draw $\mathbf w = \mathbf m + \mathbf s \odot \boldsymbol\epsilon$, compute $\mathbf g = \nabla_{\mathbf w}\log p(\mathbf y, \mathbf w)$, and step $\mathbf m$ along $\mathbf g$ and $\log\mathbf s$ along $\mathbf g \odot \mathbf s \odot \boldsymbol\epsilon + 1$.

After 3,000 noisy steps the variational mean is within 0.08 of the MAP weights, and the posterior standard deviations are about 0.16 to 0.18, a little narrower than the Laplace approximation's 0.16 to 0.20, as mean field (Day 3) predicts: it ignores the correlations between weights that the full Laplace covariance keeps. Nothing about the model was conjugate; all the algorithm needed was the gradient of the log joint, which is why stochastic VI applies to almost any differentiable model.

<Step n={3} title="Training an encoder" minutes={35} />

Now amortise. In the probabilistic PCA model of Day 1, with the decoder $W$ known, train an encoder that maps each observation to $q(\mathbf z \mid \mathbf x) = \mathcal N(A\mathbf x, \text{diag}(\mathbf s^2))$, a linear mean and a shared diagonal variance, by stochastic gradient ascent on the average ELBO over minibatches. Day 1 showed that the exact posterior mean is $M^{-1}W^\top\mathbf x$ (data centred), and its variances, for a diagonal $q$, should be $\sigma^2/M_{jj}$ (Day 3).

The learned encoder matrix is within 0.017 of the exact posterior-mean matrix, and the learned standard deviations, 0.241 and 0.177, match the mean-field optimum, 0.242 and 0.178, to three decimals. Every quantity came from minibatches of 64 observations and one reparameterised sample each, with no posterior ever computed. The learning rate had to be small, because the reconstruction gradient is divided by the noise variance and is large; with a rate ten times larger the updates overshoot and diverge, which is why practical VAEs use adaptive optimisers such as Adam (Week 13).

<Step n={4} title="From here to the VAE" minutes={10} />

A **variational autoencoder** takes three steps beyond today's encoder. The encoder becomes a neural network whose outputs are the mean and log-variance of $q(\mathbf z \mid \mathbf x)$; the decoder becomes a neural network whose output parameterises $p_\theta(\mathbf x \mid \mathbf z)$, Gaussian, Bernoulli or Poisson; and the decoder's parameters are trained on the same objective at the same time, so inference and learning happen in one stochastic gradient loop. Automatic differentiation computes what you derived by hand today. Week 16 introduced the reparameterisation trick for exactly this use; Day 5 applies the idea to spike trains.
