<Step n={1} title="Poisson observations of a latent state" minutes={20} />

A natural model of spike counts $x_{tn}$ (bin $t$, neuron $n$) is

$$
\mathbf z_t \sim p(\mathbf z_t \mid \mathbf z_{t-1}),\qquad x_{tn} \sim \text{Poisson}\left(\exp(\mathbf c_n^\top\mathbf z_t + d_n)\right),
$$

a few latent variables $\mathbf z_t$, a loading vector $\mathbf c_n$ and baseline $d_n$ for each neuron, and an exponential link that keeps rates positive, the Poisson GLM of Week 35 with a latent input. Factor analysis is the special case with Gaussian noise and no time structure. Smith and Brown (2003) and Yu and colleagues (2009) developed versions with linear and Gaussian-process dynamics; Pandarinath and colleagues (2018), today's paper, replaced the dynamics with a recurrent neural network and inference with an encoder, training everything as a variational autoencoder.

  import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

<Step n={2} title="A variational posterior for each bin" minutes={35} />

With a Gaussian prior $z_t \sim \mathcal N(0, 1)$ in each bin and a Gaussian $q(z_t) = \mathcal N(m_t, v_t)$, the expected Poisson log-likelihood has a closed form, because $E_q[e^{c z}] = e^{cm + c^2v/2}$:

$$
\mathcal L_t = \sum_n\left[x_{tn}(c_nm_t + d_n) - e^{c_nm_t + d_n + c_n^2v_t/2}\right] - \frac12\left(m_t^2 + v_t - \log v_t - 1\right) + \text{const}.
$$

Its derivatives are simple, so each $m_t$ and $v_t$ can be found by Newton's method. Compare the result with two Gaussian shortcuts: projecting square-root-transformed counts on the loadings, and PCA.

The Poisson variational posterior correlates with the true latent at 0.927, a little better than projecting square-root counts on the known loadings (0.921) or taking the first principal component (0.918). With forty neurons and known loadings, the Gaussian shortcuts are nearly as good; the Poisson model's advantage grows when rates are lower, loadings must be learned, or neurons differ greatly in rate. Its posterior variances are informative in a way the shortcuts are not: they range from 0.18 to 0.45 in standard deviation, smallest where the latent drives many neurons to fire and largest where most bins are empty.

<Step n={3} title="Smoothness in time" minutes={30} />

Latent states in the brain change smoothly: neighbouring bins share most of their state. Replace the independent prior by a first-order autoregressive one, $z_t = a z_{t-1} + \sqrt{1 - a^2}\,\eta_t$, whose prior precision matrix $Q$ is tridiagonal. The MAP latent trajectory now solves one Newton problem over all bins at once, with a Hessian that is tridiagonal plus diagonal, so each step costs time linear in $T$. The Gaussian-process factor analysis of Yu and colleagues (2009) uses a smoother Gaussian-process prior in the same way (Week 64).

The autoregressive prior raises the correlation from 0.927 to 0.982, cutting the missed variance from 14% to under 4%. Each bin now borrows evidence from its neighbours, roughly thirty bins wide for $a = 0.97$, and the posterior band shows the remaining uncertainty. The prior's timescale is a choice: set $a$ too close to 1 and fast changes in the latent are smoothed away; set it too low and the benefit disappears. In GPFA and LFADS such timescales are learned from the data by maximising the ELBO or marginal likelihood, which Week 64 does for Gaussian processes.

<Step n={4} title="Sequential autoencoders" minutes={20} />

**LFADS** (latent factor analysis via dynamical systems; Pandarinath and colleagues, 2018) makes three changes to today's model. The latent dynamics become a recurrent neural network, the generator, which can produce rotations, attractors and other nonlinear trajectories (Week 22). Each trial's initial condition, and optionally its inputs, are inferred by an encoder network that reads the whole trial's spikes. The whole system, encoder, generator and Poisson readout, is trained on the ELBO with reparameterised gradients, exactly the recipe of Day 4. On motor cortex recordings LFADS inferred single-trial trajectories that predicted reach kinematics better than smoothing or GPFA, and found perturbations such as unexpected inputs during a trial. The cost is the usual one for flexible models: it needs many trials, careful regularisation and validation on held-out neurons, the "co-smoothing" test of Week 73.

<Key title="Before you go">
<ul>
<li>Spike counts are best modelled as Poisson observations of a latent state, not Gaussian ones.</li>
<li>With Gaussian q, the Poisson ELBO has a closed form and each bin's posterior can be found by Newton's method.</li>
<li>Priors that make latents smooth in time share evidence across bins and sharpen single-trial estimates.</li>
<li>LFADS scales these ideas up with recurrent generators and encoders trained as a VAE.</li>
