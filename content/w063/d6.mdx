Both were learned together from nothing but spike counts: the E step uses the current loadings to infer the latent, and the M step uses the inferred latent, with its uncertainty, to refit each neuron as a Poisson GLM. The $c_n^2v_t/2$ terms are where the uncertainty enters: they stop the M step from treating the inferred latent as if it were observed exactly.

**Part B: a tighter bound.** The ELBO is $E_q[\log w]$ with importance weight $w = p(\mathbf x, \mathbf z)/q(\mathbf z)$. Burda, Grosse and Salakhutdinov (2016) proposed the **importance-weighted bound** $\mathcal L_K = E\left[\log\frac1K\sum_{k=1}^Kw_k\right]$ with $K$ samples from $q$. It equals the ELBO at $K = 1$, never decreases as $K$ grows, and converges to the log evidence. Check this on Day 1's probabilistic PCA, where the log evidence is known.

With one sample per estimate the bound is the ELBO, 3.5 nats below the log evidence of $-22.29$, because $q$ was deliberately poor. Ten samples close most of that gap, to 0.4 nats; a hundred to 0.03; a thousand to 0.003. Importance weighting corrects a poor $q$ by weighting its samples, so the bound becomes tight even when $q$ is not. Used as a training objective (IWAE), it gives better generative models, but its gradient signal for the encoder weakens as $K$ grows, since a good bound no longer depends much on how good $q$ is (Rainforth and colleagues, 2018).

<Prove title="1. The ELBO gap" task="For any $q(\mathbf z)$ with the same support as the posterior, show that $\log p(\mathbf x) = \mathcal L(q) + \text{KL}(q(\mathbf z)\,\Vert\,p(\mathbf z \mid \mathbf x))$, where $\mathcal L(q) = E_q[\log p(\mathbf x, \mathbf z) - \log q(\mathbf z)]$.">

Since $\log p(\mathbf x)$ does not depend on $\mathbf z$, $\log p(\mathbf x) = E_q[\log p(\mathbf x)] = E_q[\log p(\mathbf x, \mathbf z) - \log p(\mathbf z \mid \mathbf x)]$.

Add and subtract $\log q(\mathbf z)$ inside the expectation: $E_q[\log p(\mathbf x, \mathbf z) - \log q(\mathbf z)] + E_q[\log q(\mathbf z) - \log p(\mathbf z \mid \mathbf x)]$.

The first term is $\mathcal L(q)$ and the second is $\text{KL}(q\,\Vert\,p(\mathbf z \mid \mathbf x)) \ge 0$. So the ELBO is a lower bound, tight exactly when $q$ equals the posterior almost everywhere.

</Prove>

<Prove title="2. The Gaussian expectation of an exponential" task="Show that if $z \sim \mathcal N(m, v)$ then $E[e^{cz}] = \exp(cm + c^2v/2)$.">

Write $E[e^{cz}] = \int\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi v}}\exp\left(cz - \frac{(z - m)^2}{2v}\right)dz$.

Complete the square in the exponent: $cz - \frac{(z - m)^2}{2v} = -\frac{(z - m - cv)^2}{2v} + cm + \frac{c^2v}{2}$.

The remaining integral is that of a Gaussian density with mean $m + cv$, which is 1, leaving $\exp(cm + c^2v/2)$. This is the moment-generating function of the Gaussian, and it is what makes the Poisson-Gaussian ELBO of Day 5 and Part A available in closed form.

</Prove>
