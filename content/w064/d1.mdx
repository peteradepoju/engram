<Step n={1} title="Bayesian linear regression for a tuning curve" minutes={30} />

Model a neuron's response to orientation $\theta$ as $f(\theta) = \boldsymbol\phi(\theta)^\top\mathbf w$ with features $\boldsymbol\phi$, a Gaussian prior $\mathbf w \sim \mathcal N(\mathbf 0, \alpha I)$, and noisy observations $y_i = f(\theta_i) + \epsilon_i$, $\epsilon_i \sim \mathcal N(0, \sigma^2)$. With $\Phi$ the $n \times M$ matrix of features at the data, the posterior over weights is Gaussian, the full distribution around the MAP estimate of Week 17:

$$
\Sigma_w = \left(\frac{\Phi^\top\Phi}{\sigma^2} + \frac{I}{\alpha}\right)^{-1},\qquad \mathbf m_w = \frac{\Sigma_w\Phi^\top\mathbf y}{\sigma^2},
$$

and the prediction at a new input has mean $\boldsymbol\phi_*^\top\mathbf m_w$ and variance $\boldsymbol\phi_*^\top\Sigma_w\boldsymbol\phi_*$ (plus $\sigma^2$ for a new noisy observation). The posterior mean is ridge regression with penalty $\sigma^2/\alpha$ (Week 55).

The posterior mean follows the tuning curve where orientations were tested and relaxes to zero, the prior mean, beyond 1.8 radians, where nothing was measured. The predictive standard deviation says so: 1.4 at 1 radian, where several trials constrain the curve, and 3.2 at $\pi$, almost the prior's own spread. A ridge fit would give the same mean with no warning that the right half of the curve is a guess.

<Step n={2} title="The function-space view" minutes={30} />

<Derivation title="Predictions need only inner products">
<div>

Under the prior, the function values at any set of inputs are jointly Gaussian with mean zero and covariance $\alpha\Phi\Phi^\top$. Define the **kernel** $k(\theta, \theta') = \alpha\boldsymbol\phi(\theta)^\top\boldsymbol\phi(\theta')$ and the $n \times n$ matrix $K = \alpha\Phi\Phi^\top$.

</div>
<div>

The observations and a new function value are jointly Gaussian:

$$
\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf y\\ f_*\end{pmatrix} \sim \mathcal N\left(\mathbf 0, \begin{pmatrix}K + \sigma^2I & \mathbf k_*\\ \mathbf k_*^\top & k_{**}\end{pmatrix}\right),
$$

with $\mathbf k_*$ the kernel between the data and the new input.

</div>
<div>

Conditioning a Gaussian (Week 16) gives

$$
E[f_* \mid \mathbf y] = \mathbf k_*^\top(K + \sigma^2I)^{-1}\mathbf y,\qquad \text{Var}[f_* \mid \mathbf y] = k_{**} - \mathbf k_*^\top(K + \sigma^2I)^{-1}\mathbf k_*.
$$

Only an $n \times n$ matrix is inverted, however many features there are. The same identity gave kernel ridge regression in Week 57; here it also gives the uncertainty.

</div>
</Derivation>

The function-space predictions agree with the weight-space ones to $10^{-13}$, rounding error, and needed a 12-by-12 inverse instead of 30-by-30. With 1,000 or a million features the weight-space computation becomes impossible, while the function-space one stays 12-by-12, provided the kernel can be computed without listing the features. That is exactly what the next step arranges.

<Step n={3} title="Infinitely many features" minutes={25} />

Now let the number of bumps $M$ grow, spaced ever more densely, with the prior variance of each weight shrinking in proportion to keep the total variance fixed. The kernel becomes an integral over bump positions, and for Gaussian bumps the integral is again a Gaussian in the distance between inputs: the **squared exponential kernel** $k(\theta, \theta') = s^2\exp(-(\theta - \theta')^2/2\ell^2)$. Neal (1996), today's reference, showed the same for neural networks: as a one-hidden-layer network with random weights gets wider, the distribution of functions it computes converges to a Gaussian process, a result that returned in 2018 as the neural network Gaussian process and neural tangent kernel (Week 67).

With 5 bumps spread over 12 radians, the kernel is nothing like the limit, because the bumps are further apart than their width and the functions are lumpy. With 20 it is within 40%, and with 100 or more the bump kernel equals the squared exponential to four decimals. The length-scale of the limit, $\sqrt2$ times the bump width, comes from convolving two Gaussians. From here on the bumps are forgotten: a squared exponential kernel is the prior of a regression on infinitely many of them.

<Step n={4} title="What a Gaussian process is" minutes={20} />

A **Gaussian process** is a collection of random variables, one for every input, any finite number of which have a joint Gaussian distribution. It is specified by a mean function $m(x)$ and a covariance function, the kernel, $k(x, x')$, written $f \sim \mathcal{GP}(m, k)$. Nothing else is needed: to make predictions at any finite set of inputs, write down the Gaussian over those inputs and the data, and condition. The kernel must produce positive semi-definite matrices for every set of inputs, the same condition as Mercer's in Week 57. The rest of the week asks what kernels mean (Day 2), how to compute with them (Day 3), how to choose their parameters (Day 4) and what they do for neural data (Day 5).

<Key title="Before you go">
<ul>
<li>Bayesian linear regression gives Gaussian posteriors over weights and predictions.</li>
<li>Its predictions depend on the features only through the kernel, so an n × n matrix suffices.</li>
<li>Infinitely many features with shrinking weights define a fixed kernel.</li>
<li>A Gaussian process is a distribution over functions, defined by a mean and a kernel.</li>
