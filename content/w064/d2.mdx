<Step n={1} title="Sampling from a prior over functions" minutes={25} />

To see what a kernel believes, draw functions from it. Choose a grid of inputs, form the covariance matrix $K_{ij} = k(x_i, x_j)$, factor it as $K = LL^\top$ (Cholesky, adding a tiny "jitter" to the diagonal for numerical stability), and set $\mathbf f = L\boldsymbol\epsilon$ with $\boldsymbol\epsilon \sim \mathcal N(\mathbf 0, I)$. Each draw is a whole function on the grid.

Every kernel matrix is positive semi-definite, as a covariance must be. The samples differ in character, not just in scale: Matérn 1/2 draws are jagged, with a mean squared step between neighbouring grid points 60 times that of the squared exponential's smooth curves; periodic draws repeat exactly every 2 units; linear draws are straight lines through a common region. None of these properties was fitted to anything. They are what each kernel asserts before seeing data, and a GP posterior can only ever be a reweighting of functions like these.

<Step n={2} title="A gallery of kernels" minutes={25} />

The **squared exponential** $k = s^2e^{-r^2/2\ell^2}$, with $r = |x - x'|$, gives infinitely differentiable functions; its length-scale $\ell$ sets how far apart inputs must be before their values become nearly independent, and $s^2$ sets the vertical scale. The **Matérn** family $k_\nu$ controls smoothness directly: $\nu = 1/2$ gives $e^{-r/\ell}$, the covariance of the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process of Week 24, continuous but nowhere differentiable; $\nu = 3/2$ and $5/2$ give once and twice differentiable functions, often more realistic than the squared exponential for physical signals. The **periodic** kernel repeats with a fixed period. The **linear** kernel gives straight lines, and is Bayesian linear regression with one feature. Duvenaud and colleagues (2013), today's paper, treated this gallery as a grammar.

<Step n={3} title="Adding and multiplying kernels" minutes={25} />

Sums and products of kernels are kernels. Adding two independent Gaussian processes gives a process whose kernel is the sum, so **sums model superpositions**: a slow drift plus an oscillation. Multiplying kernels gives a process that has both properties at once, so **products model interactions**: a periodic kernel times a squared exponential is a rhythm whose shape slowly changes, and a linear kernel times anything makes the amplitude grow with the input. Duvenaud and colleagues searched over such compositions automatically, choosing among them by marginal likelihood (Day 4), and recovered interpretable structure from time series.

Both combinations are valid kernels. Under the pure periodic kernel, points three periods apart are perfectly correlated, 1.000: the rhythm can never change. Multiplying by a squared exponential of length-scale 4 keeps the correlation one period apart high, 0.88, but lets it fall to 0.33 three periods apart, so the waveform can slowly drift in shape, the right prior for an oscillation in a recording whose shape changes over a session. The samples show the difference: drift plus rhythm wobbles around a slowly wandering baseline; the locally periodic kernel keeps repeating but forgets its exact shape.

<Step n={4} title="A kernel for orientation" minutes={30} />

Orientation is circular: 0 and 180 degrees are the same stimulus. A squared exponential on the raw angle treats them as far apart, so a tuning curve fitted with it can disagree with itself at the ends. A periodic kernel with period $\pi$ (in radians) builds the circle into the prior, and every function it allows wraps round.
