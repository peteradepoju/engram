<Step n={1} title="The algorithm" minutes={30} />

Inverting $K + \sigma^2I$ directly is slow and numerically fragile. The standard algorithm (Rasmussen and Williams, 2006, algorithm 2.1; Rasmussen, 2004, today's paper) uses the Cholesky factor $L$ with $LL^\top = K + \sigma^2I$:

$$
\boldsymbol\alpha = L^{-\top}L^{-1}\mathbf y,\quad \bar f_* = \mathbf k_*^\top\boldsymbol\alpha,\quad \mathbf v = L^{-1}\mathbf k_*,\quad \text{Var}[f_*] = k_{**} - \mathbf v^\top\mathbf v,
$$

$$
\log p(\mathbf y) = -\tfrac12\mathbf y^\top\boldsymbol\alpha - \textstyle\sum_i\log L_{ii} - \tfrac n2\log 2\pi.
$$

Triangular solves cost $O(n^2)$ each, and the factorisation $O(n^3)$ once. $\text{Var}[f_*]$ is the uncertainty about the underlying function; a new observation $y_* = f_* + \epsilon$ has variance $\text{Var}[f_*] + \sigma^2$.

The Cholesky computations agree with the explicit-inverse formulas and with scipy's Gaussian log-density, and the plot shows the two kinds of interval. The inner band, for the latent function, is narrow where the 25 points are dense and opens up beyond $\pm3$, where there are none. The outer band adds the observation noise, a constant $\sigma^2 = 0.04$ everywhere, and is the one to use when predicting a new measurement. Confusing the two is a common error: reporting the latent band as the range of future data understates how variable the data will be.

<Step n={2} title="Are the error bars right?" minutes={25} />

A GP's intervals are only as good as its assumptions. A simple check is **coverage**: the fraction of held-out observations that fall inside the 95% predictive interval for a new observation, mean ± 1.96 times $\sqrt{\text{Var}[f_*] + \sigma^2}$. Too few means overconfidence, too many means caution, and either says a hyperparameter or the kernel is wrong.

With the noise variance set correctly, 96.3% of 2,000 held-out observations fall inside the nominal 95% intervals, close to right (the slight excess is because the GP's length-scale was a guess, not fitted). With the noise variance ten times too small, coverage collapses to 58%: the model believes the data lie almost exactly on the latent function, and both its function intervals and its noise term are too narrow. Day 4 removes the guesswork by learning the noise and the length-scale from the data.

<Step n={3} title="A place field in two dimensions" minutes={35} />

A rat explores a square arena while a hippocampal place cell fires when it is near one location. The firing map is a function of two variables, position $x$ and $y$; a GP with a squared exponential kernel in two dimensions, $k = s^2\exp(-\lVert\mathbf p - \mathbf p'\rVert^2/2\ell^2)$, estimates it from activity recorded along the path, and its variance shows where the rat never went. Here the activity in each 200 ms bin is a square-root-transformed spike count, which makes Poisson noise roughly Gaussian with constant variance.

<Step n={4} title="Costs and shortcuts" minutes={10} />

Exact GP regression costs $O(n^3)$ time and $O(n^2)$ memory, which is comfortable for a few thousand points and impossible for a million. The main shortcuts approximate the kernel matrix by a low-rank one: **inducing points** summarise the data through $m \ll n$ pseudo-inputs (Titsias, 2009, gave the variational version), and **random features** (Week 57) turn the GP back into Bayesian linear regression with $m$ features. For one-dimensional time series, Matérn kernels can be written as linear state-space models and solved in linear time by the Kalman filter of Week 65.
