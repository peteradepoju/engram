<Step n={1} title="Fit against complexity" minutes={25} />

For a GP with kernel hyperparameters $\boldsymbol\theta$, the marginal likelihood of the data is Gaussian with covariance $K_\theta + \sigma^2I = C$:

$$
\log p(\mathbf y \mid \boldsymbol\theta) = \underbrace{-\tfrac12\mathbf y^\top C^{-1}\mathbf y}_{\text{data fit}}\ \underbrace{-\tfrac12\log|C|}_{\text{complexity penalty}}\ - \tfrac n2\log2\pi.
$$

The first term is large when the data are typical of the model. The second penalises models whose prior spreads probability over many possible datasets: a flexible GP with a short length-scale can produce almost anything, so it assigns little probability to any particular dataset, including this one. MacKay (1992), today's paper, made this the basis of his "evidence framework" for choosing regularisation and models in neural networks.

The two terms pull in opposite directions. As the length-scale shrinks, the data-fit term improves steadily, because the GP can pass through every point; the complexity term falls away just as steadily, because a wiggly prior puts only a tiny probability on any particular dataset. Their sum peaks at 0.76, close to the 0.7 that generated the data. Nothing was held out: the penalty came from integrating over functions, not from a validation set.

<Step n={2} title="Optimising hyperparameters" minutes={30} />

All three hyperparameters can be found together by maximising the log marginal likelihood. Optimise their logarithms, so that they stay positive, with a quasi-Newton method; the gradient has a closed form, $\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_j}\log p = \frac12\text{tr}\left((\boldsymbol\alpha\boldsymbol\alpha^\top - C^{-1})\frac{\partial C}{\partial\theta_j}\right)$ with $\boldsymbol\alpha = C^{-1}\mathbf y$, though a numerical gradient is fine for three parameters. This is called **type II maximum likelihood**, or empirical Bayes: the function is integrated out, and only the hyperparameters are fitted.

Optimising all three hyperparameters recovers a noise standard deviation of 0.302 against a true 0.3, and a length-scale of 0.61 against 0.7, which is within the uncertainty one should expect from 40 points of a single random function. Three starting points were used because the objective is not convex; with one unlucky start it is possible to land in a solution that calls all the structure noise. In practice always restart, and look at the fit, not only the final number.

<Step n={3} title="Choosing between kernels" minutes={30} />

The marginal likelihood also compares kernels with different structures, each with its own hyperparameters fitted. Because it integrates over functions rather than fitting one, a kernel that could explain many datasets is penalised for not committing, and a kernel whose structure matches the data wins even if a more flexible one fits equally well. This is the **automatic Occam's razor** (MacKay, 1992; Rasmussen and Ghahramani, 2001).

The periodic kernel's marginal likelihood is 13.5 units higher than the squared exponential's, strong evidence, and it learns a period of 1.296 s against the true 1.3. The practical difference shows beyond the data: asked to predict 4 to 6 seconds, the periodic model continues the rhythm with a root mean squared error of 0.15, while the squared exponential decays to its prior mean and errs by 0.75. The squared exponential could fit the observed window just as well by using a short length-scale, but it would then have to admit that it could equally have produced countless other wiggly datasets, and that admission is the complexity penalty.

<Step n={4} title="Cautions" minutes={15} />

The marginal likelihood is a powerful default, but it has blind spots. It can have several local optima, for example one where a short length-scale explains the data as signal and one where a long length-scale explains it as noise, so optimise from several starts. Fixing the hyperparameters at their optimum ignores uncertainty about them, which matters with little data; a fully Bayesian treatment would integrate over them with MCMC. And it trusts the model class: if no kernel in the comparison is right, the marginal likelihood still picks one, sometimes with misplaced confidence, which is why held-out checks like Day 3's coverage remain useful.
