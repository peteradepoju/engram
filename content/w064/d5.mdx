<Step n={1} title="Gaussian-process factor analysis" minutes={35} />

**GPFA** (Yu and colleagues, 2009, today's paper) combines factor analysis (Week 61) with Gaussian-process priors over time. Each latent $z_j(t)$ has its own squared exponential kernel with timescale $\tau_j$; the population at time $t$ is $\mathbf x_t = C\mathbf z_t + \mathbf d + \boldsymbol\epsilon_t$ with diagonal noise $\Psi$. Because everything is Gaussian, inference over the whole trajectory is a single large but structured Gaussian conditioning: stack the latents over time, and the posterior is

$$
\Sigma_{\text{post}} = \left(K^{-1} + \tilde C^\top\tilde\Psi^{-1}\tilde C\right)^{-1},\qquad \boldsymbol\mu_{\text{post}} = \Sigma_{\text{post}}\tilde C^\top\tilde\Psi^{-1}(\tilde{\mathbf x} - \tilde{\mathbf d}),
$$

with $K$ block-diagonal over latents (one $T \times T$ kernel matrix each) and $\tilde C$ block-diagonal over time. Smoothing and dimensionality reduction happen together, rather than smoothing first and reducing afterwards.

GPFA recovers both latents with absolute correlations of 0.99, against 0.96 to 0.98 for smoothing each neuron with a fixed window and then projecting. The gap comes from doing both jobs at once: smoothing first applies the same window to every neuron, including the noisy ones, and the projection then inherits that noise, while GPFA weights each neuron by its reliability (through $\Psi$) and each latent by its own timescale. The posterior also comes with a covariance, so single-trial trajectories can be plotted with error bands rather than as if they were measured.

<Step n={2} title="Learning timescales" minutes={20} />

In GPFA the timescales are learned, not chosen: each $\tau_j$ is a hyperparameter of the marginal likelihood, which for fixed loadings is Gaussian with covariance $\tilde C K\tilde C^\top + \tilde\Psi$. Different latents end up with different timescales, which is itself a result: a population can carry a slow signal and a fast one at once.

The marginal likelihood recovers timescales of 24.9 and 9.9 bins against the true 25 and 8. The slow latent's timescale is pinned down well, the fast one less so, because a fast latent is harder to distinguish from independent noise: with a bin width of, say, 20 ms, this is a distinction between a 500 ms signal and a 200 ms one. Reporting timescales from GPFA is common in population studies, so it is worth checking how certain they are, for example by profiling the marginal likelihood around its maximum.

<Step n={3} title="Choosing the next stimulus" minutes={35} />

Recording time is the scarce resource in an experiment. **Bayesian optimisation** fits a GP to the responses measured so far and chooses the next stimulus by an **acquisition function** that scores how useful each candidate would be. **Expected improvement** over the best response so far, for a GP with mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma$ at a candidate and best-so-far $y^+$, is

$$
\text{EI} = \sigma\left[z\Phi(z) + \phi(z)\right],\qquad z = \frac{\mu - y^+}{\sigma},
$$

which is large where the response is predicted to be high or is uncertain. The same machinery underlies adaptive stimulus selection in neurophysiology, where the aim may instead be to reduce uncertainty about the whole tuning curve (Lewi, Butera and Paninski, 2009).

Over 20 simulated neurons with randomly placed preferred stimuli, 25 trials of Bayesian optimisation find responses averaging 1.19, against 1.09 for 25 random stimuli (values above 1 include the broad background component). The difference is largest for neurons whose peak falls where random sampling is sparse. Each new stimulus goes where the GP thinks the response may exceed the best so far, either because it is predicted high or because it is uncertain, and that balance is what the expected-improvement formula encodes: $\sigma\phi(z)$ rewards uncertainty and $\sigma z\Phi(z)$ rewards predicted gain.

<Step n={4} title="When adaptive design fails" minutes={15} />

Adaptive stimulus selection assumes that the response surface is well described by the GP's kernel and that the neuron is stable. Both can fail. If the true tuning is sharper than the kernel's length-scale, the GP smooths the peak away and the search may never visit it; if the neuron adapts, drifts or the recording degrades, early and late measurements are not comparable and the procedure chases a moving target. Adaptive designs also produce data that are not independent of the model, which complicates later analysis: a tuning curve estimated from adaptively chosen stimuli is dense near the peak and sparse elsewhere, so comparisons across neurons must account for the design. Standard safeguards are to interleave a few randomly chosen stimuli, to re-measure a reference stimulus periodically to check for drift, and to analyse with the design taken into account.
