<Step n={2} title="Weekly challenge" minutes={60} />

**Part A: a receptive field with learned smoothness.** A neuron's response to white-noise stimuli is a linear filter plus noise. Estimating the filter by ridge regression needs a penalty; a GP prior over the filter, smooth in space, does better and lets the smoothness be learned by maximising the evidence, the idea behind automatic smoothness determination (Sahani and Linden, 2003). With a Gaussian prior $\mathbf w \sim \mathcal N(\mathbf 0, K)$ and Gaussian noise, the posterior over the filter is Gaussian and the evidence is available in closed form.

The evidence chooses a length-scale of 3.45 pixels and a noise standard deviation of 0.48 against a true 0.5, and the resulting filter is less than half as far from the truth as the best ridge estimate (0.11 against 0.24), although ridge had a validation set and the GP did not. Ridge penalises every pixel equally and independently, so its estimate is noisy pixel by pixel; the GP prior says that neighbouring pixels are similar, which is what a receptive field looks like. Sahani and Linden called this automatic smoothness determination, and extensions learn a different smoothness along each axis, which also prunes irrelevant dimensions.

**Part B: a Poisson tuning curve.** Spike counts are not Gaussian, so the posterior over a GP tuning curve is not Gaussian either. The **Laplace approximation** (Rasmussen and Williams, chapter 3) fits a Gaussian at the posterior mode: find $\hat{\mathbf f}$ by Newton's method on $\log p(\mathbf y \mid \mathbf f) - \frac12\mathbf f^\top K^{-1}\mathbf f$, and use the Hessian there as the posterior precision. Predictions at new stimuli follow the usual GP formulas with $\hat{\mathbf f}$ in place of the data.

Newton's method converges in a few steps and the fitted rate correlates at 0.92 with the true tuning curve, from a single presentation of each of 24 orientations. The posterior standard deviation of the log rate is 0.25 where stimuli were dense and 0.65 where they were sparse. The Hessian explains both: its data term is $\text{diag}(\lambda)$, so each stimulus contributes information in proportion to its expected count. Densely sampled regions accumulate more such terms, and high-rate regions contribute more per stimulus, which is why the flanks of a tuning curve, where the rate is near zero, stay uncertain however often they are presented.

<Prove title="1. The GP predictive equations" task="With $\mathbf y = \mathbf f + \boldsymbol\epsilon$, $\mathbf f \sim \mathcal N(\mathbf 0, K)$ and $\boldsymbol\epsilon \sim \mathcal N(\mathbf 0, \sigma^2I)$, and $f_*$ the function value at a new input, derive $E[f_* \mid \mathbf y]$ and $\text{Var}[f_* \mid \mathbf y]$.">

$\mathbf y$ and $f_*$ are jointly Gaussian, because both are linear functions of the Gaussian $(\mathbf f, f_*, \boldsymbol\epsilon)$. Their covariances are $\text{Cov}(\mathbf y) = K + \sigma^2I$, $\text{Cov}(f_*, \mathbf y) = \mathbf k_*^\top$ (the noise is independent of $f_*$) and $\text{Var}(f_*) = k_{**}$.

The Gaussian conditioning formula (Week 16) gives $E[f_* \mid \mathbf y] = \text{Cov}(f_*, \mathbf y)\text{Cov}(\mathbf y)^{-1}\mathbf y = \mathbf k_*^\top(K + \sigma^2I)^{-1}\mathbf y$.

And $\text{Var}[f_* \mid \mathbf y] = k_{**} - \mathbf k_*^\top(K + \sigma^2I)^{-1}\mathbf k_*$, which does not depend on $\mathbf y$: in a Gaussian model the uncertainty depends on where you measured, not on what you measured.

</Prove>

<Prove title="2. Sums and products of kernels are kernels" task="Let $k_1$ and $k_2$ be valid (positive semi-definite) kernels. Show that $k_1 + k_2$ and $k_1k_2$ are valid kernels.">

**Sum.** For any inputs and any vector $\mathbf c$, $\mathbf c^\top(K_1 + K_2)\mathbf c = \mathbf c^\top K_1\mathbf c + \mathbf c^\top K_2\mathbf c \ge 0$, so the sum is positive semi-definite. (Interpretation: $f_1 + f_2$ with independent $f_i \sim \mathcal{GP}(0, k_i)$ has covariance $k_1 + k_2$.)

**Product.** The elementwise (Hadamard) product of two positive semi-definite matrices is positive semi-definite, by the Schur product theorem. A direct argument: write $K_1 = E[\mathbf u\mathbf u^\top]$ and $K_2 = E[\mathbf v\mathbf v^\top]$ for independent Gaussian vectors $\mathbf u, \mathbf v$; then the vector with entries $u_iv_i$ has covariance $K_1 \odot K_2$, which is therefore positive semi-definite.

</Prove>
