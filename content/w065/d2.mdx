<Step n={1} title="EM for sequences" minutes={25} />

<Derivation title="The Baum-Welch updates">
<div>

The complete-data log-likelihood of an HMM, if the states were known, is

$$
\log\pi_{z_1} + \sum_{t=2}^T\log A_{z_{t-1}z_t} + \sum_{t=1}^T\log p(x_t \mid z_t).
$$

Each term involves at most two consecutive states, so its expectation under any posterior needs only two sets of marginals: $\gamma_t(k) = p(z_t = k \mid x_{1:T})$ and $\xi_t(j, k) = p(z_{t-1} = j, z_t = k \mid x_{1:T})$.

</div>
<div>

**E step.** Run forward-backward to get $\gamma$, and compute the pairwise marginals

$$
\xi_t(j, k) \propto \alpha_{t-1}(j)A_{jk}p(x_t \mid z_t = k)\beta_t(k).
$$

</div>
<div>

**M step.** Maximise the expected complete-data log-likelihood. The transition and initial probabilities are expected counts, normalised, and the emission parameters are weighted maximum-likelihood estimates:

$$
A_{jk} = \frac{\sum_t\xi_t(j, k)}{\sum_k\sum_t\xi_t(j, k)},\qquad \pi_k = \gamma_1(k),\qquad \lambda_k = \frac{\sum_t\gamma_t(k)x_t}{\sum_t\gamma_t(k)}
$$

for Poisson emissions. Baum and colleagues (1970), today's paper, proved the monotonicity of this scheme before EM was stated in general (Week 60).

</div>
</Derivation>

  def forward_backward(B, A, pi):
      T_, K = B.shape
      al = np.zeros((T_, K)); c = np.zeros(T_); be = np.ones((T_, K))
      a = pi * B[0]; c[0] = a.sum(); al[0] = a / c[0]
      for t in range(1, T_):
          a = B[t] * (al[t - 1] @ A); c[t] = a.sum(); al[t] = a / c[t]
      for t in range(T_ - 2, -1, -1):
          be[t] = (A @ (B[t + 1] * be[t + 1])) / c[t + 1]
      g = al * be; g /= g.sum(1, keepdims=True)
      return g, al, be, c

From a crude start (rates at half and one and a half times the overall mean, and a symmetric transition matrix), ten iterations recover rates of 1.51 and 5.90 against the true 1.5 and 6.0, and a transition matrix within a hundredth of the truth. Nothing was told to the algorithm about up and down states: it found two rates and the persistence that makes them worth distinguishing. The states themselves are the by-product $\gamma$, which Day 1 turned into a segmentation.

Like every EM algorithm, Baum-Welch finds a local optimum. It also has a symmetry: permuting the state labels, and the rows and columns of $A$ with them, gives exactly the same model, so two fits can describe the same thing with the labels swapped (**label switching**). Order the states by something meaningful, here the firing rate, before comparing or averaging fits. A third problem is degeneracy: a state can collapse onto a few time points with a rate that fits them exactly, the sequential version of Week 60's collapsing mixture component.

Twelve random starts of a three-state model give six distinct local optima, from $-2541.7$ to $-2545.4$. Keeping the best is standard practice, and the spread is a reminder that a single run means little. The best run's rates are 1.33, 1.81 and 5.89: it kept the true high-rate state and split the low-rate one in two, which is what a model with a spare state usually does. The split states are hard to tell apart, their posterior probabilities overlapping heavily, and reporting them as distinct network states would be an overinterpretation.

<Step n={4} title="How many states?" minutes={25} />

More states always fit the training data better, so the number must be chosen by held-out likelihood, by an information criterion, or by a nonparametric prior that lets the data choose (the HDP-HMM of Teh and colleagues, 2006). For sequences, held-out data means held-out stretches: fit on one part of the recording and evaluate the log-likelihood of another, using the forward recursion. Then check the fit by simulating from the model and comparing summary statistics with the data, as in Week 50's falsification.

<Key title="Before you go">
<ul>
<li>Baum-Welch is EM: forward-backward in the E step, weighted counts in the M step.</li>
<li>It converges monotonically to a local optimum, so use many starts.</li>
<li>State labels are arbitrary; order them before interpreting.</li>
<li>Choose the number of states by held-out likelihood and check the fit by simulation.</li>
</ul>
