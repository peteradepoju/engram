<Step n={1} title="The model and the recursion" minutes={30} />

<Derivation title="Predict and update">
<div>

The **linear Gaussian state-space model** is

$$
\mathbf z_t = A\mathbf z_{t-1} + \mathbf w_t,\ \mathbf w_t \sim \mathcal N(\mathbf 0, Q),\qquad \mathbf y_t = C\mathbf z_t + \mathbf v_t,\ \mathbf v_t \sim \mathcal N(\mathbf 0, R).
$$

Everything is linear and Gaussian, so every distribution that appears is Gaussian and is described by a mean and a covariance.

</div>
<div>

**Predict.** Given $p(\mathbf z_{t-1} \mid \mathbf y_{1:t-1}) = \mathcal N(\mathbf m_{t-1}, P_{t-1})$, pushing through the dynamics gives

$$
\mathbf m_t^- = A\mathbf m_{t-1},\qquad P_t^- = AP_{t-1}A^\top + Q.
$$

Uncertainty always grows in this step, by the process noise.

</div>
<div>

**Update.** The measurement is $\mathbf y_t = C\mathbf z_t + \mathbf v_t$, so jointly $(\mathbf z_t, \mathbf y_t)$ is Gaussian with cross-covariance $P_t^-C^\top$ and $\text{Cov}(\mathbf y_t) = CP_t^-C^\top + R$. Conditioning (Week 16) gives

$$
K_t = P_t^-C^\top\left(CP_t^-C^\top + R\right)^{-1},\quad \mathbf m_t = \mathbf m_t^- + K_t(\mathbf y_t - C\mathbf m_t^-),\quad P_t = (I - K_tC)P_t^-.
$$

</div>
<div>

The **Kalman gain** $K_t$ decides how far to move towards the measurement: it is large when the prediction is uncertain or the sensor is precise. The innovation $\mathbf y_t - C\mathbf m_t^-$ is the surprise, and its covariance gives the likelihood term $\log\mathcal N(\mathbf y_t; C\mathbf m_t^-, CP_t^-C^\top + R)$, which sums to the log-likelihood as the HMM's scale factors did.

</div>
</Derivation>

The recursion's estimate at the last step agrees to four decimals with conditioning the whole $30 \times 2$-dimensional joint Gaussian at once, and the summed innovation log-densities equal the exact log-density of the entire observation sequence. The recursion is not an approximation: it is the same answer, computed in time linear in the length of the recording instead of cubic. Each step uses only the previous mean and covariance, which is what makes it usable online.

<Step n={3} title="What the gain does" minutes={20} />

The gain depends only on the model, not on the data, and for a time-invariant model it converges to a steady-state value. Misstating the noises does not bias the estimate but changes how it trades smoothness for responsiveness, and it makes the reported uncertainty wrong.

All three filters track the state, but they are very different estimates. Believing the measurements are ten times better than they are gives a rough estimate, mean squared step 0.61, which chases the noise and whose 95% intervals contain the true state only 51% of the time. The correct noise gives roughness 0.10 and coverage 0.958, as it should. Overstating the noise tenfold gives a very smooth estimate, roughness 0.012, and intervals that are too wide, 99.5%. The lesson for a decoder is that the noise parameters are not cosmetic: they set the bandwidth of the estimate and the honesty of its error bars.

<Step n={4} title="Decoding a cursor" minutes={30} />

In a brain-computer interface, the state is the cursor's position and velocity, the dynamics are a smooth trajectory model, and the observations are the firing rates of a recorded population (Wu and colleagues, 2006). The Kalman filter runs online, one update per bin, which is why it has been the standard decoder for intracortical interfaces.

With 12 noisy neurons, the Kalman decoder reaches a correlation of 0.87 with the true velocity, against 0.79 for bin-by-bin regression, even though the regression weights were fitted on the very data they are tested on. The filter's advantage is its prior: velocity changes slowly, so an implausible jump in the instantaneous population estimate is damped by the prediction from the previous bin. That is exactly the structure a regression on one bin at a time throws away, and it is why Kalman-style decoders have been the standard in intracortical brain-computer interfaces.
