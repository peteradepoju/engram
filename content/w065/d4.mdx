<Step n={1} title="The backward pass" minutes={25} />

The **Rauch-Tung-Striebel smoother** runs backwards over the filtered estimates. With filtered $(\mathbf m_t, P_t)$ and predicted $(\mathbf m_{t+1}^-, P_{t+1}^-)$,

$$
J_t = P_tA^\top(P_{t+1}^-)^{-1},\qquad \hat{\mathbf m}_t = \mathbf m_t + J_t(\hat{\mathbf m}_{t+1} - \mathbf m_{t+1}^-),\qquad \hat P_t = P_t + J_t(\hat P_{t+1} - P_{t+1}^-)J_t^\top.
$$

The gain $J_t$ says how much of the future's correction to pass back. EM will also need the lagged covariance $\hat P_{t,t-1} = \hat P_tJ_{t-1}^\top$.

<Step n={2} title="EM for an LDS" minutes={35} />

<Derivation title="The M step from smoothed moments">
<div>

The complete-data log-likelihood of an LDS involves $\mathbf z_t\mathbf z_t^\top$, $\mathbf z_t\mathbf z_{t-1}^\top$ and $\mathbf y_t\mathbf z_t^\top$. The E step, the smoother, provides their expectations:

$$
E[\mathbf z_t\mathbf z_t^\top] = \hat P_t + \hat{\mathbf m}_t\hat{\mathbf m}_t^\top,\qquad E[\mathbf z_t\mathbf z_{t-1}^\top] = \hat P_{t,t-1} + \hat{\mathbf m}_t\hat{\mathbf m}_{t-1}^\top.
$$

</div>
<div>

**M step.** Each parameter is a weighted least-squares solution. Writing $S_{11} = \sum_{t\ge2}E[\mathbf z_t\mathbf z_t^\top]$, $S_{10} = \sum_{t\ge2}E[\mathbf z_t\mathbf z_{t-1}^\top]$, $S_{00} = \sum_{t\ge2}E[\mathbf z_{t-1}\mathbf z_{t-1}^\top]$:

$$
A = S_{10}S_{00}^{-1},\qquad Q = \frac{1}{T-1}\left(S_{11} - AS_{10}^\top\right),
$$

$$
C = \left(\sum_t\mathbf y_t\hat{\mathbf m}_t^\top\right)\left(\sum_tE[\mathbf z_t\mathbf z_t^\top]\right)^{-1},\qquad R = \frac1T\sum_t\left(\mathbf y_t\mathbf y_t^\top - C\hat{\mathbf m}_t\mathbf y_t^\top\right).
$$

</div>
<div>

Ghahramani and Hinton (1996), today's paper, set out exactly this algorithm, noting that it unifies factor analysis (no dynamics), mixtures and HMMs (discrete states) and Kalman smoothing (continuous states) under one roof.

</div>
</Derivation>

<Step n={4} title="What is identifiable?" minutes={20} />

For any invertible $M$, replacing $\mathbf z_t$ by $M\mathbf z_t$, $A$ by $MAM^{-1}$, $C$ by $CM^{-1}$ and $Q$ by $MQM^\top$ gives exactly the same distribution over observations. The latent coordinates are therefore arbitrary, and so are the individual entries of $A$ and $C$. What survives the transformation is what can be reported: the **eigenvalues** of $A$ (rotation frequencies and decay rates), the subspace spanned by $C$, the predictions, and quantities computed from them.

The eigenvalues, $0.9844 \pm 0.0877i$, are identical, because a similarity transformation preserves them. So a statement such as "latent 1 drives latent 2" is a statement about the chosen coordinates, not about the brain, while "the population has a rotation of 5 degrees per bin that decays with a time constant of 60 bins" is about the data.

<Key title="Before you go">
<ul>
<li>The RTS smoother adds the future to the filtered estimates and never increases uncertainty.</li>
<li>EM for an LDS turns smoothed moments into least-squares updates for A, C, Q and R.</li>
<li>Co-smoothing tests a fitted latent model on held-out neurons.</li>
<li>Latent coordinates are arbitrary; report eigenvalues, subspaces and predictions.</li>
